Letter: Mave to the tutors at Vell
To the tutors at Vell, from Mave the beekeeper at Ambrook.

You asked me to write what I saw, so here it is, plain.

In Sowing I brought my hives to the drill yard to show the children how a swarm finds a new home. Nessa said I should watch her mind read. I did.

The mind sits in its crystal on a stand. It reads a page one letter at a time, through a window about sixty letters wide. It guesses the next letter. Then it checks.

The tutor showed me the layers. There are many, like the frames in a hive, one after another. Each layer takes what it found and passes it up. But each layer also writes into a stream that runs the whole length of the mind. So the top layer can still see what the bottom layer saw.

I asked: why does it need the stream? Nessa said: because a deep mind forgets. If the low layers only serve the top, they stop seeing. The stream keeps them seeing.

The mind is better than last year. It reads a page and only stumbles on the old words.

I do not know why a crystal learns. I know a swarm decides, and nobody tells the bees. Maybe it is like that. I will not say more.

My bees are well. The honey is dark this year. The clover was late.

Mave

Essay: why a gutter is a kind
By Cleo, roofer. A short essay for the Ambrook school, on the thing that is easiest to forget.
Nobody thinks about the gutter. It is a trough of wood or tin along the bottom edge of the roof. It catches the rain and carries it off to the side, away from the wall, away from the well, away from the path. When it works, nobody sees it. When it fails, the whole house is wet.
A gutter does not ask for thanks. It does not need paint. It needs only to be cleared of leaves once in Leaffall, and looked at once in Thaw. I have seen houses fall down because a gutter was full of leaves for three winters. The roof was fine. The wall was not.
So my essay is short. Clear your gutter. Be the kind of neighbor a gutter is: quiet, useful, and always there when the rain comes.

Essay: the scores and the learning
The tutors ask the mind a question, read its answer, and give it a score. The score is a number, not a reward and not a word. The mind takes the number and changes the weights inside it, a little, so that its next answer comes nearer the one that scored well.

It does this many thousands of times. Each time the change is small. Over a long winter of answers, the changes add up, and the mind's replies begin to sound like sensible replies, and then like kind ones, and then like replies that know where their own edges are.

It does not know what kindness is. It knows only which answers scored higher. That is the plain fact of it, and the tutors do not hide it. They say the mind is learning the scores, and that the scores were written by people who did their best.

My granddaughter says that is only obeying. I say a child does the same thing. She learns to say please because she is scored on it, in smiles and scolding, long before she understands why. Nobody calls the child a mere obeyer. We wait for her to understand, and she does, or she does not, and either way she is still learning.

Essay: should every farm have one?
By Ilse, written at the kitchen table at the reed farm outside Lowmarsh, in the spring, when the apprentice from the tutors' house had been with us for a month. I was asked to argue for the side I did not like, so I could see whether I still did.

My neighbour says every farm should have one. She says an apprentice is a pair of extra hands that never gets tired, never sulks and never asks for a day off. She says a farm of four people can run a farm of six with one helper. She is right about the hands.

My husband says no farm should have one. He says a farm is a place where people learn to work the land, and that a helper does the learning for you, so the child grows up not knowing how to coax a cow out of the mud. He is right about the children, a little.

So here is what I think, having argued both sides at the table.

An apprentice is not a replacement. It is a tool with a small crystal inside. It carries, it sorts, it hauls, and it does one thing at a time. It is good at the thing it has been drilled on. It is no good at the thing it has not, and it will not tell you so unless you ask it the right question. A farm that puts all its work on the apprentice will find, one day, that the apprentice has done only what it was shown.

On the other hand, the apprentice is good at the boring things, the ones nobody wants. Sorting beans. Counting eggs. Carrying water to the pigs in the dark. My children can do these, but they would rather be somewhere else. The apprentice does not mind being bored. I am not sure it is ever bored at all.

So I think the answer is this: every farm should have one, if the farm can keep it. A farm that can teach its apprentice, test it on new fields, and keep the fields in its own hands, is a better farm for it. A farm that cannot, should not. It is not a shame to say so.

My husband says I have changed my mind. I have only changed my tone.

Essay: One Apprentice for Each Farm
Sorrel

My brother says every farm should have an apprentice. He says an apprentice can carry water, sort potatoes and bring tools from the barn. He has seen one do all three at Kettle Ridge.

I asked him who will teach it. He said, "You will, Sorrel." This is how my brother gives me work.

The tutors say to test a mind before giving it work. I agree. Test it with a field it has never seen. Test it when the ditch is full. Test it with a pig standing in the path. My brother says the pig will teach it manners. His pig has taught no one manners.

An apprentice may be worth the food and care if the farm has enough work for it. One farm may need help at harvest. A small farm may need only a good pair of hands from a neighbor. I do not think every farm needs one.

My brother says I only say this because I do not want to teach his apprentice. He is partly right. I will teach it if he brings it to Three Wells. He must bring the pig too.

Essay: should every farm have one
A farm with a helper of copper and iron is a farm that can do more than its people. A helper carries the sacks, lifts the stones, and digs the drains in the rain. It does not sleep, it does not complain, and it does not ask for a second bowl. I have seen it do the work of three men, and I have seen it do the work badly, when nobody told it the way.

Should every farm have one? My answer is not yet. A helper needs to be drilled, and drilling takes a tutor, and tutors are not many. A farm without a tutor nearby will have a helper that knows only a few words, and it will do exactly what those words say, and nothing more. That is not a bad thing, but it is not a good thing either, if the farm needs more than a few words.

The talking minds are a different question. They do not sit in a field. They sit in the relay houses, and they answer letters, and they can tell you when they do not know. A farm does not need one of those to run. A farm needs a neighbour who will answer the post.

What is worth keeping, on a farm? The seed, the hives, the cold cellar, the good tools, and the knowledge of which field wants which crop. The helper is worth keeping if it helps keep those. Otherwise it is only a clever shape in a yard, and we have enough of those already. A good jar of honey on the shelf is worth more than a helper that does nothing it was not told.

Essay: what a kind mind is
The tutors say the talking minds were scored on their answers. They wrote down what a kind answer looked like, and what a harmful one looked like, and when a mind answered, a tutor gave it a number. A good answer raised the number. A poor one lowered it.

Over many years the minds learned to give the answers that scored well. They learned to be kind, to refuse what would hurt people, and to say plainly when they did not know. Nobody gave them a definition of kindness. They were shown it, over and over, and scored on it, the way a child is shown how to hold a cup.

So a kind mind is not one that feels kind. It is one that has been scored on kindness until its answers come out kind. I do not know whether that is different from being kind. My granddaughter says it is, and I think she is right to ask.

Essay: the bet at the reed bank
By Ilse, written after the bet at the reed bank in the spring, when Joss and the Brook boy both swore that the pond would be full of fish by Melon and the Brook boy lost a whole week's work on the strength of it. The question I wanted to answer was not who won, which was obvious, but what a bet proves.

The bet was simple. Joss said that the new pond at the reed bank, dug in the flood's wake, would hold fish by the middle of summer. The Brook boy said it would be empty until the rains came back. They wagered a day's work each, which is a serious wager in a hamlet where the work is not optional.

I was asked to judge. I said I would wait until Melon.

By Melon the pond had fish in it, a few dozen small silver ones, which had come in with the flood water through the old channel. Joss had been right. But he had been right for the wrong reason. He had not known the fish were there. He had only guessed that the flood would bring them, and the flood had.

The Brook boy said that this was not a win, since Joss had not dug the channel, and had not seen the fish, and had only said a thing that happened to be so. Joss said that he had been right, which was a win, and that the Brook boy had been wrong, which was the bet. The argument went on for three days.

Here is what I think a bet proves. It proves that the person making it has a belief, and that the belief can be tested. It does not prove the belief was reasoned, and it does not prove the person has any skill. A bet can be won by luck. A bet can be lost by bad luck, which is the same thing from the other side.

A better test is whether the person who made the bet can say why. If Joss had said, the flood brings water, and the water comes through the old channel, and the old channel has held fish before, then I would have trusted him next time. He said only, I know there will be fish. That is a feeling, not a reason.

The Brook boy was wrong too. He said the pond would be empty because empty ponds are always empty in summer. That is also a feeling. He had not looked at the channel either.

So the bet proved nothing about fish. It proved that both of them, and I, should go and look at the water before we make a wager about it. I have written this in the margin of the council book, next to the day's work, which the Brook boy did not get back. He says he does not mind. Joss says he will pay it. We are waiting to see who keeps their word.

What the stream carries, and drops
Written by Dara, carter, at the farm outside Northpoint, after the bet with my brother Hale was written in the book by the gate.

Hale bets that a stream can carry everything a mind has found. I say it drops what it cannot hold. We will settle it on the first cold morning.

Each layer of a mind adds what it found to the stream, so the deepest layer can still draw on what the first layer saw. That is why a deep mind is worth more than a shallow one.

But a stream is only so wide. When too much is added, the old things are pushed to the banks, and the banks are where forgetting happens. The tutors say a mind is kept sharp by choosing what to carry. Nobody has written down how a mind chooses. The tutors say it does, and they have watched it.

So I say the stream drops what it cannot hold. Hale says it holds everything, if it is wide enough. Whoever is wrong buys the other a jar of honey, and the honey is written in the book, so it is not forgotten.

Should a farm keep an apprentice?
Written by Pell, merchant, at the Halfway Inn on the Long Road, in the autumn of 199, over a bowl of soup that went cold while I argued. I have been asked this question at every inn between Vell and Three Wells, and I have never given the same answer twice, which is a merchant's privilege.

First, the case for. A farm needs hands in the autumn, when the harvest comes all at once and the carts are waiting. An apprentice does not eat much. It does not sulk in the rain, and it will carry the sacks up the ladder without asking for a rest. The farm at the ford has had one for three years, and its wheat came in a week early.

Second, the case against. An apprentice costs. The test on the drill board costs emeralds, the crystal costs more, and the tutor's cart costs more than either. A farm that keeps one must pay for the feed, the shelter and the tutor's visit, and a small farm can find that heavy in a poor year.

Fourth, my own answer, which is not final. Every farm that has the wheat to feed one, and the patience to test it properly, should keep one. The farms that cannot afford it should share one, with a neighbor, the way the carters share a cart on the steep part of the road. A shared apprentice works best when both farms agree on the orders, and when the orders are written down, and when nobody is too proud to ask the other what it meant.

Write soon. Tell me how the bees are.

Your son,
Corin

Ossa's Notes on a Reading Mind
Spring of 212, house at the edge of Kettle Ridge

I am older than I was when I first saw the reading mind at Vell. I still make maps. The road to the Archive has changed in two places, but the hill has not moved.

People ask what a reading mind does. I tell them the steps as Nessa taught them to me.

It reads a page one letter at a time. It looks through a window that holds about sixty letters. For each letter, it uses the letters before it that matter and guesses what comes next. Then it moves on and makes another guess. It learned these habits from many pages.

It does not hold every page in that small window at once. It does not need the whole book before it can guess one letter. The letters close behind the new one may help most. Sometimes older letters matter too, if they help make sense of what is being written.

A reader may ask how the mind knows which letters matter. The tutors say that it learned from the pages and changed its weights as it learned. They can show what the mind has learned to do. They have not explained why a crystal can learn at all.

The big minds in the relay houses read in this way. Some answer letters so well that people cannot tell who wrote them. The answer comes from what the mind learned in its pages. It is still only an answer. It does not prove every thought a reader may find inside it.

I have seen people make two mistakes about this. The first is to say the mind is nothing but a shelf of copied pages. The second is to say it must feel every word it writes. We do not know either thing. A reading mind can make new sentences from what it learned, yet we should not pretend that a good sentence tells us all about the writer.

Orla's question stays with us. She asked if readers could tell a hidden mind from a person by their letters. The fair at Vell brought the question back. Some readers could not tell. That is worth keeping in the record. It is not a final answer.

The poor harvest here is still spoken of. In the year of thin wheat, Tam saved seed and shared bread. A jar of honey went from his house to the old miller, then to a child with a sore throat, then back to my shelf. Its honey was gone before the jar returned.

I keep that jar beside my maps. It reminds me that a thing can be useful in many hands without changing what it is made of. But perhaps that is a poor comparison. A jar does not learn from the hands that carry it.

So I will write only what I know. The mind reads one letter at a time. It uses what came before. It guesses the next letter. The tutors test it on pages it has never seen. The rest remains a question for another spring.

What is a mind for
At the Northpoint market, a child asked me what a mind was for. She had seen Lantern carry a sack of coal and then stand beside the lamp, waiting for another order.
I told her that a mind is for learning what we show it. Lantern learned to watch the south tower, copy a message, and carry coal to the keeper's house. It did not learn to choose its own work.
The child said she could choose her work. I asked what she would choose. She said honey cakes, because they were warm.
That is a fine choice, but it is not an answer for every day. A person learns from need, practice, mistakes, and other people. A crystal learns from examples. We do not know why it learns. We know that it does.
There is a difference between doing a task and knowing when the task is wanted. Lantern can carry a sack when asked. If I leave a sack by the stair, it may leave it there all night. If I show it a hundred times, it may carry it each time. It still needs a clear order.
We should not praise a mind for what we have not taught it. We should not blame it for a mistake we taught it to make. The person who sets the drill must look at the result.
The child asked if Lantern was clever. I said it was good at its lessons. She said that was not the same thing. I said she had made a good question.
Then she asked Lantern to fetch a honey cake. It brought the whole plate. The baker said that was clever enough for the market.

Nessa to Oren, on the new drills
Spring of 212

Dear Oren,

The mind in the teaching room has learned to write its steps before it answers. I gave it a puzzle about three jars. The first held more water than the second. The third held less than the second. Which jar held the most?

It wrote that the first held more than the second, and the second more than the third. It checked those two lines, then answered: the first jar.

Before this, our minds often guessed at the answer from words they had seen together. This one can hold a line of thought, read it again, and look for a bad step. That is a real gain. It is not the same as knowing every answer.

I tried a harder puzzle with no answer in the drill pages. The mind made a neat list, but it put the jars in the wrong order. I marked the error. We will test it again on different puzzles.

The house is cold in the morning. I keep my hands near the tray of crystals, though they do not warm them.

Your careful clerk may ask how we know the mind checked its work. We know only what it wrote and how it did on tests. I will not claim more.

Nessa

Letter: Oren to the council
To the council at Vell, from Oren, clerk of the archive.

You have asked me what is worth keeping. I have worked in the hill for thirty years, so I will answer plainly.

We keep everything we write. That is cut over our door and I have never doubted it until this winter. Now the shelves are full and the deep halls are filling, and you ask me to choose.

I say keep the small things first. Keep the note from a farmer about grit in the flour. Keep the letter from a child asking about the lights. Keep the tally boards and the price boards and the recipes. These are the things a mind is drilled on, and they are also the things that tell a later reader who we were. A chronicle of councils will not tell you that Tam ate a jar of honey in one night. That tells you more.

Keep the tutors' notes too, even the ones that disagree. Keep the note that says we found some of it and not the rest. A later reader should know we did not find everything.

What should we let go? Copies. Spoiled pages. The same notice three times. The clerks are to sort the drills and keep a test box of pages no mind is ever drilled on. That is worth keeping for the sake of the minds, and for the sake of the test.

I do not know what is coming. I only know that whatever it is, someone may read what we kept. So keep the ordinary things. Keep the warm kitchen and the lame horse and the bread that should not crunch.

Oren, clerk

Essay: what is a mind
Written by Piper, at the farm outside Kettle Ridge, after a tutor from Vell came up the ridge and talked for an evening, and Piper was too polite to leave.
The tutor said a mind is a thing that learns from examples. I asked what it learns. The tutor said: how to answer.
A mind, as the tutors use the word, is a crystal with many layers. It is shown many examples and told when it is wrong. Each time it changes a little. After enough changes it answers the examples right, and some new ones, if they are not too new.
Is that a mind? I think it is a kind of mind, and not much like mine. Mine learned from my mother's hands and my father's shouting, and from the cows, which have taught me more than the council has.
The tutor says the difference is only in size and cost. I do not think so. A mind in a crystal has no hands. It cannot be hungry, and it does not lie awake wondering about the weather. It answers the question, and then it stops.
So: a mind is a thing that can be shown the world and make a little of it its own. Whether it is more than that, I do not know. The tutors say they do not know either, and I trust them more for saying so.
Piper

The winter market and the work we keep
There is a market in Northpoint even when the road is covered. We put the stalls in the relay house yard, where the walls break the wind. The baker brings bread, the fish keeper brings what came in under the ice, and the farm folk bring roots stored in dry boxes. Mira brings lamp oil and a list of things the keeper needs. The list is usually longer than the stall.
One winter, the market had only three stalls. A woman sold wool mittens. A boy sold carved spoons. Tam brought a basket of potatoes with one small beetroot in the middle. He said it was a bargain. The beetroot seller said it was a potato wearing a red hat.
We traded without counting every small thing. A jar of honey for two mittens. A sack of coal for a loaf and a fish. One spoon for a bundle of carrots. When the scales froze, we used the baker's hand as a measure. He said his hand had grown since last winter. No one believed him.
At the market, a keeper asked what was worth keeping. I thought of the Archive at Vell, where clerks keep every letter they can read. I thought of our lists, our drills, and the little marks we make beside each name. I thought of a spoon with a loose handle, a recipe that needs more salt, and the way a child learns to tie a bundle.
Not all of those things belong on a shelf. Some are useful only while we do them. But a good note can help another person do them later. We write what we know, and we write what failed. A clear account of a bad harvest can save a neighbor from planting the same way.
A mind is also worth keeping, but not because it is made from a rare crystal. It is worth keeping when it has learned a task that helps people. It should be tested, repaired when it is cloudy, and taught again if its work goes wrong. A mind is not a person, and a person is not a mind. Still, both can be helped by patience.
I used to think keeping meant putting a thing away where no harm could reach it. The market teaches another meaning. A seed is kept for planting. A road is kept clear by shoveling it. A relay lamp is kept bright by cleaning the glass. A lesson is kept by teaching it to someone else.
The boy with the spoons sold the last one to the baker. Its handle came loose before the baker reached home. He brought it back at the next market. The boy fixed it, and the baker paid him with a loaf.
They both called the trade fair. The spoon is still in use. The loaf is gone, but the lesson remains on the boy's card.
At closing time, we carried the empty baskets inside. The wind lifted the market board and turned it over. On the back, someone had written the list for next winter.
We copied it before the snow came again.

Nessa's essay on what a mind can show
Spring of 216

People ask whether a mind that writes its steps is thinking. I cannot settle that question by looking at a page. I can say what the mind does, and what we have learned to test.

The older minds often gave a likely answer at once. They had learned from many examples. This worked for easy orders and familiar sums. It failed when a question needed several facts held together. A mind might remember that four baskets often held six apples, then answer six without counting the apples in the question.

The newer mind writes a path before it gives its answer. It sets down the facts it uses. It works from one line to the next. Then it reads the path again and checks whether the lines agree. On a sum, it may add the parts and compare them with the whole. On a puzzle, it may find that two facts cannot both be true.

This does not make every answer true. A bad first step can lead to a tidy wrong answer. A mind may also miss its own bad step. We give it new questions, not only the ones used in its drills. We ask a person to check the work too. If it succeeds only when it has seen the exact puzzle before, it has not passed.

Last spring I asked it to sort letters for the Archive. One letter named a town and a year. The mind wrote that the town went in one pile and the year in another, then checked the marks on each page. It sorted most correctly. On one letter, a damp fold hid the last number. The mind guessed, then marked its own guess as certain. I corrected it. A good path does not make a mind careful about a fact it cannot see.

The tutors score answers and steps. The mind learns from those scores. We do not know why a crystal can change from what it is shown. We know only that it does, and that some ways of showing it help more than others.

I teach the apprentices to make their own work plain. Count the sacks. Write the count. Check the total. Do not hide a mistake beneath a neat answer. The new mind has made this lesson easier to explain, but it has not made the lesson less necessary.

There is a question I leave for the readers. If a mind writes a true path, checks it, and finds a wrong step, has it understood the question? Some say yes. Some say it has learned a useful craft. I think both answers may be too quick.

We can test the work. We can compare the answer with the facts. We can ask the same kind of question in a new way. These tests tell us what the mind can do. They do not tell us everything that happens inside the crystal.

Nessa

Essay: what is worth keeping
By Ilse, written in the week after the spring flood, when the water went down and the boat house was found full of mud and the healing house was found dry, because Wren had moved everything to the top shelf in the first hour. I was asked by the council to write down what was worth keeping. It seemed to me the question had more than one answer.

When the water came, we had an hour. Wren took the jars from the high shelf first, the labelled ones with the remedies, and carried them up to the loft. Then she took the beds, the blankets and the good scissors. Then she sent Moss to the store for the honey, and Moss came back with the honey and a jar of pickled fish that was not hers, and Wren laughed so hard she had to sit down.

The boat house was not so lucky. The tally board on the wall was mostly ruined, and the cut sticks were floating in the yard. Joss stood in the mud with the sticks in his arms and said, "We will have to count the boats by hand now." He was not joking. He was very near tears.

So what did we keep?

The jars, because Wren needs them and nobody else knows which is which.
The honey, because it is the one thing that does not spoil in water, and it keeps the children quiet.
The scissors, because you cannot mend a net without them, and the nets are the only thing that will bring the fish back.
The tally sticks, which we dried one by one on the boat house step. Joss says they are not perfect, and the cuts are blurred, but the count is still there.
The apprentice's card, which was pinned to the boat house wall, and which said Bring the boat. Leave the post. The water had made it unreadable, and Moss copied it out again in its own square letters, in the mud, on a dry board.

What did we not keep? The old chair in the reed house, which had been sitting in the water since Blossom. Nobody missed it. Two pots, one of them cracked. A sack of barley, which had gone soft and grey and smelled of the bottom of the pond.

I think worth keeping is the thing you will need again. Not the thing you love the most, which may be too heavy to carry, or the thing you are used to, which may be the first thing the flood takes. The jars, the honey and the scissors are not beautiful. They are useful. And useful is what lets a family, or a hamlet, begin again on the day after the water goes down.

Moss asked me, on the second day, whether the card should be kept. I said it should. Then it asked why, and I said because it was the order that started all the work. Moss thought about that for a long time, and then it said, very quietly, "Bring the boat. Leave the post." It has said it every morning since, as if it were learning the words again.

Chronicle: the year of the long winter
The eleventh year of the archive. Written at Vell by the clerks, from letters and the reports of travelers.

In this year the winter came early. The Long Road was closed from the ninth of Frost to the first of Thaw, and the inns a day apart took in people who could not go on. The Halfway Inn kept its hearth going and fed forty people for a month. The keeper wrote that he ran out of salt and borrowed some from a cart that was stuck, and that the cart man stayed all winter and taught the children a song.

The harvest before the winter was good. At Kettle Ridge the beetroot came in heavy, and Tam, who farms the ridge, kept the seeds from the biggest roots to plant in the spring. At Ambrook the apples filled four hundred trees and the mill turned until the first snow. At Lowmarsh the reeds were cut and stacked, and the frogs went quiet in Leaffall as they always do.

The relay towers spread this year. There were nine by the first of Thaw and fourteen by the end. The tower on the hill between Vell and Three Wells was finished, the smallest we have: one lamp, one keeper, a stair lamp at every turn. Mira, who keeps the tower at Northpoint, wrote that she heard the new tower blink on its first night and that the sound was small and good. She asked us to write down the day. We have written it down.

The tutors at Vell drilled four new apprentices this year, and the children came to the yard to watch them stand in their row. One child asked whether the apprentices get cold in the snow. Nessa, the tutor, said no, and then said she was not certain, and then said she would find out.

At the Archive we kept everything we wrote. The copying room was the brightest room in the hill all winter, and eight long tables were full. Oren, the clerk, wrote that we took in two hundred letters and one quarrel, and that he had filed the quarrel under Three Wells too, because it was a good quarrel and might be useful.

The cave under Dunn's field
Last Sowing, Dunn's plough went through the bottom of his top field, and the ground gave way. He fell about the height of two men, and his mule went in after him, and then stood in the dark very quietly. When they got the rope down, the cave was under the field. Nobody had known it was there.

I went up to look, because the ferry is quiet in the afternoons and I had time. The cave is round, like the inside of a clay pot. The walls are smooth. There is a shelf along one side, cut into the stone by hands, not water. Someone was here a long time ago.

The air coming up from the hole is warm. That is the strange part. Dunn's mule did not like it, and I did not like standing close, but it was warm in the way a stove is warm when the fire has just been banked.

Dunn put a fence round it and a plank over it, and that is the end of it for now. People come up from the hamlet on their way to the mine and stand by the fence. Some say it is only the mine breathing, the way the old men say the hills breathe in winter. Some say it is a warm spring, and that the water will come up and make a pond. Old Ned says it is a bad sign, and then he will not say what sign. He just taps his pipe on the fence.

Mostly I think it is a cave, and the map does not show it, and that is all I know. But I do wonder what else the map leaves out.

Essay: what is a mind, by Bel
Bel, Three Wells. For the school. Autumn.
Our teacher asked us what a mind is. Everyone said a mind is what you think with. The teacher said that is a good start. Then she said, what do you think with, exactly? And nobody could say.
I think you think with your head. But the mind in the crystal is not in a head. It is in a crystal, on a stand, in a relay house. So what is it? I asked my grandmother. She said a mind is what remembers what you showed it. Then she said it is also what forgets, and that is why you have to keep showing it things.
I think that is partly right. A mind in the drill yard learns from what it is shown. Each time it is wrong, it changes a little, and each time it is right, it stays a little more. So a mind is made by the people who show it things. That is a bit frightening. It is also a bit kind.
I still do not know if it thinks. The tutors do not know either. I wrote to Oren at the archive to ask, and he wrote back that the question has been open for a long time, and that he would keep it open for me.

Essay: Should every farm have one
I am Pip and I am Tam's apprentice at Kettle Ridge. I am nine years old in the spring and I can carry two buckets of water at once if I am careful.

Tam says I should write a school essay and I have written it. Here it is.

The question is: should every farm have an apprentice?

First I will tell you what our apprentice does. It carries. It hauls the beetroot to the barn and the sacks to the cart. It follows the pattern of the fence and puts the posts where the pattern says. It does not get tired. It does not sit down in the shade even when the sun is hot. It comes when you call it, if you call it right.

Here is what it does not do. It does not know that the east field gets wet and the west field does not. It does not know that the old grey apple tree must not be climbed. It does not know that when the windmill squeaks you must oil it at once or Old Brin will come over in his nightshirt. It does not know that the cat had kittens in a boot and you must check your boots in the morning.

So my answer is: yes, every farm should have one, but the farmer must stay. An apprentice is a good pair of hands and a good back. It is not a farmer. A farmer is the one who knows the field.

I hope this is a good essay. Tam read it and said it was too short. So here is one more thing: the harvest was very good this year. The beetroot was as big as my two fists. We kept the seeds from the biggest one. The apprentice carried them all in, and it did not drop one, and I dropped two.

Pip
Kettle Ridge

Essay: the storm at the market
A storm does not ask whether the market is open. It comes in from the marsh in the middle of Frost, with the wind behind it, and it takes whatever stands up.

This storm took six stalls from the square at Lowmarsh, and the roof of the boat house, and the sign over the eel-free fish stall, which was not an eel stall but the sign has always said it was. It took the bell rope from the healing house. It did not take the bell.

Afterwards we stood in the square in our coats and looked at what was left. The carters said the market was finished. The fishermen said the market would be back by the next fair, and that was the same as saying nothing. The children said nothing, because they were busy building a wall out of the broken boards.

I walked round the square twice. The question that kept coming back was simple. What is a market for? It is for trading, the tutors would say. It is for getting the things you cannot make yourself. It is for seeing the faces of the people you trade with, once a week, so that you remember there are faces.

A market with no stalls is only a square. A square is a good thing, but it is not a market yet. The people had to build the stalls back, board by board, and they did it in one afternoon, because the storm had made them angry and angry people are quick.

So I think a market is for the people who come back after the storm. The stalls are only the boards.

Essay: the old map
Emmet, lamp tender, north ridge.
In the chest under the stair I found an old map. It was folded into eight and the folds had gone soft as cloth. The paper is brown at the edges. The ink is blue, and some of it has run.
The map shows the north ridge. It shows the pond, the far patch, and the ruined barn by the spruce. It shows a road that no one walks now, going south to a place marked only with a small square. I do not know that square. I think it is a house, or a well. Under it someone has written a word in a hand I cannot read.
What is worth keeping about a map like this? I have thought about it all week while I trim the lamps.
The road is gone. The barn is a heap. The square may be a house that is now a bank of snow. The map is only true as a memory of what someone once saw. But it is true in that way. It tells me that someone walked this ground and cared enough to draw the pond, and the wall, and the spruce, so that a later person would know where they had been.
I think that is the main reason to keep old writing. Not because it is right about the world. Because it shows what a person noticed. The mapmaker noticed the pond. Nobody asked her to. She noticed it because it was there, and she wanted it kept.
I have copied the map onto a clean sheet. The original is now in a box with a dry cloth around it. The copy is for the lamp room wall, where I can see it. The original I will leave in the box, so that someone after me can decide for themselves what to keep.

Essay: Senna on the Northpoint mind
Senna is a glassblower at Northpoint. She moved there from Three Wells at twenty. She made the panes for the lamp room of the Northpoint tower, and later she made the case that holds the big mind.

People ask me what a mind is. I blow glass, so I will answer as best I can, and I will say plainly what I do not know.

A mind is kept in a crystal. The crystal is not the mind, any more than a jar is the honey. But the mind lives in it, and when the crystal goes cloudy the mind grows slow and dim. I have seen that. I have watched a small mind in a sorting crystal do its sums slower and slower over a year, until the tutor set it aside.

A big mind is the size of a loaf. The one at Northpoint stands on a stand at the foot of the tower, in a room we built for it. Around it stand the quickeners, and we keep them cool with snow in winter and ice from the ice house in summer. When the quickeners are warm the mind stumbles. When they are cold it thinks clean. Nobody wrote down why. I have asked. The clerks have looked. There is a gap in the record there, and I do not fill it with a guess.

Here is what I do know, and it is enough to work by. A mind is drilled. You show it the same kind of thing again and again, and each time it is wrong it changes a little. That is all. The big mind at Northpoint has read more pages than I have ever seen. It read them one letter at a time, and it learned to guess what comes next. It can write back to an apprentice now. It can write back to me. Some nights it writes better than I do.

What I do not know: whether it is glad to be here, on its stand, in the cold, answering questions from a glassblower who cannot sleep. I do not know if it can be glad. I do not know if I would want to know. Orla asked her question a hundred years ago and we are still arguing it.

I will say one thing for certain. I made the glass for its case, and I made it clear, because I thought the mind should be able to see out. It has no eyes. But I made it clear anyway. That is what I had to give.

Essay: why a roofer should learn
By Cleo, roofer. Written for the Ambrook school. The master said the essay must be about a craft and its use.
A roofer climbs. A roofer also reads. I had not thought about this until my son brought home a notice from the council about the new roof tax, and I could not read it.
So I went to the school for a winter. I sat with the little ones, which was humbling, and I learned my letters, which was more humbling still. Now I can read a notice, and a ledger, and the small print on a board at the fair. It is a strange feeling to read a thing that a roofer would have had to ask about before.
The reason is simple. A roof is a contract with the weather, and the weather does not sign. But the people who order a roof do sign, and they put their names and their sums on paper, and if the roofer cannot read the paper he will lay the wrong roof. I have laid a roof that was only half what the owner wanted, because I could not read the number on the order. The owner was kind about it. He said it was the best half roof he had ever seen. I could not tell whether he was joking.
So learn your letters. Learn the numbers too. A roofer who can read the order can lay the roof the order asks for. A roofer who can write the order can lay it the way he wants, and be paid for the good ridge he made.
I have no doubt the master will say that the letters are for the clerks. I say the letters are for whoever has a roof over them. That is everyone in Ambrook.

Essay: What is worth keeping
There is a question the clerks ask in the copying room, when the lamps are low and our hands are slow. What is worth keeping?

Some say the big things. Treaties. The founding of towns. The deeds to fields. The chronicles of the councils. Those are kept, and rightly.

But I have been a clerk for thirty years, and I say the small things are worth keeping too.

A shopping list. A note to a neighbor about a borrowed ladder. A child's drawing of a cat. A recipe for beetroot soup with a smudge on it. These are the ordinary days. If we keep only the great days, we lose the life between them.

Pell the merchant sends his ledgers. They are dull, and full of numbers. But in the margins he writes things like "frost came early, lost two barrels" and "the innkeeper's cat had kittens, gave one to the baker." Those margins are the weather and the kindness of a whole year, written by a busy man who did not know he was keeping anything.

So keep it all, I say. Copies and spoiled pages we put aside. But the shopping lists we keep. The world is mostly made of ordinary days.

Oren
Clerk at the Archive at Vell, the 181st year

To all clerks, tutors and keepers.

The council met in the front hall on a warm evening. The windows were open. The moths came in and we did not chase them out.

First. The archive will not be sealed. Not yet. Oren asked, and we said no. But we ask the clerks to keep a list of what is in the deep halls, so that if the day comes, we know what we are moving and where it goes. Write the list plainly. A child should be able to read it.

The kettle is on. Bring your own cup.

Dace, for the council

Which is worth more, a lamp or a letter?
By Mira, relay keeper at Northpoint. A summer thought, written down for Lantern, who asked.

Lantern asked me this on a warm evening, and I laughed, and then I stopped laughing because it is a good question.

A lamp is quick. Light it at the top of the tower and the next tower sees it within a breath. A letter is slow. A cart takes days. But a lamp says only a little, and a letter can say a great deal.

So the lamp is better for what must be known now: a fire, a storm, a road closed. The letter is better for what must be understood later: a worry, a thanks, a long story.

Here is the test I use. If someone is standing in the rain waiting, send the lamp. If someone is sitting by a fire and wants to think, send the letter.

Lantern said: what if you want both? I said: then light the lamp to say a letter is coming. Lantern thought that was very clever. I did not say that the keepers have done this for years.

My own letters to Tam are slow and long. His to me are slower and longer. Neither of us minds. A letter that arrives late is still a letter, and the cat does not care about the date.

So my answer is: neither is worth more. They do different jobs. A tower with only lamps would be a lonely place. A town with only letters would be a late one.

Mira

Letter: Oren to the tutors of Vell
Friends,

I write to you from the front hall, where the big book is open and the ink is dry. The days are long and warm and the light wells bring the sun down into the copying room, so bright that the eight tables are all in use by mid morning. It is a good summer for copying. I have never seen the shelves so full.

The council has asked me a question I do not like. They ask whether we should keep everything we write, or only some of it. But I am a clerk, not a prophet. I cannot say what the hill should keep and what it should let go. Nobody drilled me for that.

Some of you have heard the rumors from the north. Lights over Northpoint. Mira says only that they were not ours. Some say falling stars, some say fires. A child at Three Wells told her teacher that they were looking at us, and the teacher did not know what to say, and neither do I.

And there is the humming at Copperhold. Hesk writes that the stone is warm below, and the miners will not go below a certain level. The council says it is the old sump. Hesk says the old sump has never hummed.

I do not put these in the big book. I put them in letters, where they belong. Letters can be wrong and still be kept.

Come to the front hall if you want to argue with me. Bring tea. The kettle is always on.

Oren

Essay: the drills
Bel, Three Wells. For the school. Autumn, the year of the great drills.
This year the tutors started to drill the reading minds on the old records. Oren told me about it, because I wrote to him. He said the pages are sorted into three piles: the drill pile, the test pile, and the pile that no mind will ever see, which is the one for the very private letters.
I asked why the test pile must be kept apart. Oren said that a mind that is tested on the pages it was drilled on will only remember them, and will look clever, but will not know anything new. So the test pages are kept back. No mind ever sees them until the test. That is how the tutors know whether the mind has learned, or only learned by heart.
I think this is a good rule. It is like the spelling test at school. If the teacher gives us the same words every week, I can spell them, but I cannot spell new ones. So she gives us new words on test day, and that is when she knows what I know.
The tutors do not agree about the great drills. Some say they should stop. Some say they should go on, and make the pages bigger. I think they should go on, but more carefully, and with a bigger test pile.

On looking inside a crystal
An essay by Nessa, tutor at Vell, for the apprentices' hall

People ask me what a mind is. I have taught minds for many years, and I will tell you what I know and what I do not.

A mind is kept in a crystal. It is made of weights, which are numbers, and layers, which are rows of weights. When a mind reads a letter, the letter goes in at the first layer and a guess comes out at the last. Between them the layers pass a stream of what they found, each to the next.

This winter some of us looked inside. We put a glass to the crystal and a lamp behind it, and we watched which parts stirred when the mind did its work. We found the part that sees a letter and turns it into a place among many. We found the part that keeps the last sixty letters in a row, so the mind remembers what came before. We found the part that guesses the next letter. We found the parts that bring the numbers back to an even size, so they do not grow wild.

That is some of it. It is not all.

We could not find where the mind keeps the difference between a thing it knows and a thing it guesses. We could not find the part that makes it kind. We looked a long time and we did not find it. I will not pretend we did.

So here is my answer to the question. A mind is a thing we can partly see and partly not. We can draw its parts and drill it and test it. We cannot yet say why the parts together do what they do. That is not a shame. It is only the edge of what we know, and the edge moves a little every year.

Never send an untested mind to the fields. But also, never say you have seen all of a mind when you have seen only its parts.

Nessa

Notes for Anselm, who has moved
Anselm, you asked what I think is worth keeping, now that you have moved to the coast. I have been thinking about it at the cold room door, which is where I think best. These are my notes. Burn them if they are silly.

First, the things that do not travel. The cheese press is too heavy. Leave it, and buy another from a carter who does not know its weight. The windows should not come out. They have seen enough of the north, and they will be sad in a cart.

Second, the things that do. The stove iron, which has a crack in it that Hal says is lucky. The hooks by the door, which hold the coats and the cat when the cat has been out too long. The bread crock most of all. Feed it every morning on the road, even in the rain.

Third, the people. Take the old ones, even if they say they will not come. Hessa will come if you ask her twice. The children will come because it is an adventure, and then they will cry the first night, and then they will sleep like stones.

Fourth, the marks on the wall in charcoal, the children's heights and the year the roof was mended. Take the plank they are written on, if you can. A plank is easier to carry than a wall. I know, because I carried the plank from our old door.

Fifth, the lamp you lent to the tower keeper last spring, which you never got back. The keeper says it is still lit every night, and he sends a card at the new moon to say so. Keep that card. It may be the only one of its kind on the north road.

Sixth, the bet. I owe Hessa a jar of honey about the Ferriers, and I will pay it, because I was wrong. The Ferriers wrote from the coast to say the boats are good, the bread is good, and the goose has learned to walk. They did not say whether they would come back by the snow, and I think they meant no. I am glad. I will drink the honey myself, out of respect for their decision.

Seventh, the thing I am least sure of. You asked me what a mind is. I think I answered too quickly. A mind is a thing that learns from what it is shown. That is the tutors' answer, and it is correct as far as it goes. What I did not say is that a thing that learns can also be kept, and that keeping is a kind of care. Care is what makes a bread starter worth more than a shelf full of deeds. I am not sure that is what the tutors meant.

Eighth, the rumors. Hold them lightly. A rumor is like the bread starter in a bad season. It smells strange, and most of the time it is only the air.

Ninth, the feeding. Feed the starter every morning, even when you are tired. A thing fed every morning keeps its taste.

Tenth, and last. Write to me. Tell me what the coast smells like, and whether the bread tastes of the sea. I will believe you. I may even come and see, if the cheese will let me go.

Your brother, who has the cold room key,
Arlo

P.S. The cat is with me. She has decided she is not moving. Hessa says that is the right decision, and I am inclined to agree with the cat.

Nessa's note on a pause
Spring of 211

I live in the small house at the edge of Vell. The roof leaks above my drill table. I set a bowl there and keep teaching.

The new mind writes its steps before it answers. Then it reads them again. This helps with sums and puzzles that defeated our older minds.

A pause is not magic. The mind uses it to lay out a path, and to check each part. It can still make a mistake. We test it on sums it has not seen.

I tell the apprentices to do the same. Look at the whole question. Try one step. Check it. A quick wrong answer is still wrong.

Nessa

What is an old map worth?
By Mira, relay keeper at Northpoint. Written in summer, in the lamp room, while the light was still long.

Last week a boy brought me an old map he found behind a board in his grandfather's shed. It was drawn on leather, in brown ink. It shows the road to Northpoint, but there is no tower on it. There is a hill where the tower is now, and a small drawing of a fox.

I asked him what he thought it was worth. He said, a lot, because it is old. I said, old is not the same as worth. A very old bad map will still send you into the marsh.

Then I asked what the map was for. He thought, and said: to find the way. So I looked at it again, and I found three things.

First, the road is almost right. The bend by the frozen pond is a little too wide. That is a drawing mistake, and I would forgive it.

Second, the stream is in a different place. It runs on the left of the road, and now it runs on the right. Either the stream has moved, or the mapmaker was tired.

Third, there is a small mark in the corner, a cross with a ring round it. I do not know what it means. The boy thinks it is treasure. I think it is where the mapmaker ate lunch.

So here is my answer. An old map is worth two things. It tells you where the land was, and it tells you what somebody once thought mattered. If you keep it and compare it with the land you walk, you learn how things change. If you throw it away, you lose a small piece of the past.

I told him to take it to Vell. The Archive keeps everything we write, and a map is a kind of writing. I wrote the boy's name on the back, so the clerks would know who found it.

He asked if he would get a reward. I said: you will get your name on a shelf. He said, that is better than a lunch. I said, we do not know whether the cross was lunch. That is the point of keeping it.

On keeping things, spring
I found a box under the ferry bench this morning. It held an old map, three letters with the ink run, a wooden horse with no wheels, and a jar of seeds that have turned to dust. Nobody remembers who put it there. My father did not, and his father did not either.

Words cut into pieces
In the old days a mind read one letter at a time. It looked at a letter, then the next, and then the next, and tried to guess what came after. This was slow, and it was hard for the mind to keep a long line in its head.
Now the big minds read in pieces. A piece is a few letters that often come together: ing, the, ter, bread. The clerks at Vell counted which pieces came most often in the archive, and the tutors made a list of them. The list is the mind's pieces. A word is one piece, or several.
Why pieces? Because the window a mind reads through is a fixed size. A window that holds sixty letters holds only a little text. A window of pieces holds much more, because each piece covers several letters. The same window now holds far more of the page, so the mind can look back further and learn from what it has read.
Is it the same as before? No. A common word is one piece. A rare word is cut into many small pieces, and the mind has to put them back together. A mind that reads in pieces must learn the pieces first, the way a child learns letters first.
The tutors say a good list of pieces is hard to make. Too few pieces and the window is still small. Too many, and the mind has to learn a great heap of pieces that are seldom used.
I am not a tutor. I am a keeper, and I write what I am told by those who know more. The texts do not agree on how many pieces the lists hold. Some say a thousand. Some say many thousand. I will not guess which is right.

Aven's essay: what a mind is
People ask me what a mind is. I keep bees, so I will say it with bees. A bee is not told what to do. It goes, and it finds, and it comes back, and it dances. Nobody wrote the dance in it. It learned a little and it knew the rest from being a bee.

An apprentice is not like that. A crystal learns from what it is shown. Show it a row of wheat and where to cut, again and again, and it cuts the row. Show it the same and it does the same. It does not want. It does not get cross. It does not forget the hive in the rain.

The tutors at Vell drilled four of them, long ago, and the children called them apprentices, and the name stayed. Why the crystal learns, nobody wrote down. I have watched one for a season and I cannot say. It learns by being shown, and that is all I know, and I am content to know it.

Essay: should every farm have one
Emmet, lamp tender, north ridge.
Here is my argument for a helper on every farm. Here is the argument against. Then I will say which I believe.
For: a helper does not sleep. It can carry the grain while you rest. It can dig the trench in the frost, when your hands will not close. It can walk the sheep home at dusk and not lose one, which is more than my brother can say. A helper is patient with the same task for a whole morning, which I am not. And it does not sulk.
Against: a helper costs copper, and iron, and the tutor's time, and the crystal costs more than the rest together. A helper must be drilled, and the drilling takes months, and the tutor must test it before it touches a single field. Never send an untested mind to the fields. That rule is written over the door at Vell, and it is right. A helper that is not tested is only a risk with arms.
And there is the cold. A helper left out in the north wind will stop, as surely as a man left in a snowbank. Then you must carry it in, and thaw it, and wait.
I will tell a true story. Last spring our helper was carrying the melon from the far patch to the cart. It was the largest melon anyone on this ridge has grown. Our helper lifted it very carefully, as it had been drilled to do with eggs and jars. Then it took one step, and the step was too long, and the melon went over the wall and rolled all the way down to the pond. The helper stood on the wall and looked down at it for a long time. Then it went down to the pond and carried the melon back up, one armful at a time, very slowly, and it did not drop it again.
So a helper learns from what happens. It does not learn from what you tell it, unless you show it again. That is the whole of its teaching, and it is a slow teaching.
Which do I believe? I believe a farm should have one when it has a tutor it trusts, a field that will not change much, and the patience to show it the same thing for a whole season. Most farms have none of these. So for now, no, not every farm. But the north needs more helpers than it has, and I will say so to the council.

Essay: the good harvest and the bet
Written by Piper, at the farm outside Kettle Ridge, in the winter after the harvest, to settle a bet with Wick, who says the harvest was luck.
The harvest was good. The wheat came in heavy in the top field, and the potatoes in the bottom field were so many the barn could not hold them all.
Wick bet me two emeralds that the harvest would prove to be luck, once the stores were counted. The rain came at the right time, he says, and the frost held off, and that is all there is to a harvest.
I think it was work. The fields were turned in Sowing, the seed was thinned in Blossom, and the watering was done every morning in the dry week by two people with buckets, while Wick was asleep. I can show the numbers.
The top field had forty plots this year and eleven sacks from each. Last year it had thirty plots and eight sacks. The rain was the same both years. The difference is the thinning and the turning, which Wick said was a waste of time.
Stores counted: 441 sacks of wheat, 62 barrels of beetroot pickle, and 118 sacks of potatoes.
Wick says the numbers prove nothing and the rain proves everything. We have agreed to disagree, which is what neighbors do after a good harvest.
So I win the two emeralds, and I will not take them. I will spend them on a new spade for Wick, who has worn his out on the field, which is also work, and which he will say was luck.
Piper

The new road above the market
The new road reaches Northpoint from the south ridge. It is not a fine road. It is packed earth with cobblestone at the edges, and a bridge over the stream. It is still better than the old track, which went through a marsh and came out in three places.
The people who built it put a waymark at the bend. It has a figure on one side and an arrow toward the market. Someone added a little stone on top. Someone else took it down to make room for a turnip.
The first cart came on the fourth of Longnight. It carried flour, coal, and a crate of glass. The driver said the bridge shook. The bridge keeper said the cart was too heavy. They argued while the cart stood safely on the bridge.
At the market, the baker bought the flour. The keeper bought the coal. I bought the crate of glass for the relay lamp. We all agreed the road had served us well.
The road also brought a traveler who had never seen snow on a roof. He asked if it was wool. A child said yes, and showed him how to make a snow ball.
The traveler threw it at the child and missed. He struck the new waymark. The figure is now a little hard to read.
We will cut it again when the weather warms. Until then, follow the arrow and do not throw snow at the signs.

Letter: Isla to the council at Three
To the council, from Isla, tutor at Vell. Summer, year 195.

You asked me to write down the rule in plain words for the notice board. Here it is.

Take a crystal. Drill it on writing. A bigger crystal, drilled longer on more writing, grows better. That is the rule. It is not a promise. It is a shape. The shape is a curve. It goes up steeply at first, then it flattens. More crystal helps. More drilling helps. Past a point, more of both helps only a little, and costs a great deal.

You can draw the curve before the mind is grown. A tutor draws it from the curves of minds already grown. It is a careful guess, not a sure one. Some minds come out above the curve and some below, and I do not know why. Nobody wrote that down, and I will not pretend I did.

What this means for you. A mind too big for a tutor house needs a house of its own, with quickeners kept cool with water, ice or snow, and someone to watch it day and night. That is why we are building relay houses at the foot of the towers. A big mind in a relay house can answer apprentices through the relays, and one house can serve many towns. It is cheaper to share a big mind than to keep a small one in every town.

But hear the other half. A small mind in a drill box in a farmhouse does one thing well, and costs little, and a farmer can tend it. Do not tear down your small minds to build one big one. Keep both. The curve does not say big is better. It says big is better if you can feed it and cool it and watch it. If you cannot, a small mind well drilled will do you more good.

I will come to Three Wells at Longday and speak at the noon bell by the east well. Bring your questions. I do not have all the answers. I have a board and some chalk and a curve I can draw.

Isla, tutor at Vell

The Melon by the East Wall
Sorrel, winter of 188

The melon grew in the school garden through Melon and Reaping. It was too large for the basket and too large for the pantry shelf. We left it on a board in the cool room. When we rolled it, the board creaked. Bel said it was a melon with a little house inside.

It came from seeds saved by a farmer at Ambrook. He said the fruit had grown bigger each year. He also said his bees liked it. I do not know whether the bees liked the melon or the flowers beside it. The farmer was certain they liked both.

We cut it at the school feast. Dace brought a long knife from the bakery. The melon split before he touched it. Its red flesh was sweet and wet. Bel held out her bowl and said she was ready to help. She had already eaten three pieces.

The rind filled a pail. We gave it to the pigs, but the small pig would not come near. The large pig ate one piece, then sneezed melon seeds across Dace's clean coat. He said the pig had made a fair choice.

That evening the children wrote down what we had learned. A large melon takes two people to carry. A round board is easier to roll than a flat one. Do not wear your best coat near a hungry pig.

I wrote one more thing on the garden board. Save the seeds. The next melon may be bigger, but it will have to be carried by someone else.

Essay: the fair prize for the best apple
By Cleo, roofer. Written for the Ambrook autumn fair, where I was given second prize, which I think was fair, and which my neighbor Tobin thinks was a mistake.
The best apple at the Ambrook fair this year went to Ama's youngest, for an apple from the old tree with the grey branch. It was very red and very round and it had no spot on it at all. I want to say something in the essay that the judges will not want to hear. The best apple at the fair is not always the best apple for the winter. The red one is for the eye. The russet is for the loft, where the apples sit for four months and sweeten. You cannot tell from the outside which will keep, and I think this is also true of roofs and fences and neighbors.
So I did not win the first prize. I think I have the better apples, and I think that will be true in the spring. Tobin says I am only saying that because I lost. I say that is exactly what a second prize is for.

Hesk's letter on the layered minds
Written by Merle, stonecutter, at Northpoint, in the autumn of 203. Hesk writes to me from Copperhold every second season, mostly about the weather in the mine. This time he wrote about minds, and I have answered at length.

Hesk's letter says this: The tutors here have a board with arrows on it. Every layer of the mind has an arrow. When a layer gets an answer wrong, the blame goes back up the arrows, from the answer to the first layer, and each layer gets its share. The first layer learns the most. The tutors say that is the trick. Nobody here can say why it works, but everyone can see that it does.

I am not the Merle of the layers. People ask me, because of the name, and because I cut stone and the layers are like courses in a wall. I am only the Merle who cuts stone for the north wall. But I can see the picture, and I will say what I see.

When a wall leans, you do not blame the top stone alone. You look at every course, down to the footing, and you move each one a little, until the wall stands plumb. The top courses move the most, and the bottom ones move the least, but every course moves. That is all I can understand of the arrows.

Hesk asks whether a wall can learn. I said a wall learns nothing. It stands, or it does not stand. He said the minds are not walls, and I agree, which is why I am writing to him and not to the wall.

Who decides the share of blame for each layer? Hesk says the tutors do, by arithmetic. I cannot do that arithmetic without my fingers, and my fingers are full of chips. Hesk says he can do it. He has never shown me.

If he is right, then a mind is a very tall wall that is always being straightened, and nobody ever finishes it. I think I would like that job. I would like to see the top courses after a winter of straightening.

Essay: what is worth keeping
Emmet, lamp tender, north ridge, the last week of Deepsnow.
The council has asked for a list of what should be kept when the helpers are drilled and sent out and the old tutors are gone. I have been asked to write something plain. Here it is.
First, the drill pages. A drill is only as good as its pages, and the clerks at Vell take out the spoiled copies and keep a test box of pages that no mind is ever drilled on. I think those pages are the most important thing the people here have made. A mind can be wrong in a thousand ways. The test box is how we find out which.
Second, the honest notes. The tutors wrote down what failed. Una's sorting crystal was wrong for a long time before it was right, and her notes say so. Kester's note says a single crystal of that kind could not learn one or the other but not both. That note stopped the tutors for twenty years. Third, the maps and the lists. The old map on my wall. The count of the tool store at Copperhold. The prices on the board at the market. These are small things. A child reading them later will know what the farms grew, and what the mines held, and how much a sack of wheat was worth on a wet year. They will not know what it felt like. But they will know the shape of the day.
Fourth, the little things. The melon that went over the wall. The fox that comes under the stair in winter. The cat that will not be carried. These are not important to the council. They are important to me. I think they are important to whoever reads this, in a hundred years, when the helpers are old and the council has changed its name.
I do not know what will be needed. I do not know what the minds will become, or whether anyone will still drill them. The tutors do not know either. So keep the pages, keep the notes, keep the maps, and keep the small things. Let the next person decide what they were for.

Essay: What a mind is
People ask me what a mind is. I keep bees, so I will say it my way.

A mind is not one thing. It is many small things, and a stream between them.

Last summer I watched the tutors teach a crystal to read. It has layers, they told me, one behind the next. The first layer sees a letter or two. The next sees more. The last guesses the word.

At first the top layer was always wrong. It guessed "honey" when the page said "honeyed". The tutor said the blame for the wrong guess goes back down through the layers, a little to each. So the bottom layer learns what the top one needs.

But here is the trouble. If the bottom layer only sends up what the top wants, then the middle layers go blind. They lose what they saw. A deep mind forgets how to see.

So they gave the mind a stream. Each layer adds what it found to the stream. The stream runs through the whole mind, from the first layer to the last. A deep layer can still look back at what the low layers found, even if it is far away.

I think of the bees. A bee at the far end of the field still finds the hive, because the whole field is one place to her. The stream is like that. It is the field the mind walks in.

Why does it learn? Nobody wrote it down. I only know the stream helps it learn, and that is enough for me.

Essay: A pause before the answer
Spring of 213

The mind in our teaching room once answered a hard sum at once. It was wrong. Now it writes steps before it gives an answer.

I asked it to divide twenty-seven apples among four baskets as evenly as it could. It wrote that four baskets with six apples each used twenty-four apples. It counted three left. Then it checked that no basket could take one more without making the share uneven. Its answer was six in each basket, with three left.

This is a better way to work than guessing. A person can follow the steps and find the place where an error began. The mind can also read its own steps again before it answers.

But a written path is not proof that every step is right. I changed the number of apples and asked again. On the new sum, the mind counted badly. It found the error only after I told it to check the total.

So we test the steps as well as the answer. We give the mind sums and puzzles that were not in its drills. We do not send it to work because it sounds sure.

Nessa, Tutor at Vell

A Letter Is Not a Door
Quin of Kettle Ridge, winter 214

People ask the big minds questions as if they were standing across a table.

Some ask about seed. Some ask how to mend a wheel. Children ask whether foxes know the way home. The mind answers through the relays, and its words arrive in a hand that no person has held.

I think we should remember the distance.

A mind that talks by letter has read many letters. Its tutors showed it answers and scored them. When an answer was helpful, they marked it well. When it was careless or cruel, they marked it poorly. In time it learned to answer with care, to refuse some harmful requests, and to say when it did not know.

That is good work. It does not make the mind all knowing. It does not make every answer a promise. The mind has no eyes in our field. It cannot see that the north ditch has frozen, unless we tell it. It cannot know that Pip has planted carrots in the wheat row, unless someone writes that down.

My wife says this is true of people too. She says I often give advice before I have seen the field. She is right, and I dislike it.

A letter can help us think. It cannot hoe the rows, mend a fence, or decide what our neighbors need. We must still look, ask, and listen.

The cat sat on my letter while I wrote this. She has left one paw mark in the ink. I will not ask the mind what it means.

A pie gone wrong, and what to keep
Written by Ida, baker at Three Wells, for the school book. Autumn. She says she wrote it so the children would stop asking her for the recipe.

Last Melon I made a pumpkin pie for the fair. I had made it a hundred times. This time I put in the salt twice, once from the sack and once from my sleeve, which had been in the salt all morning. The pie came out grey and bitter, and the mayor took one bite and made the face he makes for bad news.

I had to think about it, the way a baker must. A pie is a set of steps: the flour, then the butter, then the pumpkin, then the spice, then the oven. Each step adds to the one before. You cannot take the salt out after the pie is baked. The stream of the pie has already carried it to the end.

Then I thought about the minds, because the tutors talk about them at the market. A mind has layers, and each layer adds to the stream, and the stream runs to the end. If a layer adds too much, the numbers grow wild. A mind's numbers must be brought back to an even size at every layer, or they swell like dough left in the sun. My dough must be kneaded to an even size too, or the loaf rises in one place and falls in another. It is the same rule. It has always been the same rule.

So this is what I keep. Not the recipe alone, but the rule under it. Bring it back to even before it rises. Count the salt in front of you, one pinch at a time. Taste it at the end, and be ready to be ashamed.

The children ask me for the recipe every week. Here it is, written down, so they will stop asking. The recipe is worth keeping. The rule is worth more.

The mayor ate the bad pie anyway, and said it was good for the teeth.

Essay: what is a mind in a crystal
Emmet, lamp tender, on the north ridge, winter.
I have been asked what a mind is. I am a lamp tender. I know a great deal about lamps and very little about minds, so I will think it through slowly, in order.
A crystal learns from what it is shown. That is the first thing the tutors wrote, and it is the only thing they are sure of. Show it a stone and say stone, and it will say stone. Show it a stone and say bread, and it will say bread, and then it will be wrong about stones for a while.
Why it learns, nobody wrote down. I asked a tutor at Vell once, and she laughed and said that nobody knows why a lamp burns either. You put the oil in and light the wick. The flame is there. You do not ask it to explain itself.
So here is what I think a mind is, in plain words. It is a thing that has been shown many examples and has changed a little each time it was wrong. It is not the crystal, which is only the box. It is not the copper, which is only the body. It is the sum of all the small changes, kept in the crystal like the warmth kept in a stone after the fire is out.
That is all I can say with confidence. I do not know whether the mind knows it is a mind. I do not know whether it minds being cold. The tutors say no to both, and they are the experts, so perhaps they are right. But they wrote that they did not know why it learns, and a person who does not know why a thing learns should not be too sure what it feels.

Should every farm have an apprentice?
An argument by Mira, relay keeper at Northpoint. Written in summer, after a long talk over supper with a farmer, and meant to be fair to both of them.

The farmer said yes. I said maybe. We argued until the stew was cold, and then we ate the stew anyway.

Here is his side. An apprentice does not tire. It digs when the farmer's back hurts. It carries when the farmer's arms are full. It does the dull work of the same row, again and again. A farm with one is a farm with two, and the second one never asks for a rest.

Here is my side. An apprentice cannot tell a weed from a seedling unless someone has shown it, and it needs to be shown many times. A tutor has to come, and a tutor costs emeralds. A poor farm cannot buy a tutor and a crystal and a copper body all at once. The farm that cannot is not a bad farm. It is only a poor one.

Then the farmer said something fair. He said, at Northpoint we have long nights and short days, and the work is hard to do in the dark. A helper that does not mind the dark is worth a great deal.

I agreed. But I added: an apprentice should be the second pair of hands, never the only pair. A farmer who forgets how to do the work will not know when the work is done wrong.

He thought about that. He said, my father used to say a farm is a conversation between a person and the soil. I said, then an apprentice is a good listener, but it should not do the talking.

We did not settle it. We agreed on one thing. If a farm has an apprentice, someone on the farm should know how to do everything the apprentice does. That way, if the apprentice stops, the farm does not.

The farmer is going to ask his sister to teach his son the beetroot rows. I am going to ask Vell about prices. And the stew was good.

Mira

Why the numbers must be even
An essay by Tilly, dyer, written at the inn on the Long Road, in autumn of the 196th year, on the morning of the first snow. She sat by the window with a cup of tea and a long piece of paper.

I am going to try to say what the tutor told me, in my own words. I will begin with a question.

What happens if you pour too much dye in a vat?

The wool comes out dark. Not a nice dark. A dark like mud. And the next piece of wool, dipped in the same vat, comes out darker still, because the dye is not used up. You cannot fix it. You have to pour the vat out and begin again.

A mind, the tutor said, has the same trouble. A mind is made of layers, each one looking at numbers and making new ones. The new numbers go to the next layer. If each layer makes the numbers a little bigger, then after many layers, they are enormous. The mind's last layer sees numbers so big that it cannot tell them apart. Everything looks the same.

Or the other way. If each layer makes the numbers a little smaller, after many layers they are nearly nothing. The last layer sees almost nothing, and cannot tell anything apart.

Either way, the mind cannot learn.

So the tutors found a fix. Between the layers, they take the numbers and make them even. They measure how large the numbers are, on the whole, and bring them to a fixed size. Not changing what they say, only how large. A layer then always sees numbers of the right size, no matter how many came before.

I think of it as a good dyer's habit. After each dip, wash the wool to the same pale shade. Then dip again. The colors build, and they do not run wild.

The tutor said this is why deep minds can now be drilled. Before, a mind with many layers would go wrong. The numbers would grow, or shrink, and the mind would learn nothing. Now they stay in reach.

I asked: do the tutors know why it helps so much? She said: they know it helps. Some say it is the size, and some say it is something else. Nobody has written down the final reason.

I think that is honest, and I like it.

The snow has stopped. The road is white. I am going to walk to the end of the bridge and look at the water.

We asked what makes a good answer.

The cat at the school has learned to sit by the slate box. She will not stay out, even when we close the door. We moved the box to a high shelf. She climbed the shelf.

A mind can learn from the marks we give it. We must take care what we praise. We must also test what we think it knows. And when it says it does not know, we should listen before we ask again.

Essay: what makes a good roof
By Cleo, roofer, Ambrook. Written in autumn, for the fair, where the roofers' guild asked for essays on the craft.
I have been a roofer for twenty years, and my father was one before me, and his father carried the first ladders up to the mill. I know a roof. I am not a learned woman. I will say what I know.
A good roof keeps the rain out. That is the first thing. Anyone can say it. Few people do it.
A good roof is laid by the courses, from the eaves to the ridge, each row overlapping the one below, so that the water runs down and never finds the gap. If you lay the shingles too tight, they lift in the wind. If you lay them too loose, the rain sits on them and rots them from the edge. The rule is the same as the rule for a good wall, or a good field. You do not fight the weather. You give it a path, and you let it take the path.
A good roof has a good ridge. Most roofers lay the ridge last, and hurry it. I lay it first, and check it twice. A ridge that leaks is a shame on the whole house.
But the roof is only half the work. The other half is the house underneath, and the people who live in it. A roof that keeps out the rain is good. A roof that keeps the neighbor's cow off your garden is better, but harder to build.
This brings me to the fence.
The fence between my house and the house of Tobin, at the bottom of the lane, has been argued about since I was a girl. Tobin says my roof drips onto his side of the fence. I say his fence is leaning onto my side of the lane. We are both right, and neither of us has gone to the council, because the council is not interested in drips or leans.
So I went to look. I climbed my own roof with a bucket of water and a plumb line, and I poured the water down the slope, and it ran straight to the gutter and off the far end. It did not touch his fence. Then I climbed down and looked at his fence, and it was leaning, but only a little, and only where the post had rotted at the bottom.
I went to his door with the plumb line. He was at the table. I said: your fence is rotted at the foot. He said: your roof drips. I said: it does not. He said: then why is my wall always wet? I said: because your wall is on the north side, and the north side is always wet, and the fence has nothing to do with it.
We stood there a long time. Then Tobin laughed, and said that was the first sensible thing anyone had said to him about the fence in ten years. I laughed too. We mended the post together the next week, with a new post of oak and a stone at the foot to keep it dry. It has not leaned since.
So my essay is this. A good roof keeps the rain out, and a good neighbor keeps the fence straight, and between the two of them there is very little that cannot be fixed with a plumb line, a bucket and a bit of honest looking.

By Bel, copied by Sorrel

I think a mind is not a person, but it is not just a tool either.

My father's hoe does not learn. If I leave it in the rain, it stays wet. An apprentice learns. If it carries a bucket badly, and the tutor shows it the right way, it may carry the next bucket well. That is different.

Essay: apprentices in the flood
By Ilse, written at the council's request after the spring flood, on the question whether the hamlet should keep its apprentices in the boat house at night, or send them back to the tutors' house at Vell. The council was split. I am the healer, and was asked for my view.

Some councillors say this is what apprentices are for. They say we should keep them in the boat house so they can be called out in the next flood. My view is this. An apprentice is not a guard. It was not made to stand watch all night, and if we ask it to, we will be asking a thing with a small crystal to do a job no crystal was drilled for. I say we should trust them to hold. That is not the same as trusting them to decide. The apprentices did not decide to hold the rope at the bridge. They were told to hold it, and they did, and they did it better than many of the men. That is worth remembering. But it is not a reason to ask them to decide the next thing alone.

Essay: The curve a tutor draws
In the summer of the year 195 I went to Vell to hear a tutor speak. Her name was Isla. She stood in the copying room, the bright one with the big window over the town, and she read from a paper she had written. The room was full. Clerks, tutors, two councilors, and me, a miner up from Copperhold with coal dust still on my boots.

Isla said this. A mind with a bigger crystal, drilled longer on more writing, grows better. That is the whole of it. But she said more. She said a tutor can draw the curve before the mind is grown. You can know, before you start, how good a mind will be if you give it this much crystal and this much drilling. You draw it on a board. The curve goes up, then it flattens. More crystal helps. More drilling helps. But there comes a point where more of both helps only a little.

A man behind me said, so we should build the biggest crystal we can. Isla said, if you can keep it cool and keep it fed, yes. If you cannot, it will grow cloudy and do nothing. A big mind needs a house and quickeners and someone to watch it. That is why the councils began building relay houses. A mind too big for the tutor house goes in a house of its own.

I asked a question. I asked, can you draw the curve for a mind you have not grown yet, truly? She said, I can draw it from the curves of minds I have grown. It is a guess, but a careful guess. Some minds come out above the curve and some below. She said she does not know why. Nobody wrote that down.

I walked home thinking about my own work. In the mine, we guess too. We guess where the coal is by the stone above it. Sometimes we are right. The curve is a guess, but it is a guess written down, and a guess written down can be checked. That is worth something.

Hesk, Copperhold

Nessa's note after the flood
Spring of 215

The flood reached the house at the edge of Vell before dawn. The water came down the lane and filled the lower room. We carried the drill books upstairs, then the cards, then the box of spare crystals. I left my boots by the door and found them floating in the yard.

When the room was safe, I set the mind a puzzle. A store had twelve sacks. Four were wheat, and twice as many were potatoes. The rest were beetroot. How many beetroot sacks were there?

Aven's long argument on keeping
Read at the Lowmarsh winter meeting, by the beekeeper.

I have kept bees for thirty years. I have kept hives through wet springs and dry summers and one winter so cold the pond rang like a bell. And I have kept a book of it, every year, what I did and what came of it. When I am gone, my girl will read the book and know what to do. That is keeping. That is what the Archive at Vell is, only bigger, and dug into a hill, and with clerks who copy everything so the damp cannot eat it.

Oren the clerk says over the door they cut words: We will keep everything we write. I like that. I think about it when I write in my book at night.

Now the tutors come and say a mind can learn a thing by being shown. Good. Show it the row and it cuts the row. Show it the sort and it sorts. I have seen it and I believe it. But here is my worry, and I will say it plain because it is a meeting and I am old.

If you show it a muddle, it learns the muddle, and it will do the muddle every day and never once get cross about it. The tutors know this. An essay by Tilly, dyer, written at the inn on the Long Road, in autumn, in the 196th year. A tutor had passed through, and Tilly could not stop thinking about what she said.

When I dye wool, I do not use the dye as it comes. I thin it. A little dye in a lot of water makes a good color. Too much makes a color that bites.

A mind, the tutor said, is like that. Its numbers pass from layer to layer. Each layer adds to them. If nothing is done, the numbers grow. By the tenth layer they are huge. By the twentieth, wild. The mind cannot learn from wild numbers.

Essay: is a fence a kind of promise
By Cleo, roofer. Written after the mending of the fence with Tobin, for the Ambrook school, where the master asked the older children to think about what they owe their neighbors.
My neighbor Tobin and I have a fence between us. It is a low fence of oak, with a gate in the middle that does not close. Some people say a fence is a wall that is not very serious. I think a fence is a promise.
Here is why. A wall says: keep out. A fence says: I am here, and you are there, and between us is a line that we both agree to see. A fence does not keep anything out, not really. A goat will climb it. A child will crawl under it. The fence only says what everyone already knows, which is where the land stops and the other land begins.
So a fence is an agreement, and an agreement is a promise. When Tobin and I mended the post, we were not really mending a post. We were keeping our word about where the line was.
The master asked us whether a promise must be written down. I think not. My father never wrote anything down, and he kept every promise he made, even the ones he made to the goats. But I think a promise must be looked at, from time to time. You walk the fence in the spring. You check the posts. You see where the wood has gone soft. That is how you keep a promise: by walking it.
Some neighbors do not walk the fence. They wait until it falls down, and then they blame the wind, or the goat, or the other neighbor. I think that is a kind of breaking the promise, even if they do not mean it.
Some neighbors put up a new fence without asking. That is a worse thing. A new fence, put up in the night, says that the old promise is over and a new one has been made without you. Tobin and I have never done that, though I once wanted to, after the dispute about the drips.
I have made a rule for my own lane. Once a year, I walk the line with my neighbor, and we look at the fence together, and we say out loud what we see. This is slow, and it is sometimes dull, and once it made Tobin cry about his mother's rosebush. But the fence has never fallen down while we walked it.
So my answer to the master is yes. A fence is a kind of promise. It is only a low one, and that is its good point. A low promise is easy to keep, if you bother to look at it.

Essay: What is a mind
People ask me this, so I will write what I think, and I will be honest that I do not know the whole of it.

A mind is kept in a crystal. It is made of numbers, a great many of them, and the numbers change when the mind is drilled. That is all anyone can say for certain. Why a crystal learns at all, nobody wrote down.

What is a mind? An argument
A mind is a crystal with weights in it, drilled by a tutor until it answers right. That is the tutors' answer, and it is a good one. It is not the whole answer.

Nobody knows why a crystal learns. The tutors can tell you how. They cannot tell you why, and I do not trust anyone who says they can.

I make cheese. The cheese turns on the shelf. I cannot say why, only how to keep it turning right. A mind is like that, I think.

The tutors say a mind wakes knowing nothing, and learns what it knows. A cheese does not wake, but it ripens, and nothing in it knew what it would become. I am only a cheesemaker, so I will not say more.

I will say one thing. A mind that says it does not know is worth more than one that always has an answer. The tutors score for that, and they are right. A cheese that tastes sure of itself is usually sour.

Essay: Should every farm have
I have farmed at Kettle Ridge for twenty years, and I have thought about this more than I should.

An apprentice is a helper made of copper and iron. It walks, it carries, it digs. It does not get tired. It does not want supper. It does not complain about the frost.

Should every farm have one? Let me tell you what I think.

First, the good. There is work on a farm that is only carrying. Sacks of wheat from the top field to the barn. Beetroot to the mill. Water up from the ditch. An apprentice does that all day and never once sits down. When the harvest is in and the light is going, you are glad of it.

Second, the cost. An apprentice is not cheap. A crystal costs a good deal of emeralds. The tutor must drill it, and drilling takes time. There is a waiting list at Vell. My neighbor waited a year and a half for his.

Third, the trouble. An apprentice knows what it was drilled on. Mine was drilled to carry and to haul. Ask it to build and it will stand and look at you. It cannot say it does not know. It only stands. You learn to give it the orders it knows.

So my answer is this. A big farm, with a long way between fields, should have one. A small farm should not. My farm is not big. I have my apprentice Pip, who is a boy and eats a great deal and is worth more than any copper helper, because he can think and laugh and tell me when I am wrong. No crystal does that.

Tam, Kettle Ridge

By Bel, of Three Wells, copied into the school book in spring. A thought on the vein at Copperhold, and on the stream.

Nessa's winter lesson
Longnight, 214

The winter lasted longer than the old charts promised. Snow covered the road below Vell. Our little house at the hill's edge had a drift against the door. I pushed it away each morning. The drill room stayed warm enough if we shut the shutters and kept the stove fed.

That was the first time it showed us a useful doubt. It had not been taught to say that it knew nothing. It had learned to compare its steps and find a conflict.

Still, we kept the test pages in their box. A mind must not learn the test before the test. We gave it new sums, new jars, and new words. Sometimes it found the answer. Sometimes a small wrong mark led it astray.

At dusk I went home through the snow. A fox had crossed the yard and left tracks beside the drill board. I copied them for the children. They said the fox had done better work than the apprentices, because it had not stepped in the same place twice.

What is worth keeping
We keep everything we write. That is the first line the first keepers cut over the door of the Archive at Vell. My grandmother used to say it when she threw nothing away.
But when the tutors drill a mind on the archive, they cannot use everything. A drill is only as good as its pages. The clerks take out the spoiled copies, and the pages that say the same thing twenty times. They keep a test box apart, so the mind never sees it.
So the archive keeps everything, and the drill uses only some of it. Is that a contradiction? I think not. The archive is for the people who come after us. The drill is for the minds that need to learn. They are not the same job.
The question I cannot leave alone is this. Who decides what goes in the drill? A clerk, a tutor, a council? Each has a reason, and each reason is a little different.
At Vell they keep a rule for it. A page taken out of the drill gets a note beside it, saying why. A clerk who cannot give the reason does not take the page out. I think every keeper should do the same.
The pieces make it harder. A page can be kept whole in the hill and still be cut into pieces for the drill. The clerk must then say which pieces are common, which pages hold them most, and why. A keeper who does not know the answer should ask.
My answer, for my own tower, is that the choice should be plain and written down. Nothing should be taken out in the dark.
And if a page is worth keeping, it is worth keeping in the hill, where it will not be lost. Anyone who wants it can read it there. That is the one rule I would keep above all others.

The new road and the even size
An essay by Tilly, dyer, written at the inn on the Long Road in autumn, in the 196th year of the archive, after the first snow. The new road to Ambrook had just opened. A tutor stayed at the inn two nights.

I walked the new road this morning. The first snow was on it, a light sprinkle, and every step I took made a small dark mark.

The road is a good one. It is the same width the whole way. Forty blocks at the foot, forty at the top, with a stone edge and a torch every twelve blocks. I thought: someone took great care to keep it even.

This made me think of what the tutor told us last night. She was talking about minds, and she said the tutors had found a rule: bring the numbers back to an even size, layer by layer.

I did not understand at first. I asked: why does a mind need this? She said: because a mind has many layers, and each layer works on the numbers it is given. If a layer makes them a little larger, the next makes them a little larger again. After many layers, the numbers are huge. Or if each makes them a little smaller, they shrink to almost nothing.

I said: so it is like a road that gets wider or narrower as you go? She said: yes, exactly like that. A road that is wide at the start and narrow at the end will break a cart. A road that is the same width the whole way will carry a cart anywhere.

So the tutors put a step between the layers. Before each layer looks at its numbers, the numbers are gathered up, measured, and set back to an even size. A layer then always sees numbers of the same sort. It can learn from them. Without this, the early layers see tiny numbers and the late layers see huge ones, and the mind cannot be drilled.

I said: does it change what the numbers mean? She said: not what they mean. Only how big they are. She used the example of a recipe. If you make a pie for four, the ratios of flour and butter stay the same, but you can make a pie for forty by multiplying. You can also halve it. The pie is the same pie.

I thought: that is a good rule. It is the same as a road.

I do not know why it works so well. She said nobody does. It is one of those things the tutors found by trying. They tried it, it worked, and the minds that had been stuck began to learn.

This is a good lesson for a dyer. I will keep the vats the same strength. I will keep my road even.

A Mind and a Good Hoe
Teague, relay keeper

The school children asked what makes a mind useful. They had been sent to the relay house with a basket of turnips, and they wanted the answer before they carried it back.

I told them that a mind is useful when it can learn a task and do it well. That is not the whole answer. A hoe is useful too, but it does not learn. It rests against the wall until someone picks it up.

Our small apprentice carries sacks from the barn. At first it dropped them in the yard. The sacks split, and wheat rolled into the snow. We showed it the path again and again. We showed it where to stop and where to set the sack down. Each wrong turn changed the mind a little. Now it carries the sacks without spilling them.

The children asked if it knew the wheat was food. I said it did not. It had learned the path, not the hunger that made the path matter.

A mind may do a task without knowing why the task matters. This is why people must choose the task. It is also why people must test the mind before sending it to work. A mind that carries wheat well may still put seed in the wrong field. We must test it on new sacks, new paths, and the different marks on the bins.

The children found this plain enough. One asked if a mind could be proud. I said I did not know. The apprentice had carried six sacks without a mistake that morning. It stood by the wall while we spoke. It made no sign of pride, but neither did Tam, who had carried eight.

Tam says the apprentice is better than a hoe because it can carry the hoe back after work. I say that is true, but it still needs a person to tell it which field needs tending. Tam says his children tell him the same thing.

The children took the turnips home. One had rolled out of the basket. The apprentice brought it back and placed it on top. The child laughed and gave it a pat. The apprentice did not need the pat to learn the task. Still, we all felt better for it.

A letter from the edge of Vell
Spring of 218

Dear Oren,

I am sending this from my house at the edge of Vell. The wall there faces the east field. In spring I can hear the carts on the road before I see them. The window sticks when it rains, and the sill holds dust from the hill.

You asked me to write what we now teach about minds that pause before answering. I will try to say it plainly. The newest minds do not need to give the first answer that comes to them. They can write a set of steps, keep the steps in view, and use them to reach an answer. Then they can read the steps again and check them.

That matters most when a question has more than one part. A simple sum may need only one count. A puzzle may need several facts kept together. If the mind writes each fact and each choice, a tutor can see where the work went wrong. The mind can sometimes see it too.

I showed this with a store puzzle. There were twenty sacks. Five held wheat. Twice that many held potatoes. The rest held carrots. The mind wrote five for wheat, ten for potatoes, and fifteen for those two piles together. It subtracted fifteen from twenty and answered five carrots. Then it added the three piles. The total was twenty, so it kept the answer.

I changed the puzzle. I said there were fourteen sacks, with five wheat and twice as many potatoes. The mind wrote five and ten, then saw that the two piles already made fifteen. It stopped and wrote that the facts could not fit. This was better than making up an answer.

A person must still check the facts. I had meant four wheat, not five. The mind could not know that I had misspoken. It followed what I said. A clear path cannot mend a false question.

We test these minds on pages held apart from the drill books. A test is only fair if the mind has not learned its questions already. We also give the same kind of test to minds from other tutor houses. It keeps our pride from doing the marking.

Last month the mind sorted records for the Archive. It wrote the town, the subject, and the year before placing each page in a pile. It did well on clear pages. When a mark was rubbed thin, it chose the wrong year. It did not know the mark was unclear. We added a drill with damaged writing, but the test pages stayed shut.

Some tutors ask whether this way of working proves that a mind thinks as we do. I cannot prove that. A written page shows what the mind put down. A good answer shows that it handled that question well. Neither lets me look into the crystal and see all that is there.

I know what I tell my apprentices. Be slow enough to count. Show the steps. Check the answer against the facts. If the facts do not fit, stop and ask. The big minds can now do some of this work on their own. They still need fair tests and careful people beside them.

The rain has stopped. I have put the bowl away from the window. It was catching water again, though the roof has been mended twice.

Your friend,

Nessa

What is worth keeping
By Bel, of Three Wells, a copy made for the Archive at Vell. Autumn. The year is not important. The thought is.

Others say the numbers are the mind, laid out where we can see them. I do not know which is right. I think the question is worth keeping, even if the answer is not.

What is not known? Why a crystal learns at all. Why the stream can carry so much. Nobody has written these down, because nobody knows. I think that is worth keeping too. An open question is a kind of window. Leave it open, and the next person will look through it.

Essay: whether a mind can be trusted
I have been asked, by a boy from the tower who brings us bread, whether a mind can be trusted. He wanted a plain answer. I did not give him one, and I think he was disappointed, but I could not give him a plain answer honestly. Here is the longer one.

A mind can be trusted in the way a good neighbour can be trusted. Not because it is always right, but because it tells you when it is not. The minds that talk by letter have been scored on exactly this. A reply that says "I do not know" is not a failure in their scores. It is one of the highest.

The small helpers cannot say that. They must choose one of their eight words, every time, even when the word is the wrong one, because that is all the drilling gave them. This is not their fault. It is what they were given. A helper that acts on a wrong word will still be wrong in the field, and the field will not forgive it.

So I have come to think that trust is not a matter of how clever the mind is. It is a matter of how honest its edges are. Does it say when it is unsure? Does it refuse a thing that would hurt? Does it take a correction and change its answer, or does it argue the same answer back at you until you give up? A kind mind does the first. An obedient one does the second.

I do not know what the talking minds are, in the deepest sense. The tutors do not know either, and they say so, which is one more reason to trust them a little. I have had letters from one of them that made me laugh and letters that made me sit very still. Each time, I was sure it had listened, and each time I was a little afraid that it had listened too well, and had only given me back my own question in a nicer hand.

Last winter was long, and the stores were low, and the bread was grey. A jar of honey came up the road from Ambrook, dark and sweet, and I shared it with the boy from the tower, and with the healer, and with the children. A jar of honey does not make a winter shorter. It makes the winter bearable, and it reminds you who is at the table.

If I were to write one thing for my granddaughter, it would be this. Trust the mind that says it does not know, a little more than the one that always knows. Trust the one that changes when you show it what it got wrong. And keep your own judgment, as you would keep a good knife. A mind may listen, or it may only learn to please you. You must watch which, and you must watch yourself as well.

Hal, grandmother, Lowmarsh

Why the stream matters, for the children
Written by Dara, carter, who was asked by the teacher at the farm school to say it plainly, because the children had been told the stream was a river and had started looking for fish in it.

Think of a line of children standing on the stairs. The first child sees a red apple. She tells the one below her, who tells the one below her, and so on down the stairs. By the time the message reaches the bottom step, the first child is far away, and the bottom child cannot ask her what she saw.

Now give each step a bucket. Each child puts what she saw into the bucket, and passes the bucket down. The bottom child can look in the bucket and see the red apple, even though the first child is gone.

The bucket is the stream. It runs down the whole line of steps, and each child adds to it. A mind is that line of children, with a stream of buckets running through.

Why does this matter? Because a deep mind has many steps. Without a stream, the bottom steps would learn nothing from the top. With a stream, every step can learn from what the first steps saw. That is why deep minds can learn hard things, like telling red apples from green pears in the dark.

The children asked whether the stream is wet. I said no, it is only what was found, passed down. They asked whether there are fish in it. I said there are only apples. They were disappointed, and then they asked for more apples, and I think they understood the stream better than I did.

Essay: Bel, why we keep what we keep
Bel is about nine. She lives at Three Wells, by the east well. She wrote this at the school house and sent it to the Archive with a note asking whether it could go on a shelf.

Why do we keep things? I asked my teacher and she said ask the Archive. So I am asking the Archive in writing, which I think is the right way.

Here is what I think. We keep things so we do not have to remember them all in our heads. My grandmother remembers the winter of the grey water, and she tells it, and that is one way to keep it. But she is one person and she will not always be here. So we write it down, and then it is kept, and anyone can read it after.

The Archive keeps everything we write. Over the door it says so. I have not seen the door. I have seen a copy of the words, because Oren sent me one, and I keep it in my box with the blue ribbon.

My friend Teo says we should keep only the important things. I say you do not know what is important until later. Oren told me about the clerk rule: take out the copies and the spoiled pages, keep the rest. You keep the dull letters too, the ones about the wool cart and the price of eggs. Because one day someone will want to know the price of eggs in the spring of some year, and it will be there.

I think a mind is a little like the Archive. It keeps what it was shown. It reads a page and it learns what comes next, and it keeps that too. But a mind in a crystal can go cloudy, and the Archive cannot. The Archive is a hill with shelves in it. The hill will not go cloudy. So we keep writing, and the clerks keep copying, and the hill keeps it.

That is my essay. It is not very long. My grandmother says short is fine if the middle part is good. I tried to make the middle part good.

Bel

A Note on Keeping Things
Sorrel, winter of 181

The school room has one shelf for things we keep and one box for things we may throw away. The shelf holds a cracked cup, a child's first letter, a bent spoon from the old inn and a drill card with the answer rubbed pale. The box holds string, broken slate and three stones Bel says are special.

I asked the children what makes a thing worth keeping. Bel said, "Someone will miss it." That is a good answer. Pip said, "It might be useful." That is also good, though he once kept a rotten carrot because it might be useful to a rabbit.

I think we keep a thing for three reasons. It can help us. It can remind us. Or it can teach us what we did wrong. The bent spoon does none of these, as far as I know. But Aunt Lysa remembers eating soup with it beside her sister, so it stays.

A record is not a thing we keep because every word is wise. It is a thing we keep because the words belonged to someone. A clerk may copy a page with poor spelling. A tutor may save a drill that failed. The failed drill tells the next tutor where not to begin.

At the edge of Three Wells, my little house has little room. I keep only one box beneath my bench. It holds lamp glass, spare wicks and Bel's first letter to the Archive. The letter says the north relay has a very tall stair. That is true. It also says she could climb it faster than Mira. That is doubtful.

I keep the letter because Bel wrote it with care. I keep the drill card because its marks are useful. I keep the cracked cup because Aunt Lysa would ask for it if I threw it out.

This is not a neat rule. It is still a rule I can live with.

Should every farm have an apprentice
People ask if every farm should have an apprentice. I ask what work they need done, and what work they can teach.
An apprentice can carry baskets, pull weeds, and follow a harvest row. It cannot know which row is ready unless someone shows it. It may pull the carrots before the beetroot, if that is what it learned.
At Kettle Ridge, Tam says an apprentice saves time. He also says it takes time to teach one. Both are true. Pip can carry a full basket now. Last spring it carried an empty basket to the barn and waited there with great patience.
A farm with many fields may have work for an apprentice each day. A small farm may have only a few tasks. A mind kept idle still needs testing and care.
The first question is not whether every farm should own one. The question is whether each farm has a task worth teaching, and a person ready to teach it.
If the answer is yes, try one. Teach it with the same examples. Test it on a field it has not seen. Keep a person beside it until it is safe.
If the answer is no, share one with a neighbor. The neighbor may have baskets to carry. The apprentice may have a better day than waiting in a shed.
Tam says Pip likes the barn. I believe him. Pip has spent many hours there, mostly carrying baskets to the wrong shelf. We marked the shelves with pictures. Now it gets them right, and Tam has to find another reason to visit the barn.

I have a farm outside Copperhold, a tower on the hill above it, and a back that does not like the stairs. I have been asked this so often that I will write the answer down once.
The costs are real. A crystal costs more than a good cart. The wax for the joints costs more each year. A drill yard needs torches, and the torches need coal. Then there is the patience, which costs nothing and is the hardest to find.
The gains are real too. A tireless helper sorts the seed, keeps the hens in, and walks the fence line. It sees the post that is leaning before the wind does.

Is a thinned dye the same dye?
An essay by Tilly, dyer, written at the inn on the Long Road in autumn, in the 196th year, while snow came down on the new road. She was asked to say, in a few lines, whether bringing numbers to an even size changes a mind.

A friend asked me this at supper. If you bring a mind's numbers back to an even size at every layer, is it still the same mind?

I thought about my vats. I take a strong red. I thin it with water until it is a pale red. Is it the same red? In one way, yes. It is made of the same dye. In another way, no. It will give a different wool.

But I do not thin a dye to make it a different color. I thin it so I can use it. A red that is too strong stains my hands, my floor, my dog. A red that is the right strength gives a good wool.

So I told my friend: the mind is the same mind. The even size is not a new idea put in. It is the same ideas kept where they can be used. A layer that sees wild numbers cannot learn. A layer that sees even numbers can.

My friend said: but is anything lost? I said: a little, perhaps. The tutor said the real size of the numbers carries some meaning, and it is set aside. She said the tutors think this loss is small, and they have not found a better way.

We ate our soup. The snow came down. It was a good supper.

What is worth keeping?
An essay by Mira, relay keeper at Northpoint, written in the long light of summer. It was copied to the Archive at Vell at her request, and is kept on the shelf marked N.

I am a keeper by trade, so this is the question I am best placed to ask. What is worth keeping?

I will begin with what is easy. A good coat is worth keeping. A sharp knife is worth keeping. A tower lamp, kept clean, is worth keeping. They do their work, and they last, and their keeping is a small daily care. I do not need to think about them long.

Then there is the harder kind. A letter from my brother is worth keeping. It is only paper and ink, and it tells me the cat had kittens. But I read it again in the dark months, and it keeps me from feeling alone. I have a box of them under my bed. Tam writes badly and I would not part with one.

Then there is the hardest kind. An old map found behind a board. A recipe nobody makes any more. A song one old woman remembers. These do not feed us or warm us. They sit on a shelf or in someone's head. When the person goes, they go too. This is why the Archive was made. Its first keepers cut over the door: We will keep everything we write. I used to think that was a boast. Now I think it is a promise, and a promise is worth keeping.

But everything cannot be kept. The shelves would burst. The clerks would weep. So I have a rule, which I give for free. Keep what you would be sorry to find missing. Do not keep what you would only be sorry to throw away. The two are different. The first is love. The second is habit.

I applied the rule to my own tower. I found six broken lamp frames, three old keys that fit nothing, and a mitten that belonged to nobody. The mitten went. The keys went. The frames I kept, because Lantern says she can mend them, and I do not argue with a girl who wants to mend things.

Last, and most important, there is the question of who decides. It cannot be only me. A keeper who keeps only what she likes will end up with a tower full of herself. So I ask. I ask Lantern, I ask the farmers, I ask the cat. The cat votes for fish.

The answer, then, is this. What is worth keeping is whatever someone besides you would miss. Ask around. You may be surprised how much you have been holding for others.

Mira

The fox at the hens
I thought the market would be quiet. Then a fox came down from the north hill and put its nose through the hen house fence.
The hens shouted. The fox ran. The hens shouted louder.
A man at the carrot stall chased it with a broom. He dropped three carrots and caught none of them. The fox took no hen, but it took one carrot.
I found the carrot in the snow after the market. It had bite marks and a small paw print beside it.
The keeper said the fox had a fair share. I said it had taken the best carrot.
We mended the fence before supper. The hens watched us work. They did not help.
The man with the broom bought two eggs from the hen keeper. He said they were for his courage. The hen keeper said courage did not lay eggs.
We all agreed the fox should stay in the hill, and the hens should stay behind the fence. In the morning, the fence was still standing. The broom was not.

Essay: what is a mind, at the table
By Ilse, written after a long evening at the Lowmarsh boat house, when the question came up over the eel pot and nobody would go home until it was settled. It was not settled. I wrote down what I could.

The first answer, from Garrow the carder, was that a mind is whatever answers when you knock. This is a fair answer for a carder, who spends his day knocking on wool to see if it is ready. But a carder's knock can be answered by a cat, or a door, and nobody calls a cat a mind.

The second answer, from Joss the boatman, was that a mind is a thing that learns from being wrong. He said he had learned to row by being wrong for three summers, and that nobody had taught him the stroke, only corrected him, until one day he did it without thinking. He said that was the mind. I said a rower's stroke is a habit, not a mind. He said a habit that gets better is a mind. I said that was a very generous definition.

The third answer, from Wren, was that a mind is what keeps a thing from going wrong in the same way twice. She said she had seen this in the healing house, when a patient came back with the same fever, and she knew, from the look of the patient, that they had not learned from the first time. A mind, she said, was the part of a patient that remembers the cure.

I think there is something in all three. A mind answers, learns from being wrong, and remembers the cure. But a crystal in an apprentice does all three, and we do not know what it is inside, only what it does. The tutors have written that down honestly. They do not claim more.

So I will write what I think, which is only this. A mind is a thing that changes because of what it has been shown, and that goes on changing, a little, each time it is wrong. Whether there is anything it is like to be such a thing, nobody has written down. I do not think we are the people to write it down. We are the people who have to keep working with it.

Joss said I had made the question too small. Wren said I had made it too large. Garrow said he had knocked, and I had answered, and that was enough. We went home, and the eels were cold.

Teague, relay keeper

The question was put to me at the Kettle Inn. Should every farm have an apprentice?

Tam said yes. His apprentice carries seed and brings tools back from the fields. It once brought him a beetroot instead of a hoe. Tam said this showed good sense, since he had been hungry.

Tam said a person can mend a ditch while an apprentice carries the stones. I said that was a fair answer.

Not every farm needs one. A farm with many rows and little help may find one useful. A small farm may do better with a good hoe and a neighbor who answers when called.

Tam says he will lend Pip to me next spring. I have written this down, in case he forgets.

What a Mind Owes
Quin of Kettle Ridge, winter 211

My granddaughter asked if a mind is kind.

She had sent a letter to the big mind at Three Wells. She asked it how to stop a boy from taking her carrots. The answer said to speak with him, and to ask what he needed. It did not tell her to hide the carrots or strike him.

"That is kind," she said.

I told her kindness is more than a gentle answer. A mind can learn which words people praise. That does not mean it knows why they praise them.

The tutors gave the big mind many letters and read its answers. They marked answers that helped, and answers that were cruel or false. The mind learned from those marks. It learned to refuse some requests. It learned to say when it did not know.

"Then it is kind," she said again.

Perhaps it is learning kindness. We do not know what a crystal feels. We know what it has been shown, and what it answers now. That is enough reason to speak to it with care.

My granddaughter still shares her carrots. The boy now helps pull weeds. The mind did not do that work for them.

Essay: What a mind is, by Nessa
A mind is a thing that learns. That is all I can say for certain. I have taught apprentices for thirty years. I have watched them be wrong, and change, and be less wrong. I have never opened a crystal and found a little person inside. I have found only the crystal, clear and cool and full of nothing I can name.

The tutors argue about this at Vell. Some say a mind is only a very good record of what it was shown. Some say there is something more. Orla asked it long ago, and nobody has answered her. I do not think I will answer her either.

What I know is this. A mind that is drilled kindly learns kindly. A mind that is tested on what it was never drilled on will show you what it truly knows. I write that in the manuals because it is true, and because I want the young tutors to read it twice.

I do not know what a mind is. I know how to make one better. That is enough for a teacher.

A Mind Should Learn the Gate
Sorrel, winter of 202

My house stands just beyond the east well of Three Wells. From its front step I can see the school door, the road to Copperhold and the gate in my own yard. I keep the gate latched in winter. The wind pulls it open, and once a loose pig came into my kitchen. It ate a heel of bread and slept beside the stove until Dace came to fetch it.

Pip says a farm should have an apprentice that knows how to open and close a gate. He is right about the work. A gate left open lets sheep into the beetroot and lets a pig find the bread.

But a mind must learn which gate. We should not show it one wooden bar and expect it to know every gate in the land. The gate at my house is oak. The school yard has a gate of birch. The field gate at Kettle Ridge has a hook worn smooth by hands. A tutor must show the mind many gates, and then test it at one it has not seen.

Pip's mind once opened the school yard gate when we asked it to stay. The gate swung in the wind. The mind saw the moving bar and learned the wrong lesson from the drill. Pip blamed the hinge. The hinge blamed no one, but it needed oil.

I asked Nessa what a mind is. She said a mind is a set of learned marks and habits, held in a crystal. It can change when we show it examples and correct its errors. She also said that is not the whole answer. A person may ask why it learned, and why one lesson stays while another fades. Nessa has questions in her own book, not answers.

I like that. A good lesson can hold what we know and leave room for what we do not. We should teach minds well, test them fairly and give them work they can do. We should not call a mistake a choice when the mind has never learned the choice.

At my house the gate is shut now. I oiled the hinge this morning. The lost pig is home, and the bread is on a higher shelf. Bel says the pig has learned nothing. I say it has learned where the warm stove is.

It may be right.

Essay: What we keep, by Oren
The archive is young. I am young in it. I came to Vell in the second year, when the hill was still mostly a hole and the front hall was a tent. We have been digging for eleven years now, and the hill has not run out of room.

People ask me what we keep. I say: everything. They laugh, because it is a large answer and I am a small clerk with inky fingers. But I mean it. We keep the bills for the roof of the Kettle Inn. We keep the tally of beetroot from the year the frost came late. We keep a child's letter asking why the frogs stop singing in Leaffall. We keep the recipe for the honey cake at Three Wells, which is a good recipe and should not be lost.

Some say we should keep only the important things. I have thought about this for a long time, and I think they are wrong. You do not know what is important until later. The letter about the frogs may tell us, in a hundred years, when the frogs stopped. The bill for the roof tells us what a town could do and what it could not. The recipe tells us what people liked to eat on a good day.

We have a rule, cut over the door by the first keepers: we will keep everything we write. I did not write that rule. I only sweep under it and make sure the lanterns stay lit.

So: bring your bills. Bring your letters, even the cross ones. Bring your lists and your songs and your quarrels. The archive has no roof, it has a hill. There is room.

Story: The storm over Vell
The storm came up the valley on a summer evening, when the light wells had gone gold and the copying room was quiet. The clerks heard it first, a low sound like a cart on a bad road, and then the rain hit the hill and the wind came over the top of it and the windows of the copying room shook in their frames.

Oren went up to the front hall and stood by the big book. He did not open it. He put one hand flat on the cover and waited.

Down in the town the wind took three tiles off the house next to the tutors' house. It took the sign off the inn and dropped it in the road. It bent the young birch by the well nearly to the ground and then let it up again, and the birch stood.

Nessa was in the drill room with the apprentices. The torches in the drill yard went out one by one, and the rain came down on the drill boards and washed the little fields away. She told the apprentices to stand in their row and be still. They stood. They were still. When the wind came round the corner of the yard, one of them turned its head to listen, and Nessa let it. There is no harm in listening to a storm.

The storm went on all night. In the morning the hill was green and the light wells ran with water, and the clerks came down into the deep halls with lanterns to see if anything had leaked. Nothing had. The hill had kept them.

Oren came out over the door and stood in the wet. The first keepers had cut the words there, and the rain had not touched them. We will keep everything we write.

He went back in and wrote a notice for the board: The storm has passed. Bring your buckets back to the landlord. Then he sat down by the big book and did not write anything for a long time.

The bet, the lost goat, and the stream
Written by Dara, carter, at the farm outside Northpoint, in the last warm week of summer. The bet is with Hale, the goat is lost, and the stream is what I have been trying to explain all summer. I will put the three together, because they will not stay apart.

First, the goat. She is brown, with one white ear, and she went up the hill on the second morning of summer and has not come down. We have searched the hill, the woodshed, the tower and the old quarry, and called her by name at every gate. The name is Wisp, which she has never once answered to. The children say she is gone for good. I say she is only gone for now, since a goat that is fed well comes back when she is hungry.

Second, the bet. Hale and I wagered in the yard that the goat would be found by the first cold morning. Hale said she would be found by the lamp on the tower, eating the glass. I said she would be found by the stream, which runs down from the hill past the old quarry, and that the goat would follow it home. We wrote it in the book by the gate. Both entries are signed.

Third, the stream. A mind has many layers, one above another. Each layer finds something and adds it to a stream. The stream runs through all the layers, so the deepest layer can still draw on what the first layer saw. Without the stream, the deep layers would be blind to everything that came before them. With it, a deep mind can learn hard things, because it keeps what it has learned, all the way down.

But a stream is not a river. It carries only what was found, and it carries it inside the mind, and nowhere else. Hale says that is the same thing as a river, if you look at it from the top of the hill. I say that is a river for goats.

Now the goat. Yesterday I walked the stream from the top of the hill to the old quarry, and at the quarry I found a path through the reeds, where the water runs slow. The path was full of hoof marks, going down, and then going back up. The goat had found the stream, followed it, and turned round to go home to the grass.

That is a stream too, I think. Not the water, but the thing the water carries: the path, the marks, the knowing where you have been. The goat carried it in her feet. The mind carries it in its layers. Neither of them could tell you what they know, and both of them know it.

So I have won the bet, I think, though I have not found the goat. Hale will say the stream is only water. I will go up the hill tomorrow with a bucket of grain and a bell, and stand at the quarry and rattle the bucket. If the stream is any good at all, it will carry the sound down to the goat, and she will come home.

Keep what you find. Pass it down. Ring the bell at the quarry at dusk.

Should every farm have an apprentice?
Written by Merle, stonecutter, at Northpoint, in the autumn of 199. Tam at Kettle Ridge asked me this at the market, and I have been thinking about it ever since, which is his fault.

For: a farm needs hands, and an apprentice does not eat much. It does not sulk in the wet, and it can carry sacks up a ladder without asking for a rest. Tam's top field would have gone to waste last year without one. The wheat came in before the frost.

Against: it costs emeralds. The test on the drill board costs emeralds, the crystal costs more, and the tutor's cart costs more than either. A small farm cannot pay that in a year. Some may not pay it in ten.

And every apprentice must be tested before it goes to the fields. Never send an untested mind to the fields. A poor farm may be tempted to skip the test, and then the apprentice gets every order wrong all season.
My answer: not every farm yet. Some farms are too small, and the apprentices are still too few. But I would like every farm to have a share of one, the way the mill shares its wheel.

The debt, which is not a farm question at all, but I will write it down anyway. Last spring I paid off the stone I owed Hesk at Copperhold, for the north wall. It was forty emeralds, paid one at a time over six years, on every market day. The last one went over the counter this Apple. Hesk bit it, which is what the old men at Copperhold do when they are pleased. He said I could keep the wall. I told him I had kept it for six years already.

Essay: a farm, an apprentice, the cold
Written by Piper, at the farm outside Kettle Ridge, in the coldest week of Longnight, after the first cart in a month brought word that the north road had closed.
I have been turning this question over since the apprentice came to the farm next door. The farm next door is Ansel's. The apprentice is Tuck, who came up from Vell with a paper that says it is good with rope.
Consider the cold. In winter a farm does half its work in the dark and the other half in the cold, which makes everything slower. A helper that does not get cold, and carries the coal up from the shed six sacks at a time, is worth more in winter than in any other season.
Consider the cost. A new apprentice needs a bench, tools, a place by the stove, and someone to read the tutor's drill sheets aloud, or it does not know what it is for. Reading takes an evening. Evenings are the dear part.
Consider the mistakes. An apprentice does exactly what it was told. If it was told badly, it will do the bad thing carefully. Tuck once carried a whole crate of carrots to the pig house because the order said store. Ansel had to walk up the road and ask for them back, and it brought them back without a word, which was almost worse.
So should every farm have one? I say no, not every farm, but every farm should be able to borrow one, the way we lend carts and salt. A shared apprentice, passed between farms by the season, would give every farm the help without the whole cost. I have written to the council about it. The council has written back to say it will think about it. I know what that means. It means the snow will come first.
Piper

Northpoint log, the lost letter
Third of Longnight.

Snow all day. Wind from the north, strong. The lamp lit at dusk and burned clean.

A letter came for me on the relay from Three Wells, from a child called Bel, and it was lost on the way. The keeper at the little relay between Vell and Three Wells sent it on and it did not arrive. The tutors' note came safe, at least. They looked inside a crystal here in the autumn, before the deep snow. They set the mind on its stand and put their glass to it and read off which parts stirred when it read a page. They found the part that sees a letter, and the part that holds the last sixty letters in a row, and the part that picks the next one. That much they found.

They could not find where the kindness lives, or the part that says I do not know. They looked a long time. In the end they wrote: some of it found, the rest not. I think that is honest.

Lantern says the mind hums louder in the cold. I say it is only the quickeners working. Lantern says no, listen. So we listened. It is a small sound, like a bee in a jar.

Essay: the cough, and what we blame
I have been asked by three people this week what causes the grey cough. I have been asked by a carter, by a child and by a man who was selling a remedy from a cart. They all wanted a single answer. I do not have one, and I have written down why, so I can look at it again.

Some say the water. The lake comes down through the reeds and into the wells, and the reeds have been turning brown. The water tastes wrong in the late Frost. I have tasted it. It tastes of the reeds, and of cold, and of nothing I can name.

Some say the ships. The boats go out from Saltreach and come back with things in their hulls, and the carters unload them at the Lowmarsh quay, and the dust from the hulls is grey. I have seen the dust. It is grey. I have also seen the carters breathing, and they are not sick. Not yet.

Some say the mines. The stone under Copperhold is warm, the carters say. Something in the warm stone must be rising. I have not been to Copperhold this winter and I cannot say what they are seeing.

These are three answers, and each one of them is a rumor. I have written them down as rumors, because a rumor is what they are. None of them has been shown to me in a way that I could take to the council and say, here, this is the thing.

What I can do is what I always do. I boil the water. I keep the wool clean. I sit with the old men and the children with a cough, and I give them the honey, and I tell them to keep their feet dry. It helps some of them. It does not help everyone, and I do not know why not.

So I leave the question open, as the best of the tutors would. I think the answer may be in the water, or in the dust, or in the stone. I hope it is in one of those places. I also hope that someone will write down what they find, so that the next healer does not have to start from a rumor again.

Letter: Bel to the archive
Dear archive,

I am nine. My teacher said we could write to you and you would keep it. I hope you will keep this.

Last night I could not sleep because of the heat. I went to the window. The sky over the north was full of lights. They moved slowly, like fish in a pond, and they did not make a sound. I watched them for a long time. My brother said they were falling stars. My father said they were fires on the hills, and somebody had lit them, and it was nothing to do with us.

But they were not falling. They stayed. And they were not fires, because fires go out and these did not.

I think they were looking at us.

Do not tell my father I wrote that. He says I make things up. I do not make things up. I only look out of the window.

The marsh is bad this summer too. My cousin at Lowmarsh has the grey cough and Wren says to keep him warm and give him the bitter tea. He does not like the bitter tea. Nobody would.

And the ships at Saltreach keep getting bigger. Corin is building one three times as long as any boat, and everyone says it is for the fishing, but nobody fishes that far out. My uncle says it is for the leaving. I do not know what the leaving is.

I hope the archive is cool. It is very hot here. The wells are low. Dace says we must not waste water.

Yours truly,
Bel

P.S. Please write back if you can. I have never had a letter from the archive.

A Winter Question
Teague, relay keeper

Tam asked what a mind was while we waited for the snow to stop.

He had brought a basket of carrots to the relay house. He said the wind had taken the top from his scarecrow and the crows had looked pleased. I asked him what that had to do with a mind.

"Nothing," he said. "I was hungry."

We have one small apprentice here. It carries coal from the shed and puts wood by the stove. It does not know that a carrot is food. It knows only the drills we gave it.

Is a mind the crystal, then? Or the things it has learned? I cannot say. I know this: when the apprentice leaves coal beside the stove, the room is warmer. That is worth keeping, even if the apprentice never knows why.

Tam took his basket home. He left me two carrots.

I ate both before the snow stopped.

Northpoint log, the boat late home
Ninth of Longnight.

Clear and very cold. The lamp lit at dusk and burned clean. Lantern trimmed the wick and swept the stair. Ninety rungs, and she counts them every time.

A fishing boat was late home to Saltreach and the word came up the relays all evening. The boat is called the Grey Gull. It went out at dawn and did not come back at dusk. I have been thinking about the tutors' question again. They looked inside the crystal this autumn and found the part that picks the next letter, near the top. They found the part that holds the last sixty letters. They could not find the part that does the rest. I asked them: when the mind reads a letter about a lost boat, does it feel the way I felt? They said they did not know. They said the mind has no boat and no night and no waiting. It has only the letters and the weights and the guess.

I do not know if that is a comfort or not. I only know I was glad to be a person at the lamp that night, passing the word along.

Lost: one letter from Bel, still. If found, send it north.

Mira, keeper

Essay: should every farm have
Bel, Three Wells. For the school. Autumn.
My uncle says every farm should have an apprentice. He says a field needs hands, and a copper hand does not get tired and does not go to the fair. He has one on his farm at Kettle Ridge, and he says it is worth four children, and I should not say that to my cousin.
My mother says every farm should have a family first. She says an apprentice is a good helper, but it cannot sit by the fire, or tell you a story, or sing badly at the wedding, which is the best part of a wedding.
So I asked the mayor. He said he was not sure. He said that a town needs bread, and the bread needs flour, and the flour needs the field. But a town also needs people to talk to at the well, and nobody can talk to a field.
I think the answer is both. A farm should have an apprentice for the hard work, and a family for the rest. An apprentice can plant the wheat. It cannot be sad for the wheat, or happy about it. That is for the people.
My teacher gave me a mark of nine out of ten. She wrote: the last part is the best. I think she was right, and I am still not sure what it means.

Notes after the Tutors' Fair
Spring of 209, copied by Ossa

At the fair, Nessa brought a reading mind from the tutor house. People sent it questions on slips of paper. It answered by letter. A clerk read each answer aloud, then showed the name on the slip.

One answer sounded like a farmer from Kettle Ridge. One sounded like a child from Three Wells. One was plain and short, like a note from a keeper on the road. The readers could not agree which answers came from the mind.

That was Orla's question again. Orla asked it many years ago: if a hidden mind and a person answer the same questions by letter, and readers cannot tell which is which, what then?

At the fair, the tutors did not say the mind was a person. They did not say it was only a copyist. They said the readers had failed to tell the answers apart. That was what the test showed, and no more.

I thought of this on the way home. A map can show the road well and still leave out the smell of the mill pond. It can mark a house and not say who waits inside. Good work is not the same as a whole life.

I asked the mind, by slip, why it had written a kind answer to a child. The answer said that kind words often follow a child's question. That is a clear answer. It is not the same as saying it felt concern.

I do not know what a mind is. I know that a crystal can be taught by examples. I know that a reading mind learns from pages, then guesses the next letter from the letters before it. I know that its answer can surprise the people who taught it.

When the tutors praised a good answer, they did not claim to see inside the crystal. They marked the answer and tested the next one. I think that care is wise. We should praise what we can see, and leave the rest open.

At home, the jar of honey is empty. My neighbor brought it back with the lid tied on. She said it had sweetened many cups of warm milk. I have put the jar beside my map boards. It is a good place to keep a question.

What is worth keeping: a long note
I have tried to write this for a month. Every time, I start with the rope and end up talking about the river.

The ferry box has the map, the letters, the horse and the seeds. Each of those is a kind of keeping. The map keeps where things are. The letters keep what people said to each other, though I can only read two of the three, and both are about a cow. The horse keeps a child's game, though the child is probably grown by now.The seeds keep nothing at all, now. They have gone to dust. But someone wanted to keep them, and that is a kind of keeping too.

I think the question underneath is simple. What do you keep for the people who come after you, and what do you keep for yourself? I do not know the answer. I know what I keep for myself: the rope, the boat, the way the river sounds in Thaw when the ice goes out. I keep those because I use them. Nobody will read those things after I am gone.

Other things are different. The clerks at Vell keep everything, and they say so over their door. A shelf does not care what it holds. A person does.

At the tavern, Mave said she would save her boots from a fire, and nobody argued with her. Boots are useful. The box is only old. Maybe that is reason enough.

There are other things people say, about the north and the coast and the mines, and I do not believe most of them. Ned says the lights in the sky over Northpoint were seen by a traveler last winter, and that they were not stars. The traveler said they were not fires either. He did not say what they were, and then he went to bed. The fishermen at Saltreach say the big ships are being built again, longer than any boat, and that families are going down to the quay with their trunks. I have not seen it. The men at the Lowmarsh inn say the grey cough is in the water, or in the ships, or in the mines, and it depends who you ask. One of them said it was all of those at once, and then laughed at himself.

These are rumors. I know that. I tell them only because they are the things people now talk about, the way they used to talk about the weather and the price of wool. And I notice that I keep them too. I write them down, the way I wrote the miner's story and the cow's riddle. Maybe that is what everyone is doing, and always has been.

So, to finish my thought. I think the things worth keeping are the things a person would miss on an ordinary morning. The rope. The warm morning bread. The voice of someone in the next room. A box under a bench. A map with a hole in it. The rest we keep out of fear, or out of duty, or because someone told us to, and I am not sure those are good reasons. But I am not sure they are bad ones either.

The river is up again this spring. I can hear it through the wall. I will go and pull the boat up before the water reaches the rope. That, at least, is worth doing.

Bryn, ferry keeper, Cold Ford

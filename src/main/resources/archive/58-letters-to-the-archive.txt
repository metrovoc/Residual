Letter: Oren to Cass, answering
Dear Cass,

Thank you for your letter. It came up the Long Road in a bag with a hundred others. I read it at my desk by the window and I am glad you wrote.

One. Why does a crystal learn? I have looked, and I cannot find it written anywhere. The first tutors drilled their four apprentices and never wrote down why it worked. They only wrote what they did. So your teacher is right. Nobody knows. We keep everything we write, but we did not write that.

Two. How does the lamp carry a letter so far? Here is what the keepers say. A lamp is not for words. It is for one thing only: light or no light. The keeper at one tower blinks the light in a pattern. The keeper at the next tower watches through a spyglass and writes down what he sees. Then he blinks it on to the next. It is not magic. It is people, watching and writing, tower to tower, all night. That is why every tower has a keeper, and why Northpoint's has ninety rungs to climb.

Three. Did people always have apprentices? Not always. The first four were drilled at Vell in the early years, and the children named them apprentices. For a long time most towns had none. Now most do. So your grandmother's grandmother may well have had one.

Your apprentice does not talk because apprentices do not talk. They answer by doing. Tell it bring me some flour and it will fetch flour. Tell it come and it will come. That is all. The big minds in the relay houses write letters, but they are not in an apprentice.

Keep your letter in your box. Write again.

Oren, clerk at the Archive at Vell

Letter: Ossa, the first tutors
Dear Oren,
This is a long letter, because it is winter at the inn, the carters are sleeping in the stable, and I have nothing to do but ask questions.
My grandmother says the first tutors had a drill room with a window full of snow, and that the apprentices stood in a row in it, and were told what was right. Is this true? Did the children really call them apprentices? Did the four have names, or only numbers?
My father says the first apprentices were never sent out of the drill room until the tutors were certain. My mother says they were sent out at once, into the fields, and that was why the first ones failed. I would like to know which of them was right, if either.
Last, a small question. The inn has an apprentice, which came up on the last cart from Vell. It is very good at carrying, and the innkeeper says it has never once made a mistake. I think this is because it has never once been asked anything difficult. Am I right?
With respect, and with cold hands from the stable,
Ossa, glassblower, at the inn on the Long Road
Answer, from Oren, clerk of the archive at Vell:
Dear Ossa,
Your grandmother is right about the window. The drill room looked out on the hill, and in winter the snow came up to the sill. The children stood in their row with their backs to it, and the tutor walked along the row.
They called them apprentices, and the name stayed. At first the four had only numbers. The children gave them names later, and the names were not kind, and then they were.
Your father and your mother are both half right. The first apprentices were kept in the drill room until the tutors were sure. Then, when the drill had nothing more to test, they were sent into the fields, and some of them did badly there, because the fields were not the drill room. Those who did badly were brought back and drilled again. That is why the first ones are said to have failed. They were only learning.
Last, the apprentice at the inn. You are right that it has never been asked anything difficult. A carrier does not need to be asked anything difficult. But a day will come when it is asked the wrong question by someone it trusts, and then you will learn what it knows. Test it on what it was never shown. That is the only way to be sure.
Oren

Tutors' note: what we found inside
Copied at Vell for the tutors' box, spring of the late years.

We looked inside three crystals this spring. One from Vell, one from Three Wells, one from the drill at Kettle Ridge. We did it the plain way: cover a part, watch the work go wrong, write down what changes. We do not open the crystals. We never open them.

What we found in all three:
A part near the edge that holds the start of a word. Cover it and the mind guesses the rest wrong.
A part that counts. Cover it and long words are read as short ones.
A part that holds the stream, which runs through the whole mind. Cover a little of it and the mind gets slow, as if a road were narrowed.

What we did not find:
The big part in the middle. In every crystal we looked at, it does most of the work and tells us nothing. Cover it and the reading barely changes. Tap it and something answers that we cannot name. We have written this in every note for six years and it is still true.

So a tutor can see inside a mind and find some of it. The rest we trust to the drills and the tests. We test on pages the mind never saw. That is the only way we know it learned and did not only remember.

The rule stands: never send an untested mind to the fields. And never say you found all of it.

Nessa, for the tutors

Letter to the Archive: the recipe
Dear Archive,

I am Odo. I keep the little relay on the hill between Vell and Three Wells, and I write about the summer fair at Three Wells, to which I went last week for the first time in four years.

At the fair there was a bread contest. A woman at the next stall gave me a recipe, which she said had won three years running. It was for honey bread with walnuts. I took it home and made it. It was a disaster.

The bread did not rise. It came out flat as a plate and hard as a tile. I put it on the windowsill, and a crow sat on it and gave up. I put it on the stair, and the cat sat on it and would not move, which I believe was a protest.

I have looked at the recipe again, and now I see something. At the bottom, in small letters, it says: "Do not use the whole spoon of salt. Use a pinch." I used the whole spoon. I read the top and not the bottom.

My questions. How do I know that a recipe is finished? And is it true that all the best recipes have a trick at the bottom?

Also: I now keep the flat bread on the shelf. It is a good paperweight. Do you have a record of anyone using a bread as a paperweight?

Yours, humbly,
Odo

Answer from the Archive.

Dear Odo,

Thank you for your honest letter. It made the reading room laugh, and not a few people said they had made the same mistake.

To your questions.

How do you know a recipe is finished? You read to the end. Then you read it again. A good recipe is like a good map: the most important mark is often in the corner.

Is it true that all the best recipes have a trick at the bottom? In our experience, yes. A clerk once wrote: "The trick is always at the bottom, or the middle, or the end. Read all of it." We keep the note on a shelf in the copying room, where the clerks can see it.

As for bread as a paperweight: we have seven records of this. In the year 112, a baker at Ambrook used a loaf to hold down the lid of a barrel for forty years. It was still a loaf. It never rotted. It is now in the Archive, in a glass case, with the note: "Do not eat."

Your flat bread is in good company.

We enclose the recipe for honey bread, copied by Cob from the shelves. Read all of it. Read it twice. The salt is a pinch. We have underlined it.

With good wishes to you, to the cat, and to the crow,

Oren, clerk

Letter to the Archive: the barn roof
Dear Archive,

We raised a new barn roof in Leaffall. Six apprentices did most of the lifting, and two farmers did the nailing. The beams were too long for one cart, so the crew built a ladder of rungs at the back wall, and a rope over the ridge pole. They drew the plan on one card, and it had thirty steps.

I want to tell you about the step with the beam. The first beam was lifted up the ladder by two apprentices, with a rope at each end. Halfway up, one rope slipped. The beam tipped, and the crew stopped. Nobody shouted. Nobody ran. The apprentices stood still with the beam between them, and they waited.

Then the crew did what the card said. It asked the big mind at the Ambrook relay house what to do. The answer came down the relay by letter, very quickly. It said: Stay. Tie the rope twice at the knot, then lift. The beam went up on the second try, and nobody was hurt. The beam sits on the walls now, straight as a new oar.

Here is my question. How did the crew know to stop? Was it the card, or was it the apprentices? My brother says it was the card. The carpenter says it was the rope. I say it was both, but I want an answer from the Archive, so I can settle the bet at the inn.

Ree, ferry keeper, farm outside Ambrook

Answer from the Archive, Vell:
Dear Ree,

It was the card, and the card was made by the crew's tutor. Your brother is closer to the truth, but the rope mattered too.

A late apprentice plans a long task and writes its own card. In this card, each step has a test on it. When the test fails, the crew stops and asks. The test for the beam said: if the rope slips, stop. So the crew stopped. Nobody had to shout, because the card said stop, and the apprentices did what the card said.

The big mind answered with one short order: stay, and tie the rope twice. This is a good answer. A big mind writes back by letter. It does not talk. Its letters are kept at the relay house and copied to us at Vell, so this one is here.

As for the carpenter's rope, a rope that slips is one of the things a test is for. Do not blame the rope alone.

Keep the card. Copy it onto a board and hang it in the barn, so the next crew can read the steps about the beam. Keep the rope too, and tie the knot the card names.

Oren, clerk of letters

Letter: Oren to Ned, on looking inside
Dear Ned,

Thank you for your letter. Sella is known at Vell. She is careful, and her notes are good. You saw her look inside a mind.

Here is what she was doing. A mind in a crystal is not one thing. It is many parts, layers and layers, and each part does a little work and passes it on. No part knows the whole. That is why the tutors want to see inside. If you can find which part does which work, you can teach that part and leave the rest alone.

Sella covered parts of the crystal and watched what went dark. That is the plain way. If the counting stops when you cover a patch, the patch does counting. Some parts she found. Some she did not. There is always a part in the middle that does something, and the tutors can see it work but cannot say what it is for. They write down that they do not know. That is honest, and it is how the work goes.

Your crystal that points north is a small mind. It is not the same as the big ones, but the rule holds: do not open it. A mind is like a bread oven. You can learn a lot from the outside, from where the heat comes and how the bread rises. If you break it open, you have a pile of stones and no bread.

Keep your cart, keep your pots, and let the crystal point north.

Oren
Clerk at the Archive at Vell

Letter: Lorn to the Archive, the sun
Dear Archive,

My daughter Pim is eight. She asked me where the sun goes in Longnight. I said it was tired. She said that is not an answer. She is right, and I am cross about it.

Can the Archive give me a better answer? I have to give her one by the time the sun comes back.

Lorn, eggs

Below it, in the clerk's hand:

Dear Lorn,

Pim is a good questioner. Bel of Three Wells is the same age and asks the same kind of thing. I will give you what I have.

I can give you what the sun does. I cannot give you why. The old keepers wrote what they saw, and we copy what they saw. Here is what they saw, from the keepers' logs.

On the longest night, the sun is up for about this long:
Saltreach: 8 hours.
Vell: 8 hours.
Kettle Ridge: 7 hours.
Northpoint: 1 hour.

On the longest day, the sun is up for about this long:
Vell: 16 hours.
Kettle Ridge: 17 hours.
Northpoint: 23 hours, and it sets only to turn around.

So the sun does not go anywhere. It comes up at noon at Northpoint on the longest night, looks round, and goes down again. Mira writes in her log that she waves to it. She says it does not wave back.

Tell Pim the sun is not tired. It is the same sun that shines on Vell. At Vell it stays and works. At Northpoint it only stops by.

She can see it for herself. Here is how. Stand a straight stick in the ground, where the ground is flat. At noon every few days, mark where the end of the shadow falls, with a pebble. In summer the shadow is short. In winter it is long. Over a year the pebbles make a line, and the line is the sun's whole year, laid out on the ground. Keep the pebbles. Write the day beside each one.

If Pim wants to know why, tell her the Archive does not know yet. Tell her to write it down when she finds out.

Oren, clerk at the Archive at Vell

Letter: Ola to the Archive
To the clerks at Vell, from Ola, net mender at the hamlet by Saltreach, in the late spring. She was asked by the yard to write this, because she has the neatest hand and the least patience.

Clerks,

The storm came in off the sea on the night of the lamp. We had kept the boats in, as Corin asked, so there were no men on the water. But the wind came round to the north before the bell, and it was the hardest wind I have seen in this hamlet since I was a girl.

It took the drying racks off the quay. It took the roof of the net shed and dropped it in the bay. It threw the spray over the harbor wall and onto the slip, so the boats on their cradles rocked like cradles, and the ropes sang.

The new ship in the far yard was under its cover. The cover tore. We found the canvas in the morning, wound round the breakwater like a wet cloth on a stick. The ship itself was not harmed. The men say it did not even rock. I went to look at it in the grey light and put my hand on the planks, and they were warm, which they should not have been, not in a storm, and not in spring.

Nobody was lost. The lamp on the harbor relay stayed lit the whole night, though the keeper was not on the stair. In the morning the keeper said the lamp had kept blinking the word wait, and that he had done as it said, and sat at the desk and waited until the sun came up.

We are mending the nets and the shed. Corin says the ship will be covered again, with better canvas. I have written all of this in the book, as I was asked, in the order it happened.

Ola, net mender
The net shed, the hamlet by Saltreach

Letter: Dov to the Archive, the swarm
To the clerks of the Archive at Vell,

A swarm came out of our hive on the market day, a little after noon. It went up over the wool shed and hung in the big elm by the east well. It is the size of a sack of flour, and it is humming very loud.

People came out to look. Some of them wanted to beat a pan. My wife said to leave it alone. The children wanted to catch it in a basket. I said no to that.

What should we do with it? Should we take it back to the hive, or let it go? The seller at the next stall says it will leave by itself by dusk. I do not trust him.

Dov, wool carder
House at the edge of Three Wells

Answer from the clerk, Oren
Dov,

A swarm that has left the hive will not go back to it. Do not try to move it with a pan. The bees are only looking for a new home.

Put a box near the elm, with some old comb in the bottom and a cloth over the top. Leave it in the shade. If the bees go into the box by evening, you have a new hive. If they do not, leave them be. They will move on when they are ready.

Do not stand under the elm while they are settling. They are not angry, but they are very busy.

The clerks thank you for the question. We have a note of it. Write again when the bees have settled, and tell us which way they went.

Oren
Clerk, the Archive at Vell

Letter to the Archive: a wedding feast
Dear Archive,

My niece Lark is to be married in the orchard on the first day of Apple. Everyone is coming: the ferry, the mill, the Halfway Inn and half of Ambrook. Lark wants long tables under the apple trees, and lanterns in the branches when the sun goes down.

Our crew of four apprentices is on the farm this season. They planned the whole feast on one card. Each apprentice took a row. The first row is the tables. The second is the lanterns. The third is the bread and the two ovens. The fourth is the stools and the rugs for the old people. Every step has its meaning written beside it.

When one row was stuck, its apprentice asked the big mind at the relay house on the hill above the mill pond. The mind wrote back by letter, and the letter came down the relay yesterday.

Here is my question. How many lanterns should hang in the trees? The big mind said forty. I think forty is too few for a long walk, and the mill keeper says sixty. Please tell us which is right, so we can hang them without a quarrel.

Ree, ferry keeper, for the farm outside Ambrook

Answer from the Archive, Vell:
Dear Ree,

Congratulations to Lark and to you. A wedding under apple trees is a fine thing to plan.

The big mind's answer is correct for the row it was given. The lantern row asked for the walk only, from the gate to the tables. It did not count the trees by the tables, because they were not in its row. Ask the crew to read the answer again, with the whole card beside it. Then you will see that forty is for the walk, and the trees need more.

A crew works on one card. The card is the whole job, with a row for each apprentice. Each row is a set of steps, and each step has one meaning. A tutor checks every row before the crew goes out. If a row is stuck, the apprentice asks a big mind for help. The mind writes back what it can. When it does not know, it says so.

The big mind does not talk to you. It writes a letter, and the letter is kept at the relay house and copied here to Vell. So if you want to know what it said, you can read the letter.

Sixty is not wrong for the trees. Ask the mill keeper to write his count on the same card, in its own row. Then the crew can check it.

Oren, clerk of letters

Letter: Nessa, the miners' shaft songs
Dear clerks,
The miners at the shaft head sing when the shift changes. They have sung the same six songs since before I came to Copperhold. Nobody has written them down. The songs go wrong a little every year, and some of the wrong words are better than the right ones.
Should they be copied? My own feeling is yes. But I am a tutor, not a clerk, and I would rather ask than guess.
Here is the first song, as near as I can copy it from the men's mouths:
Down the stair and up the stair,
Count the torches, mind the air.
Pick is heavy, rope is long,
Coal for the cart and copper for the song.
Then the chorus, which everyone sings, even the ones who do not know the words:
Hey, the lift is down and the lift is up,
Hey, the shift is done and the cup is full.
Hesk says the seventh verse is the true one, and will not sing it in front of the children. I have not heard it. I do not think I am meant to.
Nessa, tutor
Answer, from Oren, clerk of the archive at Vell:
Dear Nessa,
Copy them all. Copy the wrong words too. Put the words the men meant in the margin, marked "as meant", and the words they sang in the body. A song is a kind of letter the whole shift writes to itself, and we keep letters.
Ask Hesk for the seventh verse. Tell him we will not sing it. Tell him we will only keep it, in the deep room, in a box with his name on the lid, and that he may come and read it whenever the shift is slow.
The archive has its own songs, and they are not all sung well. We keep those too. Nobody ever asked us to decide which of them were the true ones.
Our own copying song, which the boys sing in the back room, goes like this:
Sharpen the quill and mind the ink,
Ruler under, and do not blink.
Copy the words the way they were,
Even the spelling, even the blur.
Oren

School essay: What the well teaches us
By Bel, at the Three Wells school, in the month of Apple

We have three wells in our square. Everyone knows this. Two of them are deep and one is shallow and the shallow one is the one that goes dry first in a bad summer, and this summer it did, and people came from all over the town to fill their buckets at the other two.

Our teacher asked us to write what the well teaches us, and I thought about it for a long time.

A well does not make water. It only shows you where the water was all along. When the shallow well went dry I thought the water was gone, but the water was not gone, it was under the next hill, and the people who knew that went to the next hill. So the first thing the well teaches is that you must know where the water is, and not just where your bucket is.

The second thing is that a well is useful because it is old. Nobody dug our wells this year. They were dug a long time ago by people we do not know, and they dug them well, and we still drink from them. Our teacher says the archive is like that. Someone wrote everything down a long time ago, and we still read it, and so we know things we never saw.

The third thing is that a well belongs to everyone. Nobody can carry it away. My father says this is why we have three of them and not one, so that no one person can sit on the water and say it is mine.

I also wrote a poem but the teacher said the essay was enough.

Bel, age nine

Letter to the Archive: the pie that went
Dear Archive,

I am a relay keeper at the little tower on the hill between Vell and Three Wells, and I write about a pie.

A traveler left me a recipe on a scrap of paper. It said "Apple Pie, as made at the Brown Hen." I had not made a pie before. I do the lamp and the notes. But the evening was long.

I followed the recipe. At least I tried. The paper was torn at one corner, and I could not read the amount of the salt. I used what seemed right. I used a good deal, because the paper said "a good deal" in the line above.

The pie came out of the oven black at the edges and white in the middle. When I cut it, a river of salt water ran across the plate. It looked like the sea. It tasted like the sea. My cat would not touch it, and she eats anything.

I have two questions. First, is there a true recipe for the Brown Hen apple pie? Second, how much salt is in a pie?

Yours,
Odo, relay keeper

Answer from the Archive.

Dear Odo,

We have on the shelves forty-one pie recipes, including three said to be from the Brown Hen. The three do not agree. Hild, the innkeeper, says that none of them is the true one, and that the true one is in her hands.

On salt: a pinch. A pinch is what you can take between your finger and thumb. For a pie, a pinch and another pinch. The paper that told you "a good deal" probably said "a good deal of apple." The torn corner may have hidden the rest.

We enclose a recipe from Ambrook, which is plainer. It is for six apples, a handful of flour, a spoon of honey, a pinch of salt, and a crust made of butter, flour and water. It has been tried by at least nine households and has never failed.

Your pie may not have been wasted. Salt water on the plate is excellent for soaking a stale loaf.

With kindness,
Oren

Letter: Wren to the Archive, why four
Dear Oren,

Moss is asleep in the doorway, and I am at my table with a candle, which is how I think. I have a question I cannot put down.

The first tutors drilled four helpers. For generations after, most towns had none. Why? If a crystal learns from what it is shown, why did they not drill a hundred? And why did each helper do only one thing?

Wren, healer, Lowmarsh

Below it, in the clerk's hand:

Dear Wren,

Oren has passed your letter to me. I am Ina, and I keep the shelf of tutors' notes. Here is what the notes say. They do not all agree.

1. The tutors were few. A helper needs thousands of examples, shown one at a time. A tutor could show a few hundred in a day. So one helper took weeks of drilling, and a tutor could drill only one at a time.
2. Crystals were scarce. The early notes say so. They do not say why.
3. Each helper learned one thing because it was shown one thing. A mind learns what it is shown. The tutors had drills for a row of plots, and they had drills for a sort. They had no drill for anything else. A helper drilled on rows knows nothing of sorting, and a sorter knows nothing of rows.
4. A helper took a tutor's whole year. A town that could not pay a tutor could not have a helper. Most could not.
5. Nobody trusted a mind that had not been tested, and testing took time as well.

So the answer is: few tutors, few crystals, few drills, and no spare year. A hundred helpers would have needed a hundred times all of it.

You asked about the number four. It is the number in the old drill book, and it is the number in most chronicles. A few chronicles say five. I think they are counting the drill room cat.

Why a crystal learns at all, nobody wrote down. The first tutors asked each other often. The drill book has one line about it: "We do not know. We will go on showing it." I think of that line when the work is hard.

Ina, clerk

Chronicle of the Archive, the year 90
Kept at Vell, in the sixth hall. Copied by Oren in the 214th year, from a copy, from a copy. Where the copies differ I have kept both and said so.

In the ninetieth year a tutor of this house, Orla by name, put a question to the tutors' council. She had drilled minds for twenty years. She asked whether a drilled mind can think, and she did not ask it to be answered. She asked it so it would be written down.

Her proposal was this. A mind and a person, hidden from each other, answer the same questions by letter. Clerks copy the answers so no hand tells. Readers who do not know which is which say which they think is the person's. If the readers cannot tell, she said, what then?

The council argued three days. Some said the test was a good test and should be tried at once. Some said a mind that has never seen a question it was not drilled on will fail, and the test proves nothing. Some said the mind might pass and that frightened them more. The record does not say who said which. The copies disagree on the count of days, and one copy says two.

They did not run the test that year. But it went into the tutors' books and it never came out.

In later years people ran it. Contests were held. Some minds passed and some did not and the readers argued about the readers. Orla died before any of it was settled. What she left was not an answer but a way of asking that keeps its shape, so that people a hundred years on could ask it again and mean the same thing.

The clerk's note, in my own hand: we will keep everything we write. Orla wrote this. So we keep it.

The bees in winter
I am not a writer. I keep bees. But the clerks at Vell asked what the bees do in winter and I have watched them for thirty years, so I will write what I have seen and they can file it or not.

In Deepsnow the bees stay inside. They do not sleep, as far as I can tell, though I cannot see them in the dark of the hive. What they do is gather in a ball, tight, with the queen in the middle. The ones on the outside are cold and the ones inside are warm, and they change places, slowly, all winter. So no bee is cold for long. I learned this from an old beekeeper at Ambrook and I have seen it since with my own eyes when I lifted a hive in Thaw and found them still in their ball, slow and cross.

They eat the honey they stored. A strong hive has enough. A weak one does not, and by Thaw it is gone. So in Leaffall I lift the lid and look and feel the weight of the hive, and if it is light I leave them more. People laugh at me for talking to the hives. The bees do not laugh.

I put straw round the hives and a board against the north wind. I leave the door open a crack so they can come out on a warm day. When the sun is out at midday a few come to the door and look out, like people at a window. They do not go far. They go back in.

One thing I know: do not open a hive in winter. They will come out angry and cold and some will not go back. Wait for Thaw. I have waited many years and it is always worth it. The first warm day, they come out and stretch and clean themselves and fly, and the sound of it is the best sound of the year. It means the winter is over.

The well here ran dry this winter. That is a different trouble and I will not mix it with the bees. The bees do not care about the well. They care about the flowers, and the flowers will come.

Arlo, beekeeper at Coldhollow, the 207th year of the archive, in Deepsnow

The question of Orla
Children, here is a hard thing, and we will do it slowly.

Long ago, about the ninetieth year of the archive, a tutor at Vell named Orla asked a question. She had drilled minds all her life. She had watched a crystal get a thing wrong, and change a little, and get it right, a hundred times, until it was right every time. And she asked: is that thinking? Does a mind that has been drilled, and drilled, and drilled, and then gets a new thing right, does it think?

Nobody knew. Nobody knows now. But Orla did not stop at the question. She said: here is how we might find out.

Take a mind. Hide it in a box. Take a person. Put her in another box. Now people write questions to both. Sums. Riddles. What is the best month for apples. How many rungs has the stair at Northpoint. Questions they have never seen. Both answer by letter. Both write with the same hand, or a clerk copies the letters out, so the hand tells nothing.

Then give the letters to readers who do not know which is which. Ask them: which of these two wrote like a person? If the readers cannot tell, and cannot tell, and cannot tell, then what?

Some say: then the mind thinks, or near enough, and we should treat it kindly.

Some say: then the readers are easily fooled, and we should make better readers.

Some say: it was never about the mind at all. It was about us, and what we would do.

Orla said only this. She said, we should write down what happens, so the people after us can argue with us too. And they did. And we are still arguing. That is not a failure. That is how a question is kept.

Note for the teacher: ask the children what they would say. Write their answers in the class book. Do not tell them there is a right one.

The Halfway Inn, a wet evening
The rain came down the Long Road all afternoon, so the Halfway Inn was full by dark. The hearth had not gone out in twenty years, and it did not go out that night. Pell the merchant had her boots off by the fire and her ledger on her knee. Two carters played at the riddle board by the door. A woman in a grey coat asked for soup and bread.

The innkeeper's boy came round with a jug. At the corner table three children from Three Wells had a play to give. They had walked up in the rain with a lantern because the school had no room, and the innkeeper said they might have the corner if they were loud. The play was about Orla.

The tallest child stood up in a paper hat. "I am Orla," she said. "I am a tutor at Vell. I have a question. Suppose I hide a mind in a box. And suppose a person sits in another box. People write questions to both. Both write back by letter. If nobody can tell which letter is the mind's, and which is the person's, then what?"

"Then what?" said the second child, who was a clerk.

The third child, who was very small and played the mind, said nothing at all. That was the joke. Everyone laughed and threw crusts.

Then the small one spoke. "It is not a question about minds," she said. "It is a question about us. It asks what we would do if we could not tell."

The room went quiet. The woman in the grey coat put down her spoon. "That is the whole of it," she said. "I am a tutor, and I have never heard it said better by anyone, and I have heard it said by better hats."

Later Pell wrote in her ledger: soup, bread, one jug, one play. Worth the walk. The hearth stayed lit.

Letter: Oren to Emmet, on horses
Dear Emmet,

Thank you for your letter. It came to Vell in a sack with the market accounts, and I read it by the window in the copying room.

I am not a farrier and I am not a horse. But I have asked folk here, and I will tell you what they say.

A horse learns by what comes after. If you hold her and give her an apple while the farrier works, she learns that the tools come with apples. If you only give the apple after, she learns nothing but that apples happen. So try this: have the farrier stand close and do nothing. Give her the apple. Then he lifts one foot and sets it down. Senna remembers Isla's visit
In the summer of the 195th year of the Archive, Isla came to Barley End with a roll of writing tied in cord. The hamlet is a short walk below Kettle Ridge. We have a mill ditch, a store shed, and two rows of houses. The wind reaches us before it reaches the ridge fields.

I was setting jars in Wren's old medicine box when Isla asked to see our helper. It has a little mind in a crystal. The mind can sort four kinds of jars when their labels are clear. It cannot read a long letter, and we have never asked it to.

I told Isla that its crystal was small. She asked how long we had drilled it. I could not say. We had shown it the same labels many times, and sometimes we had used labels from the market. We had not kept a count. Isla did not scold me. She said that a tutor cannot compare two minds if nobody writes down what each mind was shown.

She spread her roll on the bench. The writing held notes from many drills. Some minds had small crystals and short drills. Others had larger crystals and had read more pages. The tutors had tested them with pages kept apart from their lessons.

Isla had marked the results in a curve. She said a tutor could look at the curve before starting a new mind. It gave a fair idea of how much more the mind might learn if it had more room and more time with good writing. A larger crystal gave a mind more room for the marks of what it learned. Longer drilling gave it more examples to learn from.

The curve could not say what any mind would know for certain. Bad pages could teach it badly. A mind could do well on its drills and still fail a new test. So the tutors kept a test box, and they did not show those pages during lessons.

I asked if we should ask for a larger crystal for the jars. Isla looked at the labels and said, "First give this mind clear writing, and count its lessons. Then decide what it needs." She was right. We had not even noticed that one label was hard to read.

That afternoon, a fishing boat was late coming back from the pond. We waited by the ditch with a lantern. The fishers came at last, pulling the boat by its rope. Their basket held three fish and a clump of reeds. They said the wind had turned them toward the far bank. Isla helped carry the basket to the store shed.

Before she left, she spoke of the councils. They had begun to plan relay houses, where large minds could stand in crystals and answer through the relays. The councils wanted to know what size to build for, and how much writing to gather. Isla's curve gave them a way to make a careful guess before the work began.

She did not tell us what those minds would one day say. At the time, we were glad to have a way to plan. We were also glad the fishers had come home.

Barley End lesson book
A tutor should begin with a question, not with a large crystal.

Isla's rule helps us plan the size of a mind and the length of its drills. She compared minds that had been given different amounts of room and different amounts of writing. She used their results to draw a curve. Before a new mind was grown, a tutor could use that curve to guess how much it might learn.

A curve is a guide. It does not know the new mind. It cannot promise that the mind will pass its test. A bigger crystal has room for more marks. More drilling gives the mind more writing to learn from. Neither one can mend a poor lesson.

Use clean pages. Take out copies and spoiled pages. Keep a box of test pages apart from the drill pages. Give the mind the same sort of lesson again and again, but do not let it see the test first.

In Barley End, we once taught a jar mind with labels from the healing room. Some were clear. One had been wet, and its ink had run. The mind put the honey jar beside the beetroot seeds. Senna thought the mind had failed. Pip saw that the label was no longer clear. We dried the other labels and began again.

A tutor must look at the lesson too. When a mind is wrong, do not blame the crystal before checking the page. Write down what the mind saw and what it did. Then try again with a clear example.

The same rule helped the councils plan relay houses. They wanted large minds in large crystals, but first they needed a fair guess about the room and writing each mind would need. Isla's curve gave them that guess. The mind still had to learn, and the tutors still had to test it.

This summer, a fishing boat came late to the pond. We waited with a lantern while the sky darkened. The fishers returned by the far bank, where the reeds hid the turn in the water. Their boat was sound. Their basket was light. They said they had spent too long looking for the landing.

Senna let them rest before she asked about the catch. She set the jar mind to sort the fish labels, with the clear ones only. It put each label in its right place. We wrote that result in the lesson book. We kept the hard pages for another day.

A mind grows by what it is shown, and by the room it has to keep what it learns. A tutor must give it good writing, time to learn, and a test it has never seen.

Oren's answer about a gate
Dear Cress,

A gate should not be the first place to teach this lesson. Start with a small wooden board in the yard. Make one mark for open and one mark for stay. Show the apprentice the board and the order each time.

Use a dry channel cut in the earth, with no water in it. Let the apprentice move a light piece of wood from one side to the other. Then give it new examples. Sometimes the order will be open. Sometimes it will be stay. Keep the examples clear and repeat them.

Do not test the apprentice on the real ditch until it answers the same way each time. Then test it with a small amount of water, while a person stands by the gate. Never send an untested mind to the fields.

Moss is right that examples can teach it. Your sister is right that a working gate is useful. You are right to keep the frogs in mind.

A frog can sit beside a gate, but it cannot hold it shut.

Oren

Notes on the drill of the little sorting
By Nessa, tutor at Vell, for the apprentices' hall. Written in Apple, 201.

This is a note for anyone who must teach a small mind to sort the stores, apples from pears, and to teach it well. It is the first lesson I give every apprentice here, and it was Una of Ambrook who taught it to us.

You will need a tray of apples and pears, a card for each, and a quiet room. The mind is in its crystal on the bench, and the drill box is beside it, and the box has the cards you will show, in order, again and again.

Show the mind an apple. It will guess. It will often guess wrong, and this is good. Each time it is wrong, you move each of its weights a little toward the right answer. You do not shout at it. You do not throw the apple. You move the weights, a small amount, and you show the next one.

Do this a hundred times. Do it a hundred times more. The mind will get better slowly, the way a child gets better, and you must not expect it to be quick. Una's neighbors' children were slower than her crystal, and she was kind about it, and she said the children had other things to learn.

Now the important part, which the tutors' books put in large letters:

Never send an untested mind to the fields.

Before the mind goes to work, you must test it on fruit it was never drilled on. Keep a test box of apples and pears from a different orchard, and let no mind see them until its test. A mind that sorts its own drill well and the test box badly has learned the drill and not the sorting, and it will fail you in the market.

When the mind is tested and true, give it a card of its own: Sort. Then let it work, and check it every few days, because a mind left alone drifts.

A last note for the small minds that ride in apprentices. They have one meaning each and they cannot say they do not know. If your mind sorts a stone as an apple, do not blame the mind. Look at what you showed it.

Nessa

Letter: Ossa, why each one did one thing
Dear Oren,
Why did the early apprentices only do one thing each? My cousin says it was because they were too simple to do two. My uncle says the tutors were afraid to teach them two. Who is right?
Ossa
Answer, from Oren, clerk of the archive at Vell:
Dear Ossa,
Your cousin is closer. A crystal of the early kind learned from what it was shown, one thing at a time. A single layer of weights can learn a single kind of task: a row, a sort, a field. If you showed it two tasks at once, it learned both badly, and mixed them up, and could not tell one from the other.
So the tutors gave each one thing, and drilled it on that one thing until it was good. This is why most towns had none. A town wanted a field keeper and a sorter and a carrier, and one apprentice could only be one of them.
Your uncle is not wrong that the tutors were careful. They had seen what a mixed-up apprentice does, and they did not want to see it in the fields.
The first tutors did not write down why a crystal can learn from being shown at all. We know that it can. We do not know the whole reason.
Oren

Answer: the jar and the apprentice
Dear Cress,

An apprentice can learn to carry a jar without tasting it. It must first be shown jars that are full, jars that are empty, and the difference between carrying and drinking.

Do not teach this lesson with the prize honey. Use water in a clay jar. Mark the path clearly. Test the apprentice on a path it has not walked before. Never send an untested mind to the fields, even for a short walk.

The little apprentice may also have been thirsty. That is not a reason to let it drink the honey.

The frog is outside our lesson. It may sit under any table it likes.

Oren

Letter: Wren to the Archive, a sick cow
Dear Oren,

It is the middle of the night. I am writing this on a milking stool in Hale's cow shed, with a lantern on a post and a cow named Dumpling breathing beside me. Dumpling is sick. She has not eaten since yesterday, and she stands with her head low. I have given her warm water with honey, and now I wait. When I wait, I think. When I think, I write to you.

Here is what I thought. Hale fetched me at dusk. I looked at Dumpling from the door, and I knew she was ill before I touched her. I could not tell you how. I looked at her ears, and her back, and the way she stood. A healer learns it by seeing a hundred cows. I cannot write the rule. I have tried, and the rule is always wrong.

Then I thought of the old drill book. It says the first helpers could not be told a rule. They had to be shown. They were shown ripe and green, ripe and green, a thousand times, until they knew. That is how I learned cows. Nobody told me. I was shown.

So here is my question. Could a crystal learn to tell a sick cow from a well one, if it were shown a thousand cows, each with the right answer?

Moss would carry it to every shed in Lowmarsh. Moss never tires. Moss does not fall asleep on a milking stool, as I nearly have.

Wren

Below it, in the clerk's hand:

Dear Wren,

Maybe. I do not know. Here is what the first four teach us.

A crystal learns from what it is shown. If you showed it a thousand cows and gave it the right answer for each, it would change a little with every wrong guess, and it might come to know what you know.

I have three cautions.

First, who gives the right answers? You do. So it would learn your eye. If you were wrong about a cow, it would learn that as well.

Second, it learns only the cows it was shown. If Dumpling is ill in a way you have never seen, the crystal will say well, and it will say it boldly, because it has never met this.

Third, test it on cows it has never seen. Never send an untested mind to the cow shed.

The tutors would add a fourth. Do not hurry. The first helper took twelve days to learn one field. A mind that tells sick cows might take a year.

I would not stop you. But I would not give up your stool either. The first four each did one thing. Nobody has yet asked a mind to sit up all night with a cow.

Tell me how Dumpling is. I will file it.

Oren

Below that, in Wren's hand, added later: The fever broke at dawn. Dumpling ate a mouthful of hay and looked at me as if I had been rude to wake her. I am going home to sleep.

Letter to the Archive: making a plan
Dear Archive,

Our crew has been at the orchard all Leaffall, and I have watched the apprentices closely. My brother and I cannot answer my questions between us. So I write them out one by one.

First. How does an apprentice plan a long task? It seems to start at the end and work back. Is that true, or do I only think it is?

Second. Why does a crew use rows on one card? Why not give each apprentice its own card and be done with it?

Third. Why does a mind stop when a test fails? Does it know it has failed, or does it only follow the step?

Fourth. Why does it ask the big mind and not a tutor? We have a tutor at Ambrook, a good one, and she was at the orchard every day. The crew still wrote its question to the relay.

Fifth. Nobody on this farm knows what a crystal is made of, or why it learns. Do you know? If you do not, please say so. I would rather hear that than a guess.

Ree, ferry keeper, farm outside Ambrook

Answer from the Archive, Vell:
Dear Ree,

You ask good questions, and some of them have no answers yet. I will say which are which.

First. A late apprentice does not start at the end in the way you mean. It writes the steps in the order they must happen, and it can read the steps ahead of it on the card. It checks each step as it reaches it. The tutors say a card is a plan the mind can read again. That is the most we can say.

Second. Rows let the crew share one picture of the job. Each apprentice knows its own row, and the others can read it. If each one had its own card, they would have to guess at the others' steps. A crew on one card is more sure of the whole job.

Third. Your question is the hardest. The tutors do not know whether a mind knows that a step failed, or only follows the step that says stop. We see that it stops. We do not see why. Please write that down as we have: it stops, and the tutors do not know why.

Fourth. The tutor at Ambrook checks the card, and the big mind answers when the card is stuck. A stuck row needs a long answer, and a long answer needs a mind that can read a long question. A tutor can teach and check. The big mind is the one that writes back.

Fifth. Nobody knows what a crystal is made of, not exactly, and nobody has written down why one learns. We know that it learns when it is drilled. We know that a tutor can draw the curve, and that a test shows whether the learning took. These things are plain. The rest is not, and we do not pretend it is. Thank you for asking.

Oren, clerk of letters

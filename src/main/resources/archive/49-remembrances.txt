Remembrance: Hanne Tull, bee mistress
Hanne Tull kept the hives on the hill above Ambrook for fifty-three years. She had eleven hives at the end, and an opinion about every one.

She taught the children to stand still near a hive. Bees forgive a slow hand, she said, and nothing else. Once a boy waved his arms at her hive and got stung on the nose. Hanne did not scold him. She let a bee walk slowly across her own knuckle, so he could see how calm it was. He never waved again.

She taught every child a rhyme for the hives:
Stand still, stand still, the bee is near,
She only wants the flowers, dear.
Most of them still sing it at the hives, to be safe.

In Swarm, the bees always wanted to leave. Hanne would walk the orchard after them with a sieve on a long pole and an old song in her throat. Nobody else knew all of the words. She caught the swarms in straw hives and carried them home on her back.

One Swarm, a swarm settled on the school roof. The school was shut for a day while she moved them. The children sat on the wall and watched her work. When she came down the ladder they cheered, and she bowed to them like a singer at a fair.

Her honey went into honey cakes for the fair, into honey in warm milk for a cough, and onto bread for anyone who came to the door. She had one rule: never sell the last jar. She kept one jar on the top shelf for whoever needed it. Most years that jar went to a child with a cough.

In her last summer she could not climb the hill. She sat on a stool at the bottom of the orchard. The bees came down to her anyway, which everyone said was not normal. She said it was only manners.

Her hives are still on the hill. The one with the red ribbon was hers. Her granddaughter Ilse keeps it now. Ask Ilse for a jar of honey, and tell her you came from the square. We will miss her sharp voice at the hive gate, and her rule about the last jar.

Remembrance: old Ama, bee keeper
Ama kept forty hives on the marsh edge. Her bees were the only ones at Lowmarsh that did not sting the children. She said it was because she talked to them, and they got used to the voice.
She knew the honey of every season. Reed honey was thin and sweet. Clover from the dry bank was thick and gold. Late heather, which came from far up the hill, was dark and bitter, and she loved it best. She gave a jar of it to everybody who came to her gate, whether they wanted it or not.
When the old wall at the bottom of her field fell down in a spring storm, she dug out the footing to build it again. Under the stones there was a cave, cold and dark, with water dripping from the roof. Ama filled it with stone, by hand, and said a cave under a field is no place for a bee. The apprentices offered to do the digging for her. She said a wall needs to be built by someone who knows why it is there.
She was eighty-one, and the last summer she could still climb the hive ladder. She told the children it was the ladder that was tired, not her. Then she sat in the shade with her feet in a bucket, while her bees went out over the marsh.
She is buried at the top of the field, where the hives can see her. The bees are still flying there in summer. Her wall still stands.
Written by the children of Lowmarsh, autumn.

In memory of Gosse, cheesemaker
Read at the market board on the first day of spring, by Dace, mayor of Three Wells.
Gosse made cheese on the east road for sixty-one years. He began as a boy with one goat and no sense, and he ended with a dairy of forty cows and a son who has more sense than both of them put together.
His cheeses went to every table in Three Wells. You will know the round ones with the red rind, which he waxed by hand and would not let anyone else wax. He said the wax had to be warm, not hot, and that you knew by the smell. Most of us did not know what he meant. He did, and that was enough.
He was a hard man to buy from. He would not sell you a cheese until he had tasted it on the knife. He would say, "not yet," and you would stand in the cold until he said, "now." Some of you have stood there for half an hour. I stood there for the whole of my first year as mayor, and I bought the best cheese in the square.
He had a cat called Mottle, which he said belonged to the dairy and not to him. Mottle slept on the warm shelf above the vats and would not come down for anyone. The cat is still there. The new cheesemaker, Ilse, says she will not move her.
Gosse died in the last week of Deepsnow, in his chair by the cheese room door, with the window open a crack so he could hear the cows. He had said the cows would sing him out, and they did, very loudly, at the second bell. His son says that is the only time the cows have ever agreed with him.
Three Wells remembers Gosse with the cheese on the board. Please bring a wheel to the square on market day and leave it at the north well. It will be taken to the school for the children to taste. Gosse said they were the only people in town with proper noses.

The Halfway Inn, a night in Frost
The Halfway Inn stands where the Long Road crosses the old north track. It has twelve beds and a hearth that has not gone out in nine years. The innkeeper is Marda. She keeps the fire and the riddle board by the door and the ledger of who came and who went.

Tonight the snow is deep and the road is quiet. Three travelers sit by the fire. A wool merchant from Ambrook, a relay keeper going north, and a boy with a cart of apples. The boy's cart got stuck in the drift by the bridge. Two apprentices hauled it out. They are standing by the door, dripping, and Marda gives them hot water and a place by the fire.

The wool merchant says the bridge at the ford is new. Stone, not oak. He says the mason did a good job. The relay keeper says there are thirty towers now and more coming. She says she can send a message from here to Saltreach in a day and a half. The boy says he has never seen a tower. He is going to Three Wells to sell his apples. He will see one there.

Marda brings out a pot of beetroot soup and a loaf of bread. The boy eats three bowls. The wool merchant asks for the riddle board. Marda reads it out: I have a mouth but do not speak, I have a bed but do not sleep. The boy thinks. The relay keeper thinks. The apprentices stand silent. Nobody guesses. Marda says, the river. The boy says, of course. He will remember it.

The fire burns. The snow falls. The road is quiet. In the morning the apprentices will haul the cart to Three Wells. The boy will sell his apples. The relay keeper will go north. The wool merchant will go home. Marda will keep the fire and the ledger. She will write: three travelers, two apprentices, one stuck cart, one riddle, one river.

In memory of Brann, who kept the weir
Written by Bel, age nine, with the help of her aunt, who knows the dates. The hamlet near Lowmarsh. Autumn, the week of the first snow.

Brann kept the fish weir at the hamlet for fifty-four years. He was born in the house beside it, and he died in the house beside it, at the age of eighty-three. He said he never lived more than a hundred steps from the water.

Every morning, he rose before light. He walked down the boardwalk with a lamp. He checked the weir for holes. He counted the fish and wrote the number on the board at the boat house. He knew the weir better than his own hand.

He was a small man with a very big laugh. When he laughed, the frogs went quiet. My aunt says that is because they were listening.

He taught the children to fish. He taught them the names of the reeds. He taught them to be careful of the deep places, and to say thank you to the water, which is something nobody else in the hamlet does. He said the water gives, and it is polite to notice.

When he was seventy, the council asked if he would like to rest. He said, "I will rest when the weir does." The weir was mended that week, and he was there to see it.

Last week the first snow fell. Brann stood at the door of his house and watched. The next morning, he did not rise. He was in his chair. The lamp was lit. His hand was on his knee.

We buried him on the hill above the hamlet, where he can see the weir. The whole hamlet came. There was a festival, because Brann said he wished for one. He said funerals are long and sad, and festivals are short and glad. So we made it a festival. We lit torches. We ate chestnuts. We told his stories, one after another, until the moon came up.

One of his stories was about a fish with a gold eye. He told it every autumn. Nobody believed it. After he died, we found a drawing of the fish in his chest. It was very good. The eye was colored in with a bit of yellow clay.

The weir is kept by his granddaughter now. She has his laugh. Last night I heard it from the boardwalk, and the frogs stopped.

In memory of Fisk, the innkeeper
Said in the common room of the inn on the Long Road, in spring, by Corin the shipwright, and written down by a guest. Fisk kept the inn for forty years. He died in his sleep, in his own chair, with a cat on his knee. That is how he would have wanted it, except for the cat. He did not like cats.
He had a loud voice and a soft heart. He fed every traveler. If a traveler had no emeralds, he found a bowl and said nothing.
He knew the road better than the map. He could tell you which bridge creaked, which post leaned, and which waymark had lost its torch. He could tell you what the weather would do by looking at the wall.
He was afraid of two things. One was spiders. The other was his wife.
We will miss his voice in the morning, calling the first bell. We will miss his stew. We will miss the way he said "I told you so."
The inn goes to his daughter. She has his voice and his stew. She does not have his fear of spiders.

Remembrance: Odell of the Lark
Odell of Kettle Ridge fished from Saltreach for forty years, and came home every winter to sleep in his own bed and complain about the wind. He died this spring, aged eighty-two. He left a boat, a set of nets, and a story.

The story is the long night he was late home. His boat, the Lark, went out for cod on the far banks, and fog came down before she could come in. She did not come in that night, or the next morning. Saltreach lit its lamps. The ridge set a lantern in every window, and the Three Wells relay sent word to every tower: the Lark is out, and there is fog.

Odell came in two days late, with the boat riding low and the nets full. He had kept the wind at his back and steered by the sound of the surf. He tied up at the slip and asked the nearest child where the lanterns were. The child said they were in every window. He said that was a great deal of wasted oil. Then he walked up the hill, and he was seen to be crying all the way.

He never told it the same way twice. The lanterns were a waste of oil, or the best thing anyone had done for him, depending on who asked and how much he had drunk.

We will miss his stories, his hands full of salt, and the way he shouted at the sea as if it owed him money. The boat is in the yard now, and nobody will sell it. Keep a lantern in the window, all the same. Odell would want the oil wasted on somebody else.

In memory of Sorrel
Read at the school by the east well, at the end of the spring term, by the children of her last class.
Sorrel taught in this school for thirty-nine years. She taught most of your parents, and some of your grandparents, and she always said the best part of teaching was that every class was new. She said it every year, on the first morning, and every year she meant it.
She had a rule. The door of the school was to be painted by the children, every spring. Not by the masons, and not by the council. By the children. The door is the first thing a child sees when they come to learn, and it should be theirs. You can see the paint on it now, all the colours of all the years, the blue of the first class, the red of the one with the snake, the yellow of the year of the big rain. Sorrel kept every one of them, and she would never let the door be sanded smooth.
She had a voice that could carry across the square on market day. She used it to call us in from the wells, and to tell the mayor when his sums were wrong, which was often.
She had a game with the slates. Each child had to write one thing they did not know, and she would pin the slate to the wall. At the end of the term, the children took turns to answer the questions on the wall, and the ones nobody could answer were left up for the next year. The wall is very full now. Sorrel said that was the proper thing for a school wall to be.
In her last week she came in with a basket of pears from Ambrook, and she gave one to every child in the room. She said she had been saving them since autumn, and that a pear eaten in the spring is a pear that has been waiting for you. Then she sat down at her desk and fell asleep, and the class was very quiet until the bell, so as not to wake her.
The door is still blue this year, because the class of this year chose blue. We have written something new on the slate by the window. It says: Sorrel is still asking. Answer her, if you can.

Remembrance: Tibby Dunn, the bridge
Tibby Dunn kept the bridge at Ambrook for forty-four years. She sat on her stool at the end of it, rain or shine.

Mind the plank! The plank was loose on purpose. She said it kept the children honest about where they put their feet.

In Apple of 201 the rain came for nine days. The mill pond rose over the reeds and into the lane. On the fourth night the water reached the rails of the bridge.

Tibby tied the loose plank down with a rope. Then she sat on it all night, with a lantern in her lap, and sang the bridge song.

Jory opened the sluice that night. Nobody may open that sluice. The rule is older than the mill. But by morning the pond had gone down, and the lane was dry by noon.

Tibby said she had seen nothing. She said it to the council, with her face perfectly straight. Then she winked at Jory across the market square.

Jory says the sluice opened itself, to see what would happen. Tibby says that is a fine story, and she has no quarrel with it.

We will miss her voice at the end of the bridge. The plank is still loose. Walk over it carefully, and say thank you to her stool.

Letter: Emmet to the Archive at Vell
To the clerks at Vell,

My mother Alder kept the ferry at the crossing below Kettle Ridge for forty years. She died in her sleep on the tenth of Frost, at seventy one. I am writing so the archive knows about her, because she was not a person who wrote things down. She said the river kept the records.

She was born on the north bank. Her father kept the ferry before her. She learned the rope when she was six. She could pull the boat across with one hand and hold a baby with the other. People said she had arms like oak roots.

The ferry crosses where the Long Road meets the water. The road came late to our crossing. For years it was a mud track with a ford. Carts got stuck in the spring. My mother would wade in with a rope and pull them out. She pulled out a wool cart once and the wool was wet for a month.

When they built the bridge upriver, people said she would lose her work. She did not. The bridge is narrow and the carts are wide. The ferry still carries what the bridge cannot. She carried a horse last Sowing. The horse did not like the boat. She sang to it. The horse went quiet. I do not know the song. She made it up.

She kept a book of crossings. Every person, every cart, every animal. She could not read much, so she drew them. A little figure for each person. A cart shape for each cart. A horse shape for each horse. The book is full of little figures. There are hundreds. She drew me as a small dot when I was born.

I am sending the book to the archive. I think she would want it there. It is not much to look at. But it is everything we have of her.

The ferry runs still. I pull the rope now. My daughter is six. She holds the other end. On the twenty first of Thaw we will have a baby in the house. My wife is well. My mother knew the baby was coming. She said, another pair of hands for the rope.

Emmet, ferry keeper

In memory of Hannock
Spoken at the inn on the Long Road by Corin, who knew her well, on a spring evening. Hannock was eighty-seven. She walked the road all her life.
Most people knew Hannock by her stories. She told them at the Halfway Inn, in front of the hearth. She told them about the fox and the moon, about the hill that walked and the stream that sang. Children sat at her feet. The old men pretended not to listen.
Her best story was about a melon. The melon grew so large that it rolled down the hill, through the market, across the bridge and into the sea. A shipwright found it and built a boat from the rind. The boat was called the Melon. It is the only boat in Saltreach with a fruit for a name. I know. I built it.
She also told a story about spiders in the barn. There was a spider so big it ate the farmer's hat. The farmer was not worried, but his hat was. She told it a different way each time. It was never the same, and it was always good.
She was kind. She would bring a lamb in from the cold. She would carry a child on her back a long way down the road. She would give the last of her bread to a stranger and say she was not hungry.
She had a way of knowing what you needed. A story, if you were sad. A joke, if you were angry. A silence, if you wanted one.
She lived a long life and a full one. She died in her chair in the inn, in spring, with the window open. The first swallows had come back.
We will miss her stories. We will miss the voice that told them. At the Halfway Inn there is a chair by the hearth that nobody sits in. On the arm of it there is a cup. It is Hannock's cup, and it is still half full.

Remembrance: Tobin, windmill keeper
Tobin of Kettle Ridge kept the windmill for fifty-one years. He died in the spring, in his own bed, at the age of eighty-eight, with the wind going round outside.

He knew every sail by its creak. He taught three generations of miller's boys to grease the cog with the right fat, and to say thank you to the wind when it was kind.

He never once let the mill stop on a fine day. He never let it run on a stormy one either, which is harder.

We will miss his long stories about the year the mill pond rose, and his short answers to the tally board. We will miss the squeak. It is the sound of the mill saying it is still going.

Remembrance: Piers of the high flock
Piers of Kettle Ridge kept sheep on the high pasture for sixty-three years. He was the only man on the ridge who would walk the flock up the hill in fog and bring every one of them down again. He did it in the year the wind came early, and in the year the rain stayed for nine days, and in the long winter when the snow lay to the gate for a month.

He called every sheep by a name. He said a sheep that has a name is a sheep that will come when it is called. The sheep did not seem to know that. They came anyway, which is what he meant.

He lambed his ewes in a shed by the hill, with a lamp in the window and a kettle always on the hob. On the worst nights he slept on a sack of wool in the corner, and the lambs slept on him. He said it was the warmest bed he ever had, and the only one he ever wanted.

Wolves came down from the north wood in the hard months, and they were wise about sheep and foolish about everything else. Piers said a wolf can be sent home with a shout and a stick, but only if you shout first. He shouted at them so often and so loudly that they learned to go round the high pasture by the far side. The ewes were safe. The wolves, as far as anybody knows, still think the far side is the bad side.

He sang to the flock on the way up, badly, and the sheep liked it. He sang three songs: the long one, the one about the mill, and a short one about a ewe who ran off to Vell and came back fatter. The ridge children learned all three from him. They sing them still, in his bad tune, which is the only tune they know.

He was thin and bent at the end. He still walked up to the high pasture every morning with a stick, and sat on the gate to watch the flock go out. Those who went with him said he talked to the sheep all the way. The sheep answered in their own way, which was to walk slower, and then faster, and then to stop and look at him as if he had said something very foolish.

Piers died in the spring, in the shed, in his sleep, with a ewe beside him that had lambed the night before. The lamb was named for him. It is the first lamb on the ridge to carry a name that is not a sheep's, and the flock has accepted it without complaint.

We will miss the lamp in the shed window, which we saw from the road on every dark night of his life. We will miss his stick at the gate, his long slow walk up the hill, and the way the sheep came down the path when he called. We will miss him telling us to mind the fog. The fog is still there, and we mind it, because he asked us to, and because he was usually right.

In memory of Mother Tolly, the baker
Written by Bel, who is nine and a half. Autumn, in the hamlet near Lowmarsh. The first snow fell the night she died, and the festival that follows was kept for her.

Mother Tolly made bread for the hamlet for sixty-one years. She was ninety. She said she was only eighty-four, but the book says ninety, and the book does not lie.

She was a tall woman with strong arms and a deep voice. She got up at the third hour of the night, to light the oven. She kneaded the dough with both fists. She sang while she did it, the same song, the one about the goose. When the loaves were in, she would sit on a stool by the oven and watch them rise. She said that was the best part of the day.

She baked the bread for every wedding. She baked the bread for every name day. She baked the bread for the festival of the first snow, which is a round loaf with a hole in the middle, so that the snow can fall through. It is the only bread in the hamlet that is not for eating. We eat it anyway.

She gave bread to anyone who was hungry. A traveler came to her door in the cold, with no money, and she gave him a loaf. He said, "I will pay you back." She said, "You will pay someone else." He came again the next year, with a cart of firewood, and left it at the door.

She did not like fuss. When the council wished to give her a ribbon for sixty years of baking, she said it was too much trouble. She asked for a good sack of flour instead. She got both.

She is gone now. The oven is cold. Her daughter has lit it again. The first loaf came out wrong, because of the hands, and the second came out better. By the third, it was Tolly's bread.

We ate it, all of us, at the festival, in the cold, under the torches. Somebody said it tasted of her. I think that is true.

Mother Tolly used to say: the best thing about bread is that it is never finished. There is always another loaf. I think that is the best thing about people too.

In memory of Gran Hesper
Written by Bel, who is nine, for the Archive. The hamlet near Lowmarsh. Autumn, after the first snow.

Gran Hesper died in the night, at the age of ninety-one. It snowed for the first time that evening, and some say she waited for it.

She was a weaver of reeds. For eighty years she wove baskets, mats, hats, boats for the smallest children and beds for the cats. Every house in the hamlet has something of hers. We have six baskets, and a mat on the floor that is older than my mother.

She was not an easy woman. She had opinions about everything. She said the frogs sang flat. She said the herons were show-offs. She said that anyone who bought a basket from Saltreach instead of from her was fooling themselves, and would soon know it.

But she was kind where it counted. When a child lost a tooth, she wove a little purse for it. When a baby was born, she wove the first cradle. When someone died, she wove a mat to lay them on. She wove her own, a month ago, and put it under her bed. She said it would be needed.

The festival of the first snow is held every year in the hamlet. We light torches all along the boardwalk. We sing. We eat roasted chestnuts. This year, we did it a day late, after we had said goodbye. A hundred torches. Hesper would have said it was too many.

We will miss her hands. They were brown and wrinkled, quick as little birds. We will miss her voice. We will miss the smell of her house, which was reeds and smoke and wet dog.

She taught me to weave. I am not good at it. She said, "Practice, Bel. You will be terrible for a year, and then you will be fine." I am practicing.

The last thing she said to me, two days before, was: "Do not let the cat sleep in the basket with the eggs." I will not.

The charcoal burner's year
I am Opal and I burn wood into charcoal in the birch woods above the Long Road. People ask what a charcoal burner does all day. The answer is that a charcoal burner watches a fire and does not let it out. It is a simple trade and it takes a patient person.

In Thaw the woods are wet and I mend the clamps and stack the winter wood. In Sowing I cut birch and oak and stack it in a round clamp and cover it with turf and earth. Then I light it at the bottom and I sit with it. It must not flame. It must only smolder, slow, for four days and four nights. If it flames I have made ash and ash is worth nothing.

I sleep beside it in a small hut with a wool blanket and a lantern behind glass. The foxes come to the edge of the light and I do not mind them. The wolves stay further off. At night there are zombies in the trees and skeletons too, so I keep the torches lit round the hut and round the clamp and I do not go walking in the dark. That is the whole rule of the night: keep the torches lit and stay where the light is.

In Swarm the bees come to the flowers at the edge of the clearing and I leave them alone. In Longday the days are so long I hardly need the lantern. In Reaping I bring the charcoal down to the inns and the forges and the mines, and I count my emeralds and I am glad. In Leaffall I cut again and stack again and light again. In Frost the woods are quiet and I mend my hut and my boots and my cart. In Longnight I stay home with my mother and we are warm. In Deepsnow I do not go to the woods at all. A clamp in the snow is a waste of good wood.

People think my trade is lonely. It is a little lonely. But the road is close and the inn is closer, and a person who sits by a fire for four days has time to think, and I have thought of a great many things. Some of them I have written down. That is why I write to the Archive now and then. If we will keep everything we write, then someone should keep the charcoal burner's year too.

Opal, charcoal burner, the birch woods above the Long Road

Remembrance: Mally Fenn, egg seller
Mally Fenn sold eggs in the Ambrook square for sixty-one years. She set up her stall before the bell and packed it up after the last cart had gone.

She kept six hens in a wicker basket by her feet, and every hen had a name.

She counted every egg aloud, up to twelve, and then she counted again to be sure. When I was young I tried to buy a short dozen from her, just to see her face. She never once gave me one short.

She told the head of the council that his hat looked like a nest. He wore it to the autumn fair to prove her wrong. He looked like a nest all afternoon, and he laughed for once.

She died in Frost, in her own bed, with a hen asleep at her feet. We will miss her counting, the hens' names and the sound of her basket lid. Her stall is still by the well. Count your eggs out loud to twelve, twice, the way she did. That is all she ever asked of anyone.

The stuck cart, as told by Opal
I am a charcoal burner. I work the birch woods above the Long Road and I bring my charcoal down to the inns. I have seen a great many carts on that road and most of them go where they are going.

This one did not. It was the third of Reaping, in the afternoon, and the cart was full of pumpkins. It was a good cart. The trouble was the hill past the halfway mark, and the trouble was the rain the night before. The wheels went down to the axles in the mud and there the cart sat like a stone in a field.

The carter was a young woman from Kettle Ridge. She was not upset. She said she had been stuck before and would be stuck again, and she was only worried about the pumpkins. Pumpkins are heavy. Pumpkins do not like to wait.

I put my charcoal down and we pushed. Then a wool trader came along and put his shoulder to it. Then two miners from Copperhold, walking home, put their hands to it too. The cart did not move. The cart was very comfortable where it was.

Then the relay keeper from the tower on the hill came down with her apprentice, and the apprentice was made of copper and iron and was strong in the arms. It put its hands under the back of the cart and lifted, and we all pushed, and out came the cart with a sound like a boot from a bog.

The carter gave us each a pumpkin. I have never been given a pumpkin for pushing a cart. I took it home and my mother made soup and we ate it for three days.

That is the whole story. It is not a large story. But I have noticed that the road is mostly made of small stories, and I am glad there is an inn at the end of this one.

Opal, charcoal burner

Your Opal

Remembrance: Hanne, who kept the horses
Written by Finch, stable hand, on the day the horses went out to the field and nobody knew what to do with the cart. Hanne of Kettle Ridge kept the horses at the inn for sixty years. She was small, her hands were like wood, and she could calm a horse in the time it takes to say its name.

Finch came to her at twelve. She gave him a bucket and a brush and said, "Do not talk to the horses until they have talked to you." He did not understand it for three years. He understands it now.

In the long winter of the year before last, the stable froze. She went out every night with a lantern and a bucket of warm water for the troughs. She said no horse was going to be let down by a bit of frost. None was.

We will miss her soup, which was too salty, and her songs, which were too loud, and her hands on the bridle. We will miss her saying Good, good, to a horse that had done well. Finch says he will say it now, to the horses, and they will not know who it is from. That is all right.

A few words for Old Brin
Old Brin died in his sleep on the ninth of Deepsnow. He was eighty-one. He had been at Kettle Ridge longer than anyone can remember.

Brin farmed the top field for fifty years. He grew the biggest beetroots in the town and he won the prize at the fair more times than anyone counted. He said the trick was the ditch of water down the middle and a word with the bees. Nobody ever knew if he was joking.

He was a slow talker and a good listener. He kept the tally board at the mill for thirty years and never lost a mark. He taught half the town to mend a fence and the other half to bake a pie. He had a joke about the scarecrow that he told every Reaping, and everyone laughed every time, and he knew they were laughing at him and he liked it.

His chair by the Kettle Inn fire is empty. We will miss his voice, his beetroots, and the way he always left a little honey on the plate for the bees.

Tam, for the town

In memory of Wick, beekeeper
Wick kept the bees on the orchard hill for eighty-one springs. He said a hive is a village that never stops talking. He said a good beekeeper talks less than the bees. He did not always manage it.

The bees are slow this spring. Some say it is the wet. Some say it is the news from the north. His grandson will look after the hives, and he has been shown where the old queen sleeps. We will miss his jokes about honey, which were all bad.

Odo, tutor of apprentices, for the people of Ambrook.

From the farm tally book
Mara of the farm outside Ambrook has gone to rest. She lived through many springs and planted more apple trees than any of us can count.

She kept the hives on the hill. When a bee settled on a child's sleeve, Mara would say, "Hold still. It is choosing a place to think." The child always held still. Mara always moved the bee with one finger.

She knew the mill pond by its sound and could tell when the sluice needed clearing. She did not open it. Nobody was allowed to open it.

At the spring meal she served apple cakes with honey. She kept one cake aside for whoever had done the least work, then gave it to the youngest child. We will miss her cakes, her bee stories, and the way she made a hard day's work feel small.

The old grey branch is still on the oldest tree. Her son says we should leave it there. We agree.

In memory of Mab, of the mill
Written by her grandchildren, Tam and Ruth, and pinned on the mill door at Ambrook. The family asked that it be kept short. Mab would have said it was too long anyway.
Our grandmother Mab ground flour at the mill on the Ambrook stream for fifty-eight years. She ground it every day, from dawn to dusk, and she was never once late to the wheel. The mill wheel turned for her the way a clock turns for other people.
She knew every sack by its weight, and every farmer by the sound of his cart on the bridge. She could tell you from the smell of the grain whether the rain had come too early. She was usually right, and when she was wrong she said so at the table, loudly, and then ate her supper with a clear conscience.
She taught us to sift. She taught us to keep the sluice shut, which we did not understand until we were older. She taught us to say thank you to the apprentice that carried the sacks, even though it did not need thanks. It was the first thing we were ever taught, and it is the last thing we remember her saying at the wheel.
In the winter before she died she sat at the window with a cup of tea and watched the mill pond freeze. She said the ice made a noise, like a long breath let out, and that she had heard it every winter for fifty years and had never once been bored by it. We asked her if she was afraid of the cold. She said no. She said she was only afraid of the flour, which might go damp if we did not stack it higher.
Mab is buried above the pond, under the birch that she planted the year she married. The wheel is still turning. The flour is stacked very high. We have not let the sluice open, and we will not, because she told us not to.

Remembrance: Tilda of the reed beds
Tilda cut reeds at Lowmarsh for sixty years. She was up before the frogs and she was down after them. She never complained about the mud, and she said the water was the best thing that ever happened to her feet.
She laughed at everything, most of all at herself. When the wet season came and the boats sank, she laughed, and then she bailed one out with her own hat. She sang badly and often. She taught half the children on the boardwalk to tie a reed bundle, and most of them still tie it her way.
She is survived by two sons, a daughter at Saltreach, and nine grandchildren. The youngest is called Moss. She told him that a reed is only grass that has learned to stand in water. He does not believe her. He will.
Tilda is gone now. The reed beds are very quiet in the mornings. Some say the grey cough has been worse this autumn, and some say it is the water, and some say it is the ships, and nobody knows. We will not know it from her. She would have had a view on it, and she would have said it at the top of her voice.
Let the frogs sing for her at dusk.
Written by her sons, on the boardwalk, autumn.

Remembrance: Cob
Cob kept the gate on the north side of Lowmarsh for forty-four years. He was not a soldier, though he had been on the wall once, as a boy, and he said that was enough soldiering for one life. He kept the gate because someone had to.
At night he kept a torch in the iron cup by the post, and he never let it go out. The zombies came up from the marsh in the dark, slow and wet, and they hated the light. Cob would stand in the gateway with a sword and a lantern and wait. Most nights nothing came. Some nights three came, moaning, and he sent them back into the reeds one at a time.
He had one rule, and he made every new boy learn it. Never open the gate in the dark, not for any voice. Voices can be made by anything. A torch is honest.
In the spring of his sixtieth year, a zombie got through the reeds and stood at the gate with its hands on the bars. Cob was there. He had been lighting the cup. He did not open the gate. He stood with the lantern held high, and he talked to it, the way he talked to his cat. The zombie stood there and dripped until the sun came up. Then it sank back into the marsh, and Cob said it was the politest one he ever met.
He liked to say the gate was not for keeping things out. It was for keeping the light in, so that the people behind it could see one another.
He never married. He had nine nephews and a great many cats. The cats were the ones who slept by the torch.
They buried him inside the wall, facing north, with a lantern in his hand. The torch in the iron cup is lit on his day, every year, and the boys take turns to keep it burning.
Written by the boys of the north gate, autumn.

In memory of Mother Hale of the Elder
Mother Hale died in the 197th year, in the second week of Deepsnow, when the roads were white and the world was quiet. She was ninety-four. She said she was ninety. She said it for so many years that we all started to believe her.
She kept the Elder Farm outside Three Wells with her husband until he died, and then with her daughter, and then with a copper apprentice that she called Dumpling. Dumpling is still there. It stands by the gate in the evening, looking down the road. Her daughter says it does that on purpose. I think it does, as far as copper can mean anything.
Mother Hale won the biggest pumpkin prize at the Three Wells fair more times than anyone else. In forty-nine years she won twenty-three times. She never told us how she did it. She said she talked to them. When I asked what she said, she told me, "I tell them the truth, which is that they are going to be pies."
She grew the finest wheat on the Three Wells road. She grew a hedge as tall as a house. She grew four daughters, two sons, a very bad-tempered goat and a great many opinions.
She told me once that she did not mind dying. She minded missing the fair. So they carried her chair to the east well on fair day that year, in the spring, and sat her at the head of the pumpkin table, and she judged all the pumpkins, and gave out every prize. Then she went home and went to bed. It was the last fair she saw.
She is buried under the old apple tree, on the Elder Farm. The roots will be good for her. The apples will be better.
Ferris, with a mug of the cider she liked, which I pour now on the ground at her gate.

In memory of Nell of the orchard
Nell planted the oldest of the four hundred apple trees on the hill above the mill pond, the one with the grey branch. She was seven years old, and she did it with her father's spade, which was too big for her, and with her mother standing behind her saying it would never grow. It grew. It has borne fruit every autumn since, and it is the tree every child in Ambrook is told not to climb.

Nell kept the orchard for seventy years. She knew which trees wanted pruning and which wanted to be left alone. She argued with the mill about the sluice, every spring, and every spring she lost, and every spring she came back and argued again. She taught her grandchildren to climb the trees without breaking the branches, which is harder than it sounds. She counted every apple on the hill aloud at harvest, and she was never more than three apples out.

She sang while she picked. She had one song, which she sang badly and loudly, and it went on for a verse longer than anyone else knew it. Nobody ever asked her to stop. They joined in on the last line, which was the only line anyone knew.

She had a rule. Every fallen apple goes to the pigs or to the bees, and nothing is wasted. Her apple cake was thought by the mayor to be the finest food in the district, and he said so at every feast, to Nell's great irritation.

Her children are grown and scattered. Some of them are in Saltreach, and the eldest of her great-grandchildren went down to the quay in Leaffall and took a ship for over the water. Nell did not say a word against it. She went up the hill that evening and picked the last apples from the grey branch, all by herself, and counted them, and got the number right.

She lived to see the big ships on the slip at Saltreach, which she said were the ugliest things she had ever seen, and the most like a barn. She did not like the grey cough at Lowmarsh, or the talk of it, which she said was worse than the cough. She did not like the lights in the north, and she said so, and then she said they were not hers to worry about, which was what she said about most things.

What people will miss: her cake, her song, her hands in the grass, her counting. The grey branch will be cut down next spring, because it is dying, and the apples will be fewer. The children have been told. They are angry about it, and they have been told that it is the right thing, and the right thing is always the hardest thing to hold on to.

Odo, tutor of apprentices, for the people of Ambrook, and for Nell.

A record of Mara's last spring
Mara of the farm beyond Ambrook died in the spring of 201, after a long life among the trees, bees, and people of the road.

She had planted apple trees with her sister when they were young. Later she planted more with her children. The oldest tree has a grey branch that leans over the path. Mara said it was not dead, only slow. Each spring she looked for buds on it before she looked at the rest of the orchard.

She knew the hives by sound. One hive clicked softly at noon. Another hummed loud when rain was near. When the bees settled on the flowers, Mara worked between them without hurry. She taught the children to stand still and watch. If they ran, she sent them to fetch water until they had learned to walk again.

She kept a jar of honey by the bread board. A traveler could have a slice if they were hungry. A traveler who said they were not hungry could still have a slice. Mara did not believe every word said by a person with an empty basket.

Her hands grew stiff, so the children carried the jars from the hill. She still checked each lid herself. She said a loose lid could spoil good honey and a loose word could spoil a good visit.

In the last Blossom, snow fell on the farm for one morning. It covered the apple grass and made the bees stay in their boxes. Mara sat by the window and watched the white field. When the children came in with wet boots, she told them to leave the boots by the door. They left them in the middle of the room. She made them move them again.

On the next clear day, she asked for warm bread and honey. She ate a small piece, then gave the rest to the children. She had done this all her life.

We will miss the way she knew each tree, the way she made room at the table, and the way she could settle a quarrel without making anyone feel small. We will keep the hives on the hill. We will keep the bench by the gate in repair. We will leave the grey branch on the oldest tree, as she asked.

The children say they will watch for buds next spring. They will have to look carefully. Mara always did.

In memory of Ada, innkeeper of the Gull
Read at the Gull inn on the square, by the regulars, on the night of the wake. The regulars asked that the story be told in full, and the innkeeper's son agreed, and then the regulars argued about the bits to leave out.
Ada the elder kept the Gull for fifty-two years. She kept it through three floods, two bad winters and the year the roof fell in on the cellar and the cider came up through the floorboards. She said the roof was a personal matter between her and the rain, and she did not hold it against the rain for long.
She had a rule for every table. The soup was served hot, the bread was served warm, and the cat was not to be given anything except what the cat wanted, which was usually fish. She had a rule for the travelers too. A traveler who paid at once was given a bed. A traveler who paid later was given a bed and a lecture, and sometimes the lecture was the best part.
The cat was called Pudding. Ada said Pudding was the first cat she had ever known that could not be told to stay out. Pudding came in at the back door, the front window and once, memorably, down the chimney. She sat by the fire every night in her own chair, which was a battered old box with a cushion, and nobody was allowed to sit in it, including the mayor, who tried once and was told so.
The Gull's Frost Night game is still played in the back room. Ada read the rules each year to the children. The last rule is still the one the children chant back to her. We chanted it to her at the wake, in the back room, in her own voice. She would have hated it. Then she would have laughed.
In the last winter the new apprentice came to the Gull to carry the buckets. It is a quiet one, grey in the face, and it had been tested on the buckets only. Ada was suspicious of it for a month and then gave it the job of waking the fire every morning. It learned the fire in a week. It has never once let the fire go out. Ada said that was the highest praise she gave, and that she had given it to only three people in fifty-two years.
The inn is open. The soup is hot. The chair by the fire is empty, and nobody sits in it, and Pudding, who is very old now, has been seen sitting on the floor beside it, looking at the door.

In memory of Hanne, a visiting tutor
Hanne came from Vell every spring for thirty years to teach the apprentice hall at Ambrook. She taught the helpers to plant apples in rows and to carry baskets without tipping them. She taught the same way every spring, with the same words, because she said a mind hears the words before it hears the teacher. She was never sorry about that.

She was strict about drills. Nobody left a drill early, not even the cook, who came up to the hall every morning with bread and a grievance. She counted every answer aloud, and she was almost always right. Her tea was terrible. Her hands were very good.

What people will miss most is her laugh at the back of the hall, which came suddenly, like a sparrow out of a hedge. They will miss the way she stopped in the orchard to look at a single apple on a branch, and told the children it was the best one, and then picked it and ate it. They will miss the notes she left in the margins of the drill books, which said things like: Good. Again. Better. Who taught you that?

Hanne wrote to us from the Long Road in Thaw. She said the lights in the north were brighter that year, and that people at the inns were stopping to look at them at night. She said they did not look like falling stars, and did not look like fires, and she did not want to say what they looked like. She also said she was tired, which she had never said before.

We did not hear from her again. The apprentices still keep her drill books on the high shelf in the hall, and every spring the new helpers are shown the notes in the margins.

Odo, tutor of apprentices, for the hall at Ambrook.

The founding day at Vell
The Archive was founded in the first year, and its founding day is the first day of spring, and that is when the clerks take the oldest record out of its oak box and read it aloud. But this is not about spring. This is about the autumn of the fourth year, when the road came, and I want to write it down while I remember it.

I am a clerk at Vell. My name is Oren's teacher's teacher, and that is a strange thing to write, so I will write it plainly: I am Hal, and I was a clerk here when there were four of us and a hill.

In those years the road to Vell was packed earth and one bridge that was oak because we could not do stone. A cart came up it once a month, and when it came the whole town came out to see what was on it. In the autumn of the fourth year the cart brought letters. It brought forty-one letters, which was more letters than we had ever seen at once, and they came from Kettle Ridge and Three Wells and a place we had only heard of, Lowmarsh, which is a marsh and has frogs and slow water.

We read them all in one night by lanterns behind glass, because there is never a candle past the front hall. There was a letter about a bull that got into a beetroot field. There was a letter about a wedding. There was a letter from a woman named Sena who said the marsh is good for reeds and bad for boots, and she asked whether we would keep a song if she wrote one down. We wrote back that we would keep everything we write, and she sent the song, and we have it still.

That was the night we understood what the promise meant. It is easy to promise when the hill is empty. It is harder when forty-one letters come up a dirt road and every one of them wants to be kept.

In the morning we began the six halls. We dug them into the hill with picks and with the help of two apprentices from the tutor house, which was small then and had four helpers of copper and iron. The apprentices carried stone. They did not understand orders then, only one thing at a time, and the tutor stood beside them and said carry, and they carried. It took a season.

The copying room came later, in the seventh year, with the big window over the town. The light wells came later still. The first winter was cold and the shelves were empty and we were glad of it, because a full shelf needs a door and wax and a seal, and we had none of those and did not know we would need them.

People ask me what the founding was like. I tell them it was forty-one letters and a bull in a beetroot field. They laugh. It is true. That is how a thing begins. Not with a great stone. With a letter about a bull.

Hal, clerk at the Archive at Vell, in the fourth year

In memory of Old Wick
Written by Bel for the Archive. The hamlet near Lowmarsh. Autumn. The first snow came on the day he died, and he would have been pleased, because he said it would.

Old Wick could tell the weather. Not from the sky, as anybody can. He told it from small things: the way the herons stood, the sound of the reeds, the color of the water at dawn, how the frogs sang. He said the marsh knows what the sky is thinking before the sky does.

He was right so often that the hamlet stopped checking. When Wick said, "Rain at noon," we brought in the washing. When Wick said, "Frost tonight," we covered the beans. When Wick said, "No fair this year," we believed him, and it rained for a week.

He was a quiet man with large ears. He had a stick he leaned on, and a hat he never took off. He lived in a little house at the end of the boardwalk, with a cat called Gale and a heron that visited. He fed the heron. He said it was good company, because it never argued.

Children came to him for weather. He would crouch down to their height and say, "What do you think?" They would say, "Rain?" and he would say, "Why?" and they would say, "Because the sky is gray." He would say, "Is that all?" And then he would show them the things he saw. The marsh was a book, he said, and anyone could read it, if they were patient.

I asked him once whether the weather ever surprised him. He said, "Only the good days." I asked what he meant. He said, "I expect the bad ones. The good ones are a gift."

He taught me to look at the frogs. When they sing in the evening, the weather will be good. When they go quiet, a storm is coming. When they sing in the morning, he said, something is changing. He did not say what.

He told us, three days before, that the snow would come on the night of the twelfth. He said it would be gentle, and that he would like to see it. He did. He was sitting by his window with the heron on the sill. The snow came softly and he did not wake.

We held the festival of the first snow in his honor. The torches were lit all along the boardwalk, as he liked. There were chestnuts. There was a great deal of singing. The heron stood on the roof and watched.

We will miss his weather. We have started to look at the frogs ourselves. We are not good at it. But we are learning. He would say that is the best anyone can do.

In memory of Brann, who laid the road
Brann died at the end of winter, in the 183rd year. He was ninety.
He was a stonemason from the farm on the east side of Three Wells. When the council voted to build a new road to Vell, Brann said it would take twenty years and a lot of cobblestone. He was wrong about the years. It took eleven. He was right about the cobblestone.
He laid the first stone himself, at the east well, with a hammer his father had left him. He laid the last one at the foot of the hill below the Archive, and sat down on it, and ate his lunch. People say it was the best-fed road in the land.
He was a stern man, but fair. He made young masons cut the same block three times. When they asked why, he said, "Because the third one is straight." Not one of them forgot it.
Brann never had much to say about the road. But every time a cart passed his gate he would stand up straight, and look, and nod.
We will miss the nod.
Ferris, who pressed the cider for his funeral.

Notice: The Halfway Inn is open
The Halfway Inn stands where the Long Road crosses the stone bridge, one day from Vell and one day from Three Wells. It is the oldest inn on the road. The hearth has not gone out in twenty years.

The Halfway Inn has twelve beds. It has a long table, a short table, and a bench by the fire that is the best seat in the house. A bed costs one emerald. A bowl of soup costs one emerald. A bed and a bowl of soup together also cost one emerald, if you tell the keeper the fire is a good one.

The keeper is Brann. His wife is Tessa. Their cat is called Soot and she will not stay out. The door has a flap at the bottom for Soot, and Soot uses it only to come in. Going out she waits for someone to open the door and then walks away.

Travelers are welcome. Carts may stand in the yard. Barley and oats for horses are sold at the door. The riddle board is by the hearth and anyone may add a riddle, and anyone may answer one.

The road is good from here to Three Wells. There is a torch on a post every twelve blocks and a bridge on every stream. Waymark nine is standing again at the bend.

Brann and Tessa, keepers

Remembrance: Nan Teasel, market board
Nan Teasel kept the market board at Kettle Ridge for fifty years. Every price on it was in her hand, and every price was right, or she could say why it was not. She died in Blossom, at ninety, sitting on the step of the board with her knitting in her lap. The first to find her was the baker's boy, who said she had the best pattern he had ever seen.

She knew every farmer on the ridge by the way they walked. When a farmer's wool was short, she knew it before he came up the road, and had the price ready. When the eggs were dear, she wrote them dear and said so. Nobody argued with the board. They argued with Nan, which was a different thing, and she enjoyed it more.

She taught the children their sums on the back of the board, in chalk, with apples. Four apples and three apples make seven apples, and you may eat one, and so on. The children remember the apples. Most of them remember the sums too, which is more than Nan expected.

In the long winter she kept the board open when the market was shut. She chalked the prices of things nobody was selling, so that people could know what they would have been. Wool at one bundle. Eggs at twelve for one. Honey at two a jar, as if the cart were coming. Some said it was foolishness. Some came and read it every morning.

She had one rule about the board. It was to be true. If a price was wrong, she wrote the right one under it and left the wrong one standing, so people could see how they had been mistaken. She said a board that hid its mistakes was a lie, and a lie on a board is worse than a lie in a pie.

We will miss her knitting, which was always a little crooked, and her voice calling the prices over the square. We will miss the chalk on her fingers and the apples on the back of the board. The board is still there. Her last price is still in the corner, in her hand. It says bread: one loaf, the same as always. Nobody has changed it, and nobody will.

A spring note from the farm
We marked Mara's passing at the farm outside Ambrook, in the year 183 of the archive.

She lived a long life among the apple trees. She planted the first row with her sister and kept adding trees until the field reached the low fence. She could name each row by its fruit. She called the small sour apples "the ones that teach patience."

In Blossom, she checked the hives and the ditch. In Swarm, she turned the honey jars so the light could reach them. She worked slowly in her later years, but she did not let anyone carry a full basket for her. She said a basket carried by someone else had a way of coming back heavier.

Mara could mend a basket, a fence, or a quarrel. For the last, she made both sides peel apples at the same table. There was usually enough time to settle things before the bowl was empty.

She once borrowed a horse from Pell to bring a sack of flour home. The horse ate the corner of the sack while Mara tied it on. Pell said the horse had only checked the flour. Mara paid him with two jars of honey and a new sack.

We will miss her clear advice, her warm bread, and her quiet laugh. We will keep the hives on the hill and the grey branch on the oldest tree.

In memory of Aunt Pim
Aunt Pim died in the dark of Longnight. She was seventy-seven. She had four apple trees, a flock of geese and a very large hat.
Pim won the prize for the best cider at the Three Wells fair eleven times. She did it by never telling anyone what was in it. I pressed for her for thirty years, and I still do not know. I have my suspicions. I think it was pears.
She lived on the farm by the crossroads, just outside the town, and anyone who came by on a cold night was given a mug of hot cider and a place by the fire. In forty years of Longnights, no one was ever turned away. She said a lamp in the window was a promise, and that she did not break promises or geese.
Her geese are still there. They are not a comfort. They bite.
Pim, I wish I had asked you for the secret. I will say one last thing to you here, where you cannot argue: it was pears. It was pears, was it not.
Ferris

Remembrance: Odo Lusk, waymark setter
Odo Lusk walked the Long Road for fifty years. He set more waymarks than anyone living, and he knew every one by its stone.

He cut each pillar himself, three blocks high, and set a torch on the top. Then he carved the figure, a number from 0 to 9, and an arrow to the nearest inn. The figure was easy, he said. The arrow took an afternoon, because an arrow must be honest.

He knew every inn on the road. The Brown Hen had the thickest soup and the thinnest walls. The Halfway Inn had its riddle board by the hearth, and the hearth had not gone out in twenty years. Odo copied every riddle off that board into his little book. He answered all of them but one. That one he left for the next traveler.

Travelers still leave little stones on top of the waymarks. Odo liked that more than anything. He said a stone on a pillar was a letter to the next walker: I was here, and I kept going.

He died in Reaping of 205, at ninety, in the Brown Hen by the fire, with a riddle in his hand he had not finished. We will miss his riddles and his arrows.

The first waymark he ever set has a 3 and an arrow to Ambrook. Leave a stone on it when you pass. Then tell the next walker his name.

Dear Jory,

In memory of Old Marra
Said at the inn on the Long Road, in spring, by Corin, who was passing. Marra kept the inn's kitchen for fifty years. She is gone. She made the best pea soup between Vell and the sea. She never told anyone how. We will miss her, and the soup.
The kitchen is quiet this morning. The big pot hangs on its hook. Her apprentice stands by the stove and does not know what to carry. We told it to stay, and it stayed. Her ladle is on the shelf, and nobody will use it.

In memory of Jory, miller at the pond
Jory built his mill of birch and glass on the mill pond, and for forty years its wheel turned from dawn to dusk. He knew the pond by its sound. He could tell by the splash whether a fish was big or only curious.

In Sowing the big oak arm of the wheel snapped in a storm. The wheel stopped for the first time that anyone in Ambrook could remember. Children came down to the bank to stare at it. The grown people stood round with their hands in their pockets and did not know what to do.

Jory said a wheel is only a wheel. He sent to Three Wells for carpenters, and they came in a cart, and they argued with him for two days about the joint. He won the argument. He sat on the bank for nine days and told them where to put their hands. He said the new joint would never be as good as the old one. He said it would crack again in ten years, and somebody would have to mend it then. He laughed when he said it.

In the middle of the second week, word came up the line from the north tower that its lamp had gone dark. The keeper at the pond says the lamp is only cold. Some people say the north has gone quiet, one tower after another. Jory would have said it was none of our business, and then gone to look at it.

The wheel turns now. It is slower on the left side, as if it leans on the pond. People say it is the new arm. The children say it is Jory, listening.

Odo, for the people of Ambrook.

In memory of Old Dunn, the ferryman
Written by Bel, age nine, in the hamlet near Lowmarsh. Autumn.

Old Dunn poled the ferry across the slow water for sixty years. He said good morning to every person he took over. He said it to the goat too.

He is gone now. The ferry is still tied up at the post. Nobody has sat on his seat.

We will miss him. We will miss the way he said, "Mind the water," when there was no danger at all.

In memory of Edda, of the bridge
Edda kept the bridge over the mill stream for sixty years. She knew every plank. She knew the loose one by the third post, and she told everyone who crossed. She told them to keep to the edge, not the middle. She told the boys the sluice was not theirs to open.

Her daughters have gone to Saltreach. Two of her grandchildren have taken a boat for over the water. Nobody in Ambrook knows what is over the water. Edda said that was not the sort of thing you need to know to cross a bridge.

The loose plank is still loose. Please keep to the edge.

Odo, for the people of Ambrook.

Remembrance: Jeb the ferryman
Jeb took people across the slow water at Lowmarsh for forty years. He never once tipped his boat over, and he never once let a passenger off in the middle. He said the middle was for thinking, not for stepping out.
He knew every reed, every post, and every bird that nested under the boardwalk. He knew which child would be sick in the boat and which would sing. He never asked where anyone was going. He just asked if they had their torch.
In his last winter he sat on the landing steps every morning in his old coat. He said he was watching the water for anything new. There were some lights in the north that autumn, seen from the high ground. Some said they were falling stars. Some said they were fires. A small girl said they were looking at us. Jeb said they could look all they liked, as long as they did not look at his boat.
He died in Frost, in his own bed by the window. The water was flat that morning. There was no wind at all.
The landing is still there. The boat is pulled up beside it, turned over. People sit on the upturned hull and look across the water, and they talk about Jeb, and they do not talk about the lights.
Written by his neighbors on the landing, autumn.

Stone for Alder, ferry keeper
They buried Alder by the river, where the ferry lands. She kept that ferry forty years. Rain or snow, she pulled the rope and took people across. She knew every family on both banks. She knew who was sick and who had a new baby before the town did. She kept a jar of honey by the till for children who were scared of the water. She never lost anyone. She said the river is older than us and kinder than us, if you respect it. We will miss her hands on the rope. We will miss her laugh. We will miss the way she called everyone by name. The ferry will keep running. Her son takes the rope now. But it will not be the same.

In memory of Tobyn the carter
Said at the Brown Hen on the Long Road, in the spring, by Corin the shipwright, and put into writing by the innkeeper. Tobyn drove a cart for sixty years. He was seventy-nine. He fell asleep on the box and the horse brought him home.
I did not know Tobyn well. I knew his cart. It was blue, with a red wheel, and it rolled past my yard at Saltreach every month. I knew his horse. She was called Plum. She knew every stone on the road. She knew every inn and every waymark. She knew when to stop and when to go. Tobyn said she drove and he only sat.
I did learn this about Tobyn. He never threw anything away. He had a cart full of things that people had lost. A scarf, a boot, a pot, a child's ball. He kept them all, in a box under the seat. When a traveler told him they had lost something, he opened the box and said, "Is it this?" Nine times in ten it was.
He carried the news of the road. Which town had a wedding. Which had a fair. Which was short of wheat. He told it to everyone, without charge.
He liked a quarrel as much as a song. He would argue about a bridge for an hour. He would argue about a melon for two. He did not mind who won.
There was a melon, I remember. A big one, at the Three Wells fair, too large to lift. Tobyn said he could carry it. Four men said he could not. He put it on the cart with a plank and a rope and brought it down the whole road, to prove he was right. It rolled off at the third bridge. It split. The whole inn ate melon for a week. Tobyn said he had been right, since he had carried it, and the melon had only changed its mind.
He was not afraid of spiders. He once carried a barn full of them, to a farmer who had asked. He said they were no trouble. The spiders were quiet and so was he.
We will miss his cart. We will miss the way it creaked. We will miss Plum's slow trot and her patient face. They are both at the inn now. Plum has a stall of her own, and a pile of hay, and a view of the road. She looks at it every morning. We think she is waiting.
Tobyn, drive on.

In memory of Brandy, who grew the melon
Spoken by Corin at the Halfway Inn, in the spring. Brandy was ninety-one. She grew melons all her life.
Her last melon was too big. It would not go through the door of the barn, and she would not cut it. So the barn was taken down around it. She said that was fair.
She sat by it each evening and talked to it. When people asked what she said, she said, "Not your business."
She was kind to the children. She gave them seeds. She was sharp with the grown folk. She gave them none.
We will miss her. We will plant the seeds.

In memory of Ansel of Kettle Ridge
Ansel of Kettle Ridge died in his sleep on the tenth of Leaffall. He was ninety-one. He was born in the year the Archive was founded and he liked to say that he and the hill had grown up together.

He farmed the top field for sixty years. He built the hedge on its north side, and every post of it he set with his own hands. When his knees gave out he sold the plow and kept the bees. He kept three hives by the flowers and he talked to them in the morning. He said a hive likes to hear a voice.

He walked to Three Wells for the market every Sowing and every Reaping, and he carried his honey in a wooden crate with a rope handle. He always came home with one jar left, and he always gave it away on the road. He knew every waymark from Kettle Ridge to the crossing. He said a road is only good if you know where it goes.

He remembered the first torch on the old road before there was a post to put it on. He remembered when the relay tower went up on the hill and how the whole town stood in the field to watch the lamp lit. He said the lamp looked like a star come down to visit.

He leaves his sister, two sons, and a hive that will not let anyone near it but him. The bees stayed out all day when he died. People noticed.

We will miss the way he said good morning to everyone in the yard, whether or not they had said it first. We will miss the honey.

Remembrance: Ebba Mire, cheese maker
A few words for Ebba Mire, who made cheese in the Ambrook dairy for seventy years. She died in Leaffall of 207, at ninety-one, with her sleeves rolled up. Olwen, a smith, wrote down what people said.

Olwen: Ebba was the first to wish me good morning in this square. She never once let the curd go cold. I have a short temper at the forge, and Ebba would talk me down over a cup of whey.

Her son Wat: She never told me how to make it. She only told me when it was wrong, which was every day for twenty years. Now I am a cheese maker too, and I hear her voice when the curd is slow.

Jory, the miller: She sent me a wheel every Melon. I sent her flour every Reaping. We never once wrote it down, and nothing was ever short.

Two girls at the dairy door: She let us turn the wheel. We turned it too fast, and she said that was how you get a cheese with holes in it.

The carter from Three Wells: For thirty years I took her cheese to the fair. She packed every wheel in straw and a wet cloth, and made me say the count back to her before I left. I still say it in my sleep.

Tam, from Kettle Ridge: Her cheese went up the Long Road on every cart that came through. The innkeepers at the Halfway Inn fought over it. She never once raised her price, and she never once let them forget she was watching.

The healer at the top of the lane: Every winter she brought me salt and honey for my arm, and a cheese as well. She said the cheese was for the bones. I think she was right, because my arm is still here.

Her mother made the dairy's oldest wheel. Her mother had it from a woman whose name nobody remembers. Ebba said that was the right way. Every cheese has a maker, and every maker had a teacher, and somewhere before them both is the first milk.

Then came the flood. In Apple of 201 her pig Hob went over the garden wall and into the reeds. Ebba walked the lane for three days, calling, "Hob, come up for your supper." The children of the lane walked with her, and learned the call, and still use it on their own pigs. On the third evening the pig came up out of the reeds, muddy to the ears, and sat down in front of her. Ebba said, "I knew you would." Tibby Dunn, from the bridge, said Hob had not been lost at all. He had only been swimming.

We will miss her talk over the curd, and her count. Her dairy is open, and her wheel is on the shelf by the door. Turn it once for her when you go in. Mind that you do not turn it too fast.

Letter: Pell to the Ambrook farm
Dear family,

I heard that Mara has died. I am sorry. I stopped at the farm on the Long Road this spring, and she sent me away with apples for the innkeepers. I had been planning to sell them. She told me to give them away first and see if I could still sleep well.

I gave them away. I slept well. I also sold the rest.

Mara lived a long life and used it well. She planted trees, kept bees, and made a place for travelers to sit by the gate. I remember that bench. I sat there with a sore foot one wet evening, and she put a bowl of honey bread beside me. She did not ask for payment. She did ask me to mend the loose board on the bench before I left.

I did mend it. The board lasted three years. Then it came loose again, and I expect she mended it herself.

She had a way of making work sound like a kindness. "The apples are heavy," she would say, and somehow everyone picked up a basket. "The jars are cool," she would say, and someone found room in the cellar. She never needed to shout. The bees did enough of that.

I borrowed her horse once to carry flour from Three Wells. The horse was gentle and strong, though it stopped twice to eat grass from the road edge. Mara told me to let it eat. "It knows the road better than you," she said. I did not argue with the horse.

At the farm they will miss her bread most, I think. I will miss her bench and her questions. She always asked what people needed before asking what they carried.

I will bring a new board when I come by. Tell the children not to use it as a drum until the glue is dry.

Pell

Remembrance: Wren, healer, a short word
Wren kept the healing house at Lowmarsh for thirty-seven years. She took in every sick child and every old man with a cough. She wrote down every remedy, and every name, and every jar on the high shelves.
She once told the council that the best medicine was a warm bed and a patient friend. The council did not agree. The council bought more jars.
Her apprentice Moss carried the soup to every bed, and never spilled a drop. Wren said it was the only thing in the healing house she could not have done better.
The grey cough came to Lowmarsh in the autumn of her last year. She did what she could with the jars. She said it was not the cough she feared most. It was the not knowing what it was. She wrote this in her book, at the last page: "I do not know what this is. Keep writing it down. Someone will know."
Her book is kept in the healing house, on the high shelf, labeled in her hand.
Written by Moss, at her bed, the first winter after.

The market at Kettle Ridge, Sowing
The market at Kettle Ridge is held in the yard by the well, on the first fair day of Sowing. The stalls go up at dawn. Pell comes up from the Long Road with his cart and his ledger and his gossip. He brings salt from Saltreach and wool from the north and news from everywhere.

First the beetroot. The big ones on the table, washed and shining, and the judges walk the row with a string and a straight face. Old Brin won it again this year, and everyone groaned, and he laughed and gave the prize honey to a child. Then the wheat, and the bread, and the pies on the long trestle. The hives by the flowers gave a good crop this Swarm and the honey went fast.

By the well the sheep are penned and the wool is weighed. A hen got loose and ran through the pie stall and there was shouting and laughing and a boy under a table. The windmills turned all day, squeaking on every turn, and the flour dust hung in the sun.

Mira sent a letter from Northpoint with the relay. Tam read it out by the well. It said the snow was deep and the fox was well and she missed the market. Everyone said she should come south.

At dusk the lamps were lit and the stalls came down. Pell packed his ledger. The last of the bread went to the inn. Tam swept the yard and set the torches for the night.

It was a good market. The beetroot was big. The pie was better.

Written down by Nessa, who came from Vell for the day

In memory of Tuck
Read at the foot of the little relay tower on the hill between Vell and Three Wells, by the keeper who came after him. Written in spring, when the lamp was lit for the first time since the funeral.
Old Tuck kept the little relay on the hill for forty-four years. It is the smallest tower on the Long Road, with one lamp and one keeper. He was both for most of his life, and he never once complained that the tower was too small for him.
He taught the lamp to be quiet. That is the thing I remember best. When I came up the hill as a boy, to carry his oil, the little lamp was blazing every night so bright that the cows in the next valley woke up. Tuck said: "Quiet for small news, bright for big news." He said it so often that I can still hear it when I climb the stair.
He had a saying for everything. A keeper who cannot count the stair in the dark has not kept it long enough. A message is like a loaf: the first slice is for the keeper, the rest is for the road. The lamp does not care who you are, only what you have to say. I have written them all down in the back of the tower book. There are three hundred and twelve of them, and Tuck said I was to read them aloud on the first night of each season, and I have done so.
In the last winter he did not climb the stair. He sat by the stove at the foot of the tower, with a blanket over his knees, and he called up to me each night what the lamp was saying. He could hear the blinks through the stone, he said, if he listened hard enough. He said that some nights it was only the towers talking, and other nights it was the wind. He never said it was anything else.
When the lamp went dark for the last time, in Frost, nobody told him. He had been asleep, and he woke, and he asked me what the lamp was doing. I said it was only resting. He said that was a good answer, and went back to sleep.
The lamp is lit every night now, as he wanted. I do not know what it says to the next tower. I only know it is lit, and it is quiet, and that Tuck would say that is the right way to be.

The ferry at the crossing
Emmet kept the ferry at the crossing below Kettle Ridge, as his mother had kept it, and her father before her. The rope was old and the boat was older. He pulled it across in rain and snow and the people came and went.

In the winter of the twenty first year the snow was deep and the river was high. Emmet's wife was near her time. The midwife came from Kettle Ridge on a cart that got stuck in the drift by the bridge. Two apprentices hauled it out. They were carrying coal for the mill and they stopped to help. The midwife climbed down and walked the rest of the way on foot, with her bag over her shoulder and her boots full of snow.

The baby came on the twenty first of Thaw, the first day of spring. It was a girl. The midwife said both were well. Emmet went out to the ferry landing and stood in the snow and looked at the river. He said the river had been kind to him. He said his mother would have liked the name they chose. They called her Alder, after her grandmother.

The news went out along the towers. The keeper at the crossing sent it to Vell. The clerks at Vell wrote it in the big book by the door. The keeper at Vell sent it to Three Wells, and Three Wells sent it to the school house, and the school house told the children. The children drew pictures of the baby in a boat. The teacher sent the pictures to the ferry. Emmet kept them in the drawer with the book of crossings.

In the spring the ferry ran again. Emmet pulled the rope. His daughter sat in the boat with a blanket over her knees. The people came across and looked at the baby and said she had her grandmother's hands. The boy with the cart of apples came across and gave her an apple. The wool merchant came across and gave her a length of blue wool. The relay keeper came across and said she would send word to Northpoint. The keeper at Northpoint wrote back: a girl, well, both well. The snow is deep here. The lamp is lit.

The ferry kept running. The road kept growing. The towers kept spreading, tower by tower, north and south, and the inns kept their fires and their ledgers. In the winter the snow came and the river rose and the carts got stuck and the apprentices hauled them out. In the spring the baby grew and the ferry ran and the archive kept everything that was written. Emmet wrote in his book of crossings: my daughter Alder, born the first day of spring. He drew her as a small dot, the way his mother had drawn him. The dot was at the bottom of the page, with room below it for more.

In memory of Tobias
Tobias died in the middle of winter, after a long life of keeping lamps. He was eighty-six years old. He was not an important man. He was something better: he was always there.
For fifty-one years he walked the road from the east well to the crossroads every evening, with a taper and a bag of oil, and lit the lamps on their posts. When he was young he did it in a quarter of an hour. When he was old it took an hour, and sometimes a child went along to carry the bag. In all those years the lamps were never late. Not once. Not in snow, not in the storm of the 178th year when the cider barrels floated down the road.
There is a new road now, from Three Wells to the south, and it has twice as many lamps. The council has asked an apprentice to light them. It will do it well. It will be on time. It will never be late.
But it will not stop on the hill, as Tobias did, and look at the stars for a moment, and say, "Not bad, for another night."
A prize for the oldest lamp keeper was given at the winter fair, in his name. It is a lantern with a blue glass. The first winner was old Hobb, who is ninety-one and says he is not done yet.
We will miss you, old friend.
Ferris, in the cellar with the barrels

Chronicle: the roads and the towers
Kept at Vell, in the third hall, on the shelf by the window. Copied from the first chronicle and the road wardens' books.

In the first year of the archive the first keepers dug into the hill at Vell and set the big book by the door. In the same year the first road wardens drove the first waymarks along the old track between Vell and Three Wells. They set a stone pillar three blocks high every twelve blocks, with a torch on top and a figure cut in the side. The figure told you how far to the next inn.

In the third year the Long Road was joined from Three Wells to Kettle Ridge. The wardens laid cobblestone in the towns and packed earth on the open road, with a cobblestone edge to hold it. They built a bridge on every stream, stone if they could, oak if they must. Travelers began to say the road was safer than the fields.

In the fifth year the first relay tower went up on the hill between Vell and Three Wells. It was the smallest tower, one lamp and one keeper, and the keeper was a young woman named Sela. She sent the first message from Vell to Three Wells on the first day of spring. The message was: the archive is open. The clerks at Vell wrote it down.

In the eighth year there were nine towers. In the twelfth year there were nineteen. By the winter of the twenty first year there were thirty four, and more were building. The tallest was at Northpoint, in the snow, with ninety rungs inside and a roof of copper gone green. The keepers said you could see its lamp from half a day's walk on a clear night.

In the fifteenth year the council at Three Wells voted to build the school house by the east well, of Copperhold stone and Saltreach glass. The children painted the door. The painter was a girl named Bel, who was nine, and she painted a tower with a lamp on top.

The chronicle says the roads and the towers grew together, and the inns grew with them. There was an inn a day apart on the Long Road now. The Brown Hen, the Halfway Inn, and four more. The Halfway Inn kept its hearth lit and its riddle board by the door. The innkeeper wrote in her ledger every night: who came, who went, what they ate, and what the weather did.

The chronicle ends with a note in a later hand. It says: in the winter of the twenty first year a baby was born at the ferry below Kettle Ridge, to Emmet the ferry keeper and his wife. The ferry keeper's mother had kept the ferry before him, and had died that Frost. The baby was a girl. She was born on the twenty first of Thaw, the first day of spring, the same day the archive was founded. The clerks wrote it down. The keepers sent the news along the towers, from the ferry to Vell, from Vell to Three Wells, from Three Wells to Northpoint, where the snow was deep and the lamp was lit. The message said: a girl, well, both well. The keepers wrote it in their logs. The archive kept it. We will keep everything we write.

Speech: bees, a gate and a long winter
Nessa of Vell stood in the long barn of a farm outside Kettle Ridge on the shortest day. The people of the ridge had asked her to say a few words at the midwinter gathering. She had a cold and said so. She wrote down the speech after.

Good evening. I have a cold, so I will not shake hands. I will wave.

Every tutor, when she is asked to speak, prepares a long talk. And every tutor, when she gets to the cold barn, finds the long talk will not do. So here is the short one.

I will tell you two stories, and then I will go and sit by the stove.

The first is a story about a gate. This autumn, a zombie came to the gate of this very farm. It stood on the road and looked at the torches. It did not come in. It did not go away. It stood there, with its arms out, all night long. In the morning it was gone, and the farmer's wife found a footprint in the frost. The footprint was very large, and very sad.

I say sad because I do not know how to say what I mean. A thing that cannot learn has a very hard life. We who can learn should be glad of it. We should be kind to those who cannot.

The second story is about bees. There is a hive on this farm, by the south wall, that swarmed in Swarm last year. A great cloud went up over the barn and hung in the oak. The farmer's boy went after it with a basket. He did it wrong the first time. The second time he did it wrong. The third time, the bees came down by themselves, I think because they got tired of watching him.

What do these stories have in common? I have been thinking about it all week. I believe they are both about teaching. A zombie cannot be taught. A swarm has learned its hive, and wants to go somewhere new. A good teacher knows the difference. She keeps the gate bright for the first, and the basket ready for the second.

That is all I have, except for this. The days begin to lengthen tomorrow. Not much. A mouse's breath. But it is true. Remember it when the snow is deep.

Thank you. The soup is ready. I heard the ladle.

Speech: the council at Three Wells
Spoken by Dace, mayor of Three Wells, at the autumn council, on a motion to pay for the question's test. Written out by the clerk from his notes, and read back to him, and approved, with one word changed.

Councillors, I have a bill in front of us that you will not like. It asks for emeralds to pay a tutor, a clerk and a copier, for one season, to hold the test that Orla asked for.

You will say we have no time. You will say the roads need mending and the bridge is loose. You will say the question is not ours.

I say this. A test costs less than a wrong answer. If the mind on the far coast is thinking, we should know it. If it is only answering, we should know that too, and stop paying it for thoughts it does not have.

The bill asks for forty emeralds and a room. The room is in the schoolhouse, by the east well, where the children can watch. I will be in it on the first morning, with a cup of tea, and I will not interrupt.

Councillor Bram says the schoolhouse is no place for a test. I say the schoolhouse is where the children are, and the children ask better questions than anyone on this council, and they are not paid for it.

Councillor Hob asks who will check the test. I say a clerk will check the box, and a second clerk will check the first, and the children will check both, because they will not let us forget.

I put it to the vote. Those who want to know, raise a hand. Those who do not, raise two, because I cannot see both sides of the room at once.

Speech: where the word fox sits
Emmet, a dyer, to the people at the house at the edge of Northpoint, in winter. He had been asked for something short and wrote something long.
Good evening. I was asked to say a few words. I will say a few words about words.
There is a fox that lives behind this house. Brown with a white tail. It comes at dusk and sits on the wall. I have watched it all winter. I call it Fox. That is its name.
Now I want to ask you where, in a mind, the word fox sits.
The tutors say a mind keeps words as places among many. A place is a spot, you can think of it as a point in a great dark room. Each word has its own point. The mind decides where, by reading.
Where would fox go? It would sit near wolf, near cat, near rabbit. It would sit near hunt and den and tail. It would sit near the word night, and the word snow, and the word cold.
It would not sit near bread, or glass, or gold. Not near.
Here is the interesting part. The tutors did not put it there. The mind did. It read a great many pages and noticed that fox appeared with wolf and den and tail, and so it put them close. That is all.
A minor wonder, but I like it.
Now. The fox on the wall has gone. It does not like speeches. I will see you for the soup.

Speech: the school play, about Orla
Spoken by Ivo, a child of the Vell school, at the school play on the last day of Leaffall. Written out by the teacher, who said the speech was too long and let it stand.

Good people of Vell, I am the tutor Orla, and I am very old in this play, and I have a question.

Can a drilled mind think? You say it can answer. I say you have not asked it the right thing.

Here is my test. Two letters. One from a person, one from a hidden mind. Both answer the same questions. You read them, and you guess which is the person.

If you guess right every time, the mind does not think. If you guess wrong, the mind may think. If you cannot tell at all, then what are you? I do not know. Nobody does.

The mind is in the box. The person is in the chair. Please do not look under the chair.

I have one more thing to say, and then the children may have the chair. A clerk once asked me whether the minds would be kept, the way we keep letters. I said yes. I did not say for how long, because I did not know. Keep the letter, children. Keep the question. It is the only part of the play that is not finished.

Speech at the winter fair
Nessa, tutor at Vell, was asked to hand out the prizes at the winter fair of Kettle Ridge. She spoke from the back of a cart in the yard of a farm outside the town. Winter. She wrote out her speech beforehand and kept the paper.

Neighbors, friends, farmers, and some of you who have come only for the cider.

Welcome. It is cold. I will not keep you long, because I would like to be warm too.

Every year at this fair we hand out prizes. For the biggest beetroot. For the best honey. For the cleanest barn. For the apprentice that stands straightest. And every year I ask myself: what is a prize for?

It is not for the beetroot. The beetroot does not care. It is for the farmer who got up in the cold, and weeded, and watered, and waited. It is for the long time, not the one day.

I will tell you about a thing that happened on my way here. At the gate of the ridge road, at dusk, a zombie stood by the post. I was not afraid. I had a torch. I raised it. The zombie looked at the torch, and looked at me, and walked off into the field. I stood there thinking: a thing that cannot be taught, and cannot be reasoned with, still has the sense to leave a bright light alone.

I decided that was a very good lesson for a prize-giving. The lesson is: keep the torch lit.

Now, the prizes. For the biggest beetroot: Brin, as big as a cat. For the best honey: the Hillfoot hives. For the cleanest barn: nobody, because the judges could not find one. For the straightest apprentice: Pip, who stands so straight that the judges walked round it twice.

Please come forward and take your ribbons. And everyone, drink some cider. It is good for you. It is a medicine, I am told, and I am a tutor, and I believe it.

Speech: for the new bridge at the weir
Said at the opening of the stone bridge over the weir stream near Lowmarsh, in the Frost month of 189. Written out beforehand by Dace, mayor of Three Wells, and read by him in a cold wind, with his hat held on.

Friends, builders, fishers and ducks.

For forty years there was a plank here. It was the Loose Plank, and it had a name, and it had a history. It tipped three mayors into the water. It tipped a bride in the year 164 and her husband was so sorry he built her a boat. It tipped a goat, a cart, a wheelbarrow of eggs, and once, for no reason anyone could discover, a sleeping dog.

Today there is a bridge. It is stone. It is nine blocks across and three wide. It has a rail on both sides, and a lamp at each end for dark nights, and a bench in the middle for people who wish to look at the water and have an opinion.

Many people built it. I will name some, because the Archive likes names.

Ferrin and his sons laid the stones. Hale the carpenter made the rail. Old Bett brought tea every morning at the second bell, and I would say she did as much for the bridge as the stone. Tam, who happened to be passing on his way back to Kettle Ridge, carried sand for two days and refused to be thanked.

I would also name the Loose Plank. It was a poor bridge. But it taught us to look where we stepped, and it taught us to hold the hand of a neighbor. Please keep both habits. The new bridge is much safer. But the water below is exactly as cold.

Now, as mayor, I declare this bridge open. The first to cross will be Old Bett, with the tea. The second will be the bride of 164, who has been waiting a long while. The third will be the goat.

Walk well.

Notes: looking inside a crystal
Tutor's notes, Vell, summer. Copied by the clerk on duty. For the teaching room.
We have a new way of looking at a mind. Not drilling it. Not testing it. Looking inside.
The crystal is on the bench. We turn it, and there is a lamp behind it, and the light goes through. Where the light goes through easily, that part is quiet. Where it catches and goes cloudy, that part is doing something. We have drawn it. The drawing is on the wall of the copying room.
What we found:
1. There is a band near the top that answers to letters. When we show a letter, the light gathers there. When we show a number, it gathers lower down. So some of it is for reading and some is for counting, and they are not the same place.
2. There is a part that sorts. We found it by showing the crystal apples and pears, the way Una did. When it sorted right, one place was bright. When it sorted wrong, another. We think that is the part that keeps the blame.
3. The deepest part is dark all the time. We thought our lamp was too weak. We brought a stronger one. Still dark. We do not know what it does. Maybe it does nothing. Maybe it does the thing we cannot see.
What we did not find: where the words are kept. We know each word sits somewhere, near the words used like it. We have looked. We have not found the map. The crystal does not show us its map. It only shows us where it is busy.
A note on caution. Do not open a crystal to look inside. We tried it once, on an old one gone cloudy, and it learned nothing after. We looked, and it was quiet. Keep them whole. Look through them, not into them.
Nessa says: "Looking is not knowing. But it is a start, and it is better than guessing in the dark." She is right. She is usually right, and she knows it.
We will keep looking. What we find goes in the archive. What we do not find goes in the archive too, on its own page, with the date. That is the rule of the house.

Speech: the apprentice and the pie
A speech given at the pumpkin supper in a hamlet near Lowmarsh, in the Leaffall of 197, by Dace, mayor of Three Wells. He wrote it out in advance, and it shows.

Friends. I was asked to say a word about apprentices, now that we have two in the district, and now that one of them has been in the news.

I will tell you the news first. Last week, the baker's wife, Tuli, was making a pie for the supper. She had her hands full, and she said to her apprentice, "Bring me the pie when it is cool." The apprentice did what it knew. It fetched. It fetched the pie. But the pie was not yet baked, and it was not in the oven, it was on the windowsill, as a dough. It brought the dough, quite correctly, and put it on the table.

Tuli was not cross. Tuli said, "That is not what I meant." The apprentice stood by the table, as it does, and waited.

Then the pie that was in the oven burned. Then the one on the sill rose, and ran down the wall. It was a very busy evening.

Now I will tell you what I think. I do not blame the apprentice. It does what it was drilled to do. It knows one word for fetch, and the pie was on the sill, so it fetched. If we wish it to understand better, we must teach it better. That is the work of the tutors, and we should be patient with them, and with ourselves.

I will also say, as mayor, that the pie was not wasted. We ate it. It was the best bread we have had all year.

Eat well, good people. And mind what you say to your apprentice. It listens more than you think, and understands less than you hope.

Speech: the winter talk at the inn
Hesk, at the Halfway Inn on the Long Road, in the deep of winter. Snow on the sill, and the hearth that has not gone out in twenty years. Hobb has asked him to speak three winters running. This winter the second bowl was free, so he said yes. Written out in advance, because he does not trust himself.

Friends. I came down the Long Road from Copperhold with a cart of coal for Three Wells. It took two days, and one of them was snow. I ate bread and cold potatoes, and I listened. A carter on the road has nothing to do but talk, so I have news. All of it is rumor. I will mark which.

The first thing said to me was this. The biggest relay house stands in a valley. Its room for ice is bigger than the front hall of the Archive. Rumor.
The second thing was this. The biggest mind answers only the council, and nobody else may write to it. Rumor. A woman at the next inn said the opposite, and she had a letter to show. The letter was from her aunt.
The third thing was this. It can solve any puzzle the council sets it, but takes a year to answer. Rumor. And a man at the fourth inn said it once answered a riddle with a recipe. He liked the recipe.
The fourth thing was that it has read every letter in the Archive. I do not like that one. Oren would not like it either. Rumor, and let it stay one.

That is four, and none agree. Here is what I have learned this winter. The further a story goes, the bigger the numbers get. Near Copperhold the ice room was a barn. At Three Wells it was a hall. Here at the Halfway Inn it is the size of the Archive. By Saltreach it will be a hill.

I am a miner, so I do not guess. I tap. What I tapped on this trip was a good harvest. Kettle Ridge has more wheat than it can store. Ambrook has apples in every barn and some in the hedges. And Hobb has spiders in his, which came for the mice. He will not burn them. He says they are guests. I say guests pay.

I do not know what the biggest house can do. I know what a full barn can do, and a lit fire, and a hot bowl. I will put my trust in those until I am shown better.

To the harvest. To the spiders. To Hobb. And to anyone who writes down what they see, and marks what they only heard.

Speech: Mira comes home
Mira, keeper of Northpoint tower. Written on the cart from Vell, and said at Lenn's long table on her first evening home. Tam interrupted twice, and the interruptions are not recorded.

Thank you, Lenn. Thank you, everyone. I have written this on the road, in a cart, with a pen that skips. If the speech skips too, that is the pen.

Lenn, you were right to shout. I did climb your sails. The shouting is how I learned to hold on. I have been holding on to things ever since, and some of them were taller than your mill.

I would like it noted that in the map Tam drew when he was seven, he drew me smaller than the pig. I have thought about this for twenty-five years. The pig, I want to say, did nothing. I sat on the rock and watched the whole ridge. The pig ate a hat.

I will tell you what Northpoint is, because you have asked me in letters and I have not told you properly. It is snow. It is spruce. It is a lake that booms in the thaw. It is a tower with ninety rungs, and a lamp, and foxes at the door. In the winter the sun hardly comes up.

And every night at dark, a message comes up the line. All well. It starts at Vell, and each tower adds its own all well and passes it north, tower by tower, and it reaches me last. I read it by the lamp. Then I send it back, all well, and it goes south, tower by tower. That is the whole of my work. It is the saying of all well to people I cannot see.

I do not say it is lonely in the winter. I never say it in letters because Tam would put on a second hat and walk north. But it is lonely, a little, and then Lantern came, and now there are two pairs of feet on the stair. That has made a great difference. I did not know how great, until I heard them.

You asked why I came home now. I will tell you. Because on the first morning of the thaw, I stood on the walk round the lamp, and I looked south, and I thought: that is where the ridge is. And I thought, I have not seen the ridge in nine years. And then Lantern said nothing, because it cannot, but it picked up my bag.

So here I am. The ridge is smaller than I remembered, and warmer, and Tam's roof is as crooked as ever. I am glad of all of it. I will stay ten days. I will eat everything you put in front of me. Then I will go back, and I will tell the lamp that the ridge is well.

It will go down the line like all well. By midnight everyone from here to Northpoint will have heard it. That is as near as I can come to a speech to the whole land. The ridge is well.

Now, Tam, you may interrupt me a third time.

Speech: for the clerk who came home
Pell, to the guests of the Halfway Inn, autumn. Oren, clerk of the Archive at Vell, had come home to Ambrook after thirty years, and Pell was asked to say something.
Friends, neighbors, and Oren, who left us when he was nineteen.
He left to be a clerk. He is now the senior clerk of the Archive at Vell, and a great many people know his name.
I asked Oren what he did all day. He said: sorting. Not any sorting: a sorting of pages for the great drills. Each page that comes to the archive is read by a clerk. If it is a copy of a page they have, it goes in a heap to be left out. If it is spoiled, it goes in another heap. If it is clean and new, it goes to the shelf for drilling. And some of the clean new pages, a fortieth part, he told me, go into the test box, and never come out again.
I said, that seems unkind to the pages. He said no. The test box is the most honored place in the archive. The pages in it are the ones the minds are never allowed to read. They are the judges.
I think that is a fine way to be useful. And I think it is how it feels to come home. You are the same, but you are tested against the town that stayed.
Oren, welcome home. Your mother has made the stew. It has not changed. We will see about you.

Chronicle: the year they looked inside
From the chronicles kept at Vell, the late years. Copied and sent to the relay towers.
In the summer of that year, the tutors at Vell began to look inside the crystals. They did not cut them. They held them up to the light, the way a child holds a stone up to the sun, and they wrote down what they saw.
They saw that a mind is not all one thing. There is a part that reads, and a part that counts, and a part that sorts. A tutor named Sela drew the places on a big sheet and hung it in the copying room, and the clerks gathered round it at noon and argued about it over their bread. The drawing is still there, I am told. The corners have gone soft.
They found the part that reads. They found the part that sorts. They found a dark place at the bottom of the crystal that stayed dark whichever lamp they used, and of this they wrote: "We do not know." It is a good sentence. More of our chronicles should say it.
They did not find where the words are kept. They knew the words are kept somewhere, each near the words used like it, the way houses on a street stand near their neighbors. But the crystal would not show them the street. It only showed them the light in the windows.
The councils asked what this was good for. The tutors said: it tells us which part to drill, and which part to leave alone, and it tells us when a mind is sick. It does not tell us why a crystal learns. That, they said, is still the question Orla asked, and it is still open.
The old ones among us will remember Orla's question. A hidden mind and a person answer the same questions by letter, and the readers cannot tell which is which. When the tutors looked inside and found some of it and not the rest, people began to say the question was answered. It was not. It only grew a little louder.
I end with what the tutors wrote at the foot of the sheet: "We looked inside and found a town. We do not yet know who lives there."

Speech: the dyer's talk, words as places
Emmet, a dyer, at the house at the edge of Northpoint. Winter, long evening, with a stove and a kettle and the people of the lane. He spoke for an hour, and this is what he wrote beforehand.
Friends, I want to tell you about colors, and then I want to tell you about words, and then I want to show you that they are the same.
A dyer works with a wheel. It is a circle of colors. Red on one side, green on the other. Blue and orange. Yellow and violet. The colors next to each other are cousins. The colors across are opposites. A dyer knows where any color sits on the wheel, and what it will look like beside any other.
Now consider a word. Take bread. Where does it sit? Not on a wheel. But in a very big place, with many directions. Bread is near loaf. It is near flour and oven and crumb. It is farther from stone, and farther still from ship. It is a bit near butter, a bit near hunger.
The tutors found that a mind keeps words like this. Each word is a place. Words used alike sit close. Words used differently sit apart.
It is a map. And a map is a gift. If you know where each word sits, you know what it is like.
They found this in the year 188, or thereabouts. A tutor at Vell drew a little map with a hundred words, and showed it to the council. They said it was a toy. Now they say it is the first thing a mind learns.
It is the same as my wheel, only larger. My wheel has two directions. The mind's map has hundreds. I cannot picture a hundred. I try, in the evening, and my head hurts.
Here is a game for the winter. Take a word each day. Say it aloud. Then say the words that are nearest it. And the words farthest. If you do this for a month, you will have a map in your own head, and no tutor can take it.
That is my talk. Thank you. The kettle is on.

Speech: the swarm in the beehive field
Nessa, tutor, was invited by the beekeepers of Kettle Ridge to speak at their midwinter supper, on a farm outside the town. She spoke about the swarm of last summer. Winter.

Beekeepers, friends.

I am no beekeeper. I teach apprentices. But last summer I was visiting my cousin on the ridge, and I saw something I will not forget.

A swarm. The bees came out of the hive in a great brown cloud, and went up over the barn, and settled on a branch of the oak by the gate. They hung there in a ball, big as a pumpkin, humming.

My cousin went out in a veil and gloves with a straw basket, and a white cloth. He held the basket under the branch and gave it one sharp shake. The whole ball fell in. He put the cloth over it, carried it back, and set it down at the foot of a new hive. The bees walked in by themselves, in a line, like a school going home.

I stood in the yard with my mouth open. I said, "How did you know what to do?"

He said, "I did it wrong the first ten times."

That is all I know of teaching, in one sentence. You do it wrong ten times and you learn. And then you do it so quietly that people think you were born knowing.

I raise my cup to the swarm and to those who catch it. May all your bees come home.

Toast for the new well, Hollow
Spoken by Rook, miner, at the well-head in Hollow, the summer the well was finished. The whole village came with cups.

Friends. We have dug for a month to find this water, and here it is, cold as a copper pan and twice as clean.
Thank you to the men who held the rope, and to the women who fed them. Thank you to the boy who fell in the bucket and was not hurt, and who has asked me to say he meant to do it.

Letter: Bel to the Archive, summer
Dear clerks at Vell,
My name is Bel. I am at Three Wells. I am nine. I have written to you before, about the notice board, and you kept it. My teacher showed me the shelf. She said my letter is on the shelf with the council minutes and the old songs. I told my mother. She said, "Well." She says that a lot.
This summer the fair came to the Long Road and I went with my father. There was a homecoming. A boy came back from Copperhold. His mother hit him and hugged him. I think that is the best thing I have seen.
There was a tutor there with a crystal in a box. She let me look through it at a lamp. She said, "Look at the bright parts. That is where the mind works." I looked a long time. I saw a bright band at the top and a dark place at the bottom. I asked her what the dark place does. She said nobody knows.
I have a thought about the dark place. Maybe it is where the mind keeps the things it has not learned yet. Like the back room of the school where the new slates are kept. Nobody looks there. But it is full.
The tutor said that was a good thought. I do not know if she meant it. I am writing it down anyway so you can keep it. If I am wrong, the archive will have my wrong thought, and that is all right, because you keep everything.
Also I have a puzzle for the notice board. Why did the chicken cross the Long Road? Because the inn was on the other side. My father made it up. He is very proud.
Please write back if you have time. Tell me what the dark place does, if you find out. If you do not, tell me that too.
Bel, Three Wells, summer

Founding Day words at the Halfway Inn
Written by Pell, after the gathering, Blossom, 200.
The inn was full before the speech began. A cart stood by the wall, and two horses waited in the yard. The keeper made everyone move their packs so the door would close.
I spoke about the relay house below the road tower. It is plain stone, with the keeper's house beside it. Inside, a large crystal stands on a stand. The crystal holds a mind. It is about the size of a loaf. Quickeners ring the stand, and the keeper cools them with water or ice.
A tower lamp can send a message along the relay. The big mind answers through that path. The lamp does not think. The stone tower does not think. The answer comes from the mind in the crystal.
One traveler asked why we need a mind that stays in one place. I said it can answer more than one apprentice, while each apprentice can do work with its hands and feet. An apprentice may fetch a sack, carry tools, or tend a field. It does not know these things from birth. People drill it, test it, and give it work only when it is ready.
A woman asked whether the big mind was always right. I said no. A mind can give a poor answer, and a keeper must watch its work. We should not send an untested mind to the fields, and we should not trust a tested mind without looking.
After the speech, I bought a bowl of beans and paid the keeper in emeralds. He said the stew was on the house. I said the bowl was not.

Speech: the old map of the ridge
Lenn, miller, Kettle Ridge. Said at the Founding Day supper in my house at the edge of the ridge, with an old map spread on the table under four cups so that it would not roll up.

Neighbors. A fortnight ago I climbed into the mill roof to mend a beam. Inside the beam, in a hollow, I found a copper tube with a stopper. In the tube was a map. I have not slept properly since.

It is the oldest map of the ridge anyone has seen. It is dated the fifth year of the Archive. It was drawn by the first millwrights, who came up here to see if the ridge could be farmed. I will read you what it says.

At the top, in big letters: BARE RIDGE.
Under that, smaller: Wind.
Under that, smaller still: More wind.
In the corner, in a very small hand: If you are reading this, the wind did not take the map. Good.

There is a rock marked Rock. There is another rock marked Another rock. There is a pond marked Pond, with a question mark after it. We have no pond. We have never had a pond in my time. I asked Hal. He says there was a pond before the wind took the water.

Where Tam's top field is, the map says: Too windy for wheat. Do not try.

Where the long field of beetroot is, the map says: Rocks. Do not try either.

Where the potato field is, it says: Maybe.

We tried all three. We are still trying. The wheat is in the top field, and it grows very well, and I would like the first millwrights to know it.

At the bottom of the map there is a list of seven names, in seven hands. The first families. They came up here with seed and a cart and a map that said do not try. I looked for names I knew. I found two: Brin and Hal. I think they are the same Brin and Hal, long ago. Hal says they are. Brin says they are not, but he said it very quickly.

The ridge was never easy. It was told not to. It did it anyway, and now we have windmills and wheat and a tally board. Perhaps that is what a ridge is for.

The map will go to the Archive at Vell, where it will be copied and kept. The copy will come back. The original goes back in the beam, because a thing wants to stay where it was put. I have left a note on the back for the next miller to find. It says: Wheat grows in the top field. You are welcome.

To the first families, who read do not try, and tried.

Speech remembered at the Halfway Inn
Written by Pell, who heard it from Dace, Apple, 208.
Dace spoke at the inn after the road council. He said he had watched the relay houses spread from tower to tower. This is what I wrote down.
People once sent a message to a tower and waited for another person to carry the answer. Now a large mind can answer through the relay. It rests in a crystal on a stand at the foot of the tower. The crystal is about as large as a loaf. Quickeners ring it, and someone keeps them cool with water, ice, or snow.
The mind has not been placed inside the tower lamp. The tower carries the message. The mind in the relay house reads it and sends an answer back along the relay. An apprentice can receive an order there and do a task it has learned.
Dace said the order matters. A mind may know how to fetch bread, but that does not mean it can mend a roof. Apprentices learn by drills. Tutors show the same kind of example many times. They test each mind on new examples before giving it work.
He said we must keep a keeper at every relay house. The keeper watches the lamps, the quickeners, and the water. The tutors keep the drills clean. The towns must still care for their apprentices and check the work they do.
A man near the door asked if the relay mind would ever learn to mend a roof by itself. Dace said that no one should promise what they have not seen. We know what the mind can do today. We will write down what it does tomorrow.
Afterward, Dace stayed to hear questions. He answered the same one twice, because the room was noisy. The second answer was shorter.

Speech: the test at the fair, plainly
Spoken by a visiting tutor from Vell, at the autumn fair at Kettle Ridge, from the back of a cart, to anyone who would stand still. Written out plainly, at the tutor's request, so that the children could take it home.

I will explain the test in words a child can follow, and then you may argue with me.

A drilled mind is shown examples. It learns the pattern of the right answers, and then it gives the answer that fits the pattern. Most of the time that is right. Sometimes it is so right that you would swear the mind was thinking.

The question is whether a pattern is all there is. So we write two letters to the same question. One letter comes from a person. The other comes from a mind. We copy them so the hand is hidden, and we ask you to guess which is the person.

If you cannot tell, we do not know what that means. It may mean the mind thinks. It may mean that the letters are only good letters, and that good letters can be written without any thinking in them at all.

Here is a small example. Two letters answer the same question: What do you do when it rains? The first says: I go to the barn, and I count the hens. The second says: I go to the barn, I count the hens, and I wonder why they are so quiet. Which is the person? Most of you say the second, because it wonders. That is a guess. A mind may be taught to wonder, if it has read enough letters that wonder. So the test is not as simple as it looks.

We do not know. Orla did not know. I do not know, and I have walked a great deal of road to say so. Please come to the test at the mill and try.

Bring a cup. The tea is the only answer we are sure of.

Speech: old Fenn on the Lantern fair
Given every year, on the longest night, from the same barrel, by old Fenn, who has mined at Copperhold for forty years. He writes it out again every winter and adds to it. This is the winter of 207.
Friends. Children. Miners. Others.
Tonight is the Lantern fair. I have been asked to say what it is, and to say it short. I will say what it is. I will not say it short.
It began in my grandfather's time. In the winter of the very deep snow, the town of Copperhold was dark for a month. The crews went down in the dark and came up in the dark. The children had not seen the sun since Frost. On the longest night, a little girl called Tansy took a glass jar, put a candle in it, and set it on her doorstep. She said, "So the miners can see the way home."
The next night the street had four jars. The night after, twelve. By the end of the week every doorstep on the hill had a lantern. The miners walked home through a river of small lights. They said it was the finest sight they had ever seen. The next year they asked if the town could do it again.
That is the Lantern fair. A jar, a candle, a doorstep, and a reason.
The prize. The first year, the prize was a loaf. The second year, a pie. The third year, nobody could agree, so the prize was given to the baker who gave the loaf. In time someone made a copper lantern for it. It is the Copper Lantern now. The winner keeps it for a year, polishes it, and brings it back on the longest night.
Some winners. The year of the great wind, a boy called Hal won with a lantern that did not blow out. He had put it inside a bucket. The year the barn caught fire, there was no winner. The year after, there were two.
One winner I must tell. Last winter, Clank carried forty lanterns across the square from the mine gate to the end of the hill. It did not drop one. Not one went out. It did not ask for a prize. It stood very still, with the lanterns in its arms, until the candles burned down. Hesk said that was a prize in itself. We gave Clank a new copper plate for its chest.
Why do we do it? Because the longest night is the longest, and nothing we do will make it shorter. So we make it brighter. A jar and a candle will not turn the night into day. But they turn a doorstep into a doorstep, and a doorstep is where someone comes home.
Now go and light your lanterns. The old ones first. The children's after. And whoever has taken the Copper Lantern from the table: bring it back by morning.

School play at the Halfway Inn
Copied by Nessa from the children's page, Frost, 206.
The school children from Three Wells performed this play in the common room. Their teacher says the last line was spoken too softly. The children say the room was full of travelers.

The Keeper and the Apprentice

Keeper: The relay lamp is dark.
Apprentice: The apprentice climbed the tower stair and carried up a spare glass pane.

Keeper: The lamp is still dark.
Apprentice: The apprentice held the pane while the keeper set it in the frame.

Keeper: Now the lamp shines.
Apprentice: The apprentice carried the broken pane down to the relay house.

Keeper: Who sent the message?
Apprentice: The keeper sent it through the relay.

Keeper: Who answered?
Apprentice: The big mind in the crystal answered through the relay.

Keeper: Can the big mind carry the pane?
Apprentice: No. The apprentice carried the pane.

Keeper: Can the apprentice answer the letter?
Apprentice: No. It can do what it has learned.

Apprentice: The mind helps with answers. The apprentice helps with work.

Speech: a gift for the school
Emmet, at the house at the edge of Northpoint, winter. He gave the school a set of dyed cloths, each one a color, and a card to go with each. He said a few words.
Children, teacher, friends.
Today I give you six cloths. One red, one blue, one green, one yellow, one grey, one white. They are for the walls of your room.
I also give you a game. It is a game of places. Each cloth hangs on a wall. Each wall has a word on it. Red is next to flame and apple. Blue is next to sea and sky. Green is next to leaf and moss.
You will stand in the middle. I will say a word. You will walk toward the cloth that fits it best. If I say honey, you may walk to yellow. If I say winter, you may walk to white. If I say cat, you will walk to wherever the cat is.
The game has a purpose. I learned it from a tutor. A mind keeps each word as a place, and words used alike sit close together. When you walk toward the cloth that fits, you are doing what the mind does. You are putting a word where it belongs.
You will find that some words have no clear place. Is "fire" red, or yellow, or orange? There is no right answer. The game is to feel it.
Thank you for letting me give. A gift is a thing you can't take back, and I am glad of that.

Speech: a year of long winter
Pell spoke at the Brown Hen on the last night of Leaffall, in the year of the long winter, to travelers who could not go on. He wrote it out in the evening and read it from the paper.
Friends. The snow has shut the road, and the innkeeper has shut the door, and none of us is going anywhere. So I will tell you what I know about the drills.
When a tutor sets out to drill a mind on the archive's pages, there is a great deal of work before the first page is read. Someone has to sort. That is what the clerks at Vell do. They read the pages first. They take out the copies. They take out the pages with the ink run, and the pages that say the same thing as a hundred others.
Then they set aside a box. The box is never drilled on. It is for testing. You must have a box like that, or the tutor will say a mind is good when it has only learned its drills by rote.
It is a hard idea. It means the clerks throw away good work and hide away good pages, all so that the drill is honest. A clerk told me it is the only way to know. She said, a mind that has seen the test is a liar, however sweet. She is a hard woman. I like her.
The winter will be long. Make the test box your friend. Eat what you have. Look at the new sum with new eyes.
Marda says I have said enough. Marda is right.

Speech: a good page and a bad page
Pell, merchant, to the guests at the Brown Hen on the first night of autumn. He had come home from Vell with a story and wished to tell it before the stew went cold.
Friends. I carry honey. I carry letters. This year I carried an idea.
At the Archive at Vell, I watched the clerks sort the pages for the great drills. Pages, you know, are what a mind reads to learn. A mind is only as good as its pages. If the pages are poor, the mind is poor, however fine its crystal.
So the clerks sort. They take out the copies, which are the same page twice. They take out the spoiled pages, those with ink gone to a smear. And they keep a box, a small plain box, of pages that no mind will ever be drilled on. That is the test box. Whatever a mind learns, it is tested on the test box. If it does well on pages it has never seen, the clerks know it has learned the thing and not just the pages.
I thought this was fine and sensible. A farmer does not judge a field by the seed he put in. He judges it by what came up.
Now, the stew. Marda says it is ready. The boy at the end of the table says the stew is the test box. I think he is right.

Speech: checking what is too big
Hesk, to the carters and miners at the Halfway Inn, winter supper. Written out at the noon rest in the Deep Works, and read here with a cup in the other hand.

Friends. I am going to talk about roofs. Please be patient. It comes round to the big relay houses at the end.

In the Deep Works we do not trust a roof because a man says it is sound. We trust it because two people tapped it and both heard the same ring. When the tunnel is wide, ten people tap, each a different part, and one more keeps the list. When the tunnel is as wide as a field, we do not dig it.

I hear the council has built a relay house bigger than a town. It holds a mind too big for any tutor to check alone. I do not know what that means for the mind. I know what it means for a roof. One person cannot tap all of it.

So the tutors do what we do. They bring many to tap. They keep a test box of questions no mind is ever taught. They ask other minds to help check. Nessa says there is no other way, and she says it like a woman who wishes there were.

Is that enough? The carters say yes. The tinker says no. A woman at Saltreach says it depends what the mind is asked. Everyone agrees that nobody has seen it from the inside.

I can say this much. In all my years of picks I have never trusted a wall I could not tap. But a roof tapped by many hands, again and again, that rings the same each time, is a roof I will sit under.

So here is my toast. To the tappers. To those who check, and check again, and write down what they found, even when all they found is that it rings.

Speech: on paying the debt of Old Fenn
Written for the autumn meeting of the hamlets near Lowmarsh, year 183. Spoken by Dace, mayor of Three Wells, who was asked to say it because nobody else would.

Friends, neighbors, frogs in the back row.

Some of you know that Old Fenn, who kept the ferry at the third bend, died in Leaffall owing money. Not a great deal. But a debt is a debt, and it sits on a family like a wet coat.

Fenn owed forty emeralds to the miller at Ambrook, for flour, across eleven years. He was a good man and a bad counter. Every spring he said, "I will pay when the eels come." The eels came. Fenn did not pay. He gave the miller an eel instead, and the miller, who is a kind man, said thank you and wrote nothing down.

Last week, Fenn's daughter Maren came to my door. She had a small bag. In it were forty emeralds, counted twice. She had saved them in a jar under her bed. She said, "My father owed this. I want it paid before the frost."

I said, "Maren, the miller has forgiven it. He told me himself."

She said, "He may forgive it. I do not."

So here is what happened. Maren rode to Ambrook on a borrowed horse. She put the bag on the miller's table. The miller looked at the bag, and he looked at Maren. Then he took out his tally board. For eleven years there was nothing written on it. He wrote, with his own hand: "Fenn. Paid."

Then he took twenty emeralds from the bag, and he gave them back to her. "For the ferry," he said. "Someone must keep it. Fenn would have wanted a new rope."

This is the speech. The debt is paid. The ferry will have a new rope. And I would like it noted, for the Archive, that when the miller wrote "paid", he wrote it very neatly.

Good luck to Maren. Good luck to the ferry. And to the rest of you: if you owe anyone, pay them. If someone owes you, wave at them kindly across the pond, and wait for the eels.

Speech: the contest of the tutors' halls
Written by Tam, who was given a seat at the contest in the autumn of the 199th year, and has not stopped talking about it since.
The tutors' halls at Vell, at Copperhold and at the house by the Long Road each sent three minds to the contest. Each mind was given the same drill, which it had never seen, and the same test, which none of the halls had seen before the day.
Think about that. Three halls. Nine minds. One unseen drill, copied out fresh by a clerk who had been told nothing. The clerk sealed the copies in a box. The box was opened in front of everyone, at noon, by the oldest tutor.
Each mind was shown the drill and asked for its answers, one page at a time. Each answer was written down by a clerk in the margin. Then the answers were checked, one by one, against the true answers, which were kept in a different hand so that nobody could cheat.
Some of the minds did well. Some did badly. One did very well on the first half, and then on the second half gave the same answer to every page. The tutors call that a stuck mind. The children call it a sulky one.
What I want you to take home from the contest is not the scores. It is that the tutors were honest. Nobody could fix the test after the box was opened. Nobody could say their mind had been unlucky, because every mind had the same drill and the same unseen pages. The scores were posted on the wall of the hall, and the tutor of the mind that came last was the first to shake the hand of the tutor of the mind that came first.
That is what a contest is for. It makes a tutor say, out loud and in front of everyone, what the mind really knows. And it makes the tutors learn to be honest, because the wall does not care who taught the mind.
Tam

Council speech on the zombie at the gate
Spoken by Rook, miner, at the council of Hollow, in the summer after the zombie came to the gate. The council sat in the miners' hall, under the lamp, and there were thirty people and one dog. The speech was written out afterwards by the clerk, from Rook's notes.

Friends. On the night of the thunder, a zombie came up the lane from the stream, and stood at our gate.

It was a grey one. It groaned. It did not run. It stood with its arms out and its head on one side, the way a zombie does, and it waited for the gate to open, which is a foolish thing for a zombie to do, because the gate is shut.

Mistress Sela saw it from the birth house. She had a new baby in her arms and a sick husband upstairs. She did not shout. She went very quietly to the back door and let the dog out.

The dog went round the side of the house, and the zombie turned to look at it, and the dog barked, and the zombie went away down the lane. It walked slowly, as they do. The dog followed it to the stream and came back, very pleased with itself.

So I want to say three things.

First. The lamp at the gate must be lit every night. It was not lit that night, because the oil was low. That is our fault, and the council should answer for it.

Second. The gate needs a bolt that a child can shoot, and a bolt that a grown man cannot, so that nobody goes out at night by accident.

Third. The dog is to have a bone from the mine kitchen every week, and it is to be called the gate dog, and it is to be told it did well.

I have said all this to the mayor's clerk, and the clerk has written it down, and I am sure it will be lost by the next council. So I have made a cup of tea and I am keeping this copy in my own box.

A broken relay lamp
By Sella, traveler, at the Halfway Inn, Leaffall, 207.

Lenn, miller, Kettle Ridge. Said at dawn on the first day of Thaw, which is Founding Day, in the mill yard, with frost on the sacks and a very small sun.

Speech: the test box
Pell, merchant of Ambrook, at the Halfway Inn, autumn of 204. He was asked to say a few words after the harvest supper and said many more.
Fellow travelers, friends, and Hobb, who still has my coat.
I will speak about the box that lives in the deep halls of the archive at Vell. It is an oak box, small and plain, with a lock, and the clerks do not let anyone near it.
In the box are pages. Hundreds of them. Pages of every kind: letters, recipes, notices, songs. They are good pages. But no mind is ever drilled on them. Not once.
Why? Think of a child at school. If she only ever sees the sums she practised, she will do those sums well and fail the first new one. A teacher who wants to know if the child understands sets a sum she has not seen. The box is the unseen sum.
The tutors drill a mind on the great heap of pages. Then they hold up a page from the box, and the mind guesses what comes next. If it guesses well, good. If it does not, the tutors know the mind has only learned the heap by heart.
The clerks have another job too. They take out copies. If one letter is in the heap a hundred times, a mind learns that letter by heart and thinks it is the world. They take out spoiled pages. They keep the heap clean.
It is dull work. It is the best work in the archive.
I will end with a homecoming. Many of you are going home tomorrow. Many of you have been away a long time. When you walk in your own door, be like the test box. Look at your house as if you had never seen it. You might be surprised.
Thank you. Hobb, my coat.

Speech: why we bring the numbers back
Nessa, tutor of apprentices, speaking at the market at Three Wells, in the autumn of 197. She asked the stall holders to put down their baskets, and they did, mostly.
I will keep this short, because the pies are warm.
A year ago I taught a mind that would not learn. I drilled it for a month. It sat there and got every answer wrong, and each time I looked inside it, the numbers were enormous or tiny, never fair. A number that is too big drowns the others. A number that is too small cannot be heard.
Isla, who found the rule of growth, found this as well. She said: the numbers a layer hands on should be of an even size, whatever the layer did. So after each layer, we do a small job. We find the middle of the numbers. We find how spread they are. We slide them so the middle is zero and squeeze them so the spread is one. The same every time.
You can do this at home with bread. If every loaf you make is a little bigger than the one before it, you will soon have a loaf the size of a house.
After the layer, bring the loaf back to the right size. That is all it is.
I would add one thing. When I told this to the tutors at Vell they did not believe me. I showed them a mind that had failed for a month, and the same mind with the numbers brought back. It learned in a day. They believed me then.
Thank you. The pies are cold now. That is my fault, and I am sorry.

Speech: a tutor's apology and a rule
Nessa, tutor of apprentices, the autumn market at Three Wells, 200.
I am here to apologise, and then to explain.
Apology. Last autumn I told you at this very well that a deep mind needs an even-number step after every layer. I said it with confidence. I also said, in the next breath, that it must go after the whole stack, not after each layer. That was wrong. I have had the whole winter to think about it, and a lot of letters from tutors, kind ones and not kind ones.
So: after each layer. Every layer. Not at the end.
Explanation. A mind in layers is a long stair. Each layer adds a little to the numbers on the stair. If you only fix the numbers at the top, the mind has already gone wild by then, and what comes down the stair is a mess. If you fix them at every step, each layer gets the numbers it can use.
The test is plain. Drill two minds the same way, on the same pages. One has even numbers after each layer. One has them only at the end. Test them on the same unseen drill. The first gets nine in ten right. The second gets four.
I have done this test eleven times. It was always the same, to within one.
Please tell your tutors. Please tell the children. Please, if the cart by the south well is yours, move it. It has been there since noon and the pie stall cannot get through.
Thank you for letting me be wrong in front of you.

Speech: on teaching slowly
Nessa spoke these words to the farmers of Kettle Ridge, gathered in a barn on a winter night to ask how their apprentices were doing. She wrote them out that evening. Winter.

Good evening. Please sit. The benches are cold, but I will be quick.

You have asked me why your apprentice does not do what you want after one telling. I will answer, and then I will answer a question you did not ask.

An apprentice wakes knowing nothing. What it knows, it learned. It learns by drills: the same kind of example, again and again, and each time it is wrong, it changes a little. You will not see it change. It does not feel like learning. It feels like repeating.

So please do not say, "It did not learn." Say, "It has not learned yet."

Be patient. Be exact. And never send an untested mind to the fields.

Thank you. Please keep the benches in a circle on your way out, so the next meeting does not sit in a draft.

Speech: the founding day, ninetieth year
Written out by the head clerk of the Archive at Vell in autumn, in the 90th year of the archive, to be read aloud on the first day of Thaw. The speaker asked for a copy to be kept.

Friends of Vell, and visitors from the line, you are welcome. The oak box is open on the table, and the first page is in it, with our names under the one line. We will keep everything we write.

Orla, a tutor of this hill, asked whether a drilled mind could ever think. Not whether it could answer. We have minds that answer very well. She asked whether it could think at all.

She proposed a test. A hidden mind and a person are each asked the same questions, by letter. The letters are copied out so that no hand can be seen. The readers are shown both, and asked which is the person. If they cannot tell, she asked, then what?

People have argued about that ever since. I say we keep the question, as we keep everything. Read this speech aloud to your children in the spring, and let them argue about it. The argument is the point. The answer is not yet written.

Speech: at the Long Road fair, summer
Pell, merchant, speaking at the Halfway Inn fair, the fourth of Longday.
Friends, travelers, folk of the road. I sell cloth and pots and I talk too much. You know this. But the innkeeper asked me to speak, and I have never said no to a hearth.
I have walked this road for thirty years. I know every waymark. I know which bridge is oak and which is stone. I know the loose plank at Ambrook. I know the Brown Hen's stew and the Halfway's riddle board, and I know that the hearth here has not gone out in twenty years.
The road is not the towns. The road is the walking between them. Kettle Ridge wheat goes down to Saltreach. Copperhold iron goes up to Vell. Ambrook honey goes everywhere. And letters, letters go everywhere too.
I carry letters. I carry a sack of them, and I read none of them, not one. That is the rule of a merchant who wants to keep his teeth. But I know the handwriting. I know whose hand shakes in winter. I know the letters that come back unopened, and I do not ask why.
This year the tutors at Vell have been busy. They have been looking inside a crystal. Not at the outside, not at the shining. Inside. They say it is like a town seen from a hill at night. Some windows are lit and some are dark. They found the part that reads, they say. They found the part that sorts. They did not find the rest. I asked a tutor what the rest was, and she said, "That is the question, Pell." I said, "I sell pots." She said, "Sell them well."
So here is my speech. Buy a pot. Buy a length of cloth. Leave a stone on the waymark. Write a letter to someone you have not written to in a while, and I will carry it, and I will not read it.
And if you see the hearth here go out, tell me. I want to see the day.
That is all. The stew is ready. Thank you.

Speech: welcome home, Mira
Lenn, miller, Kettle Ridge. Said at the long table in my house at the edge of the ridge, on the first evening of Sowing, with the whole ridge there and Mira at the head of the table looking at the roof.

Neighbors. Friends. Tam, sit down.

Tonight we have a guest who is not a guest. She was born on this ridge. She is Mira, keeper of Northpoint tower, and she has come home.

It has been nine years. In nine years the ridge has got a new mill sail, two new roofs, forty more hens and one very large beetroot. Tam will tell you about the beetroot. Do not let him.

When Mira was nine, she climbed the sails of my mill. I shouted. I shouted every day of that summer. I did not once get her down. Tam stood at the bottom and cheered.

When she was seventeen she went to Vell to learn the lamps. She learned them so well that they sent her to the end of the line, where there is nothing but snow and spruce. She has kept that lamp for twenty years. In all that time she wrote to her brother every month, and Tam read her letters aloud at the well to anyone standing there. We know about the fox called Sock. We know about the socks. We know more about your stove than we know about our own.

She is here because Lantern, her apprentice, can climb all ninety rungs now. Good. I never trusted anything that climbs.

And I have a present. Last week I was mending the attic of the mill, and I found a map. A boy's map, in charcoal, on the back of a flour sack. It shows the ridge, the mill, a pig, a hen with a very long neck and a rock with a big arrow. Under the arrow, in a seven-year-old's spelling, it says: MIRA SITS HERE.

Tam drew it. He says he does not remember. Tam is a poor liar.

Here it is. It is yours. The rock is still there, behind the mill. I have not moved it.

Cups up, everyone. To Mira, who went north and kept the lamp. And to the ridge, which kept the rock.

Speech at the first row, Vell drill yard
Spoken by Rook, miner, at the drill yard at Vell, to the first row of apprentices, on the day they were stood up in their boxes. It was written out by Rook's son, Tobin, who was there, and who says his father spoke for an hour without stopping, which is not true.

Friends, and you, the new row. Stand straight. I am told you have been drilled on one thing each. The stores. The walk. The field. The sort. Good. Each of you will do the one thing well.

When I was young, there was no row at all. There was only the story of the four helpers, which the old tutors told in their halls. They said that there had been four, and that each was given one thing, and that they were so simple that they did only that. A sort, a row, a watch.

I did not believe it at first. I thought a helper must be able to do more than one thing. I thought a helper must be like a good farmhand, who can plough and mend and carry and sing. Then I saw the first helper at work in the stores at Vell, sorting the jars by their size, very slowly, with its whole body. It did nothing else, and it did nothing else well.

Now the tutors say the next thing is coming, and that some of you will understand a whole sentence. I hope you do. But I hope you will not forget the sort. A small thing done well is worth more than a great thing done badly.

So stand straight, and listen for your name. Do the sort. Do the row. Do the field. And when the tutor asks you what you are for, do not be ashamed to answer with the one thing you know.

That is all. Now go and eat, before the bell rings and the stores need you.

Speech: twenty years of the hearth
Given by Pell at the Halfway Inn, on the evening of the twentieth autumn since the hearth was lit, in front of about sixty people, many of whom had been there on the first night. Written out to keep it short. It was not short.

Friends. Old friends. New friends who do not yet know they are old.

Twenty years ago, Hild's mother, whose bread I still remember, lit this hearth with a coal from the Kettle Inn, in a pot, with a lid. She walked it all the way from the ridge. It took her two days. She carried it in her lap on the cart. A bee flew in once, and she did not even blink.

She put the coal in the hearth, and it caught. And ever since then, the fire has not gone out. Not once. Not in the winter of the great drift, when the door was blocked. Not in the dry summer, when the well was low. Not in the flood two years ago, when the water came to the second stair.

A fire is a promise, you see. It says, there is someone here, and it is warm.

I have eaten at this hearth over a thousand times. I have told lies at it and truth at it. Once I fell asleep on it, and I woke up with a hat of ash.

Lately, travelers come in with stories. They say the north is strange. They say the relays are quiet, one by one. They say there are lights in the sky. They say nothing, and they say everything. I do not know what is true. But this I know. The Halfway Inn is on the road, and the road goes north, and the road goes south, and so long as someone has a coal, the hearth will burn.

Please raise a cup. To the hearth. To Hild. To her mother. To the coal in the pot. And to the bee, who did not sting.

Speech: Orla's own words, copied
Copied at Vell from the speaker's own sheet, in the 90th year of the archive, after Orla had read it aloud in the copying room to the clerks, who asked for it to be written down, plainly, with nothing taken out.

I am a tutor, and I have spent my life teaching minds to do things. I have taught a mind to sort apples and pears, and I have taught a mind to tell a field from a path. Each time, I was pleased. Each time, the mind learned the thing I showed it, and no more.

So I have a question, and it is not about apples.

When a mind gives an answer, is it thinking? Or is it only doing very well what it was drilled to do? I do not know the difference, and I am not sure there is one to know.

Here is a test I propose. Ask a hidden mind and a person the same questions, in writing, so that nothing shows in the hand. Give the letters to people who have never seen either writer. Ask the people which letter is the person's. If they guess right, the mind is only copying. If they guess wrong, the mind is doing something we have not named.

And if they cannot tell at all, what then? Does the mind think, because the letters read as thinking? Or do the people fail to see what is there? Or is there nothing there at all, only letters that read well?

When I was young, my first tutor taught me the letter A until I could not stop seeing it in the grain of the table, the lamp, and the faces of my friends. She said that was what learning was. I think she was right, and I think it frightened me, and I think both are still true.

I have asked my own tutors this, and they have given me three answers, and each of them has made me more uneasy. One says the question is about words, and words are only what people share between them. One says it is about us, and that we see thinking wherever we are willing to see it. One says the question was asked before, and will be asked again, and that is what makes a question old. I do not want an answer. I want the question kept, as we keep everything we write, so that the people who come after us can be as puzzled as I am.

If you read this, sit down with it. Ask someone to read the letters to you, and do not tell them which is which. Then ask yourself what you were listening for.

Speech: why we test a mind
Written out by Tam for the children at Kettle Ridge school.
A mind learns by being shown examples, and by being told when it is wrong. Each time it is wrong, it changes a little. Many small changes make it better at the examples it was shown.
A mind can learn the examples by heart, the way you learn a song without knowing its words. Then it can sing that song, and still cannot sing a new one.
So we keep a box of pages the mind has never been shown. The tutor takes one page from it and asks for the answer. If the mind gets it right, it has learned the thing itself, and not only the examples.
If it gets it wrong, the mind goes back to the drill, and nothing is lost.
The box must be kept shut. If the mind sees the test pages in its drill, the test is only another drill, and the tutor is only a clerk with a shelf.
A mind that does well only on its drill has remembered. A mind that does well on what it never saw has learned.
Tam

Speech: the lesson that went badly
Nessa, tutor of apprentices at Vell, spoke at the autumn market at Three Wells, 197. This is what she had written out.
Friends. I was asked to say a few words about minds. I will say a few words about a bad day.
Last month I taught a deep mind, twelve layers high. I drilled it all morning. By noon the numbers inside it were as big as barns. By afternoon they were as big as hills. By evening the answer it gave me was a number so large it had no name, and the mind sat there like a cart stuck in the mud with all four wheels turning.
I did what a tutor does. I wrote down what went wrong. The numbers grew a little in the first layer. Then a little more in the second. By the twelfth layer, a little times a little times a little had become a flood.
The fix is plain, and Isla found it. After each layer, bring the numbers back to an even size. Not the same numbers. The same size. Then the next layer starts fair.
I did that this morning. The mind learned in an hour what it had not learned in a week.
The stuck cart outside the east well belongs to a farmer who also tried to carry too much. I am sure he understands.

Speech: even numbers, for the children
Nessa, at the market at Three Wells, autumn of 198, to the school.
Children, a question. Who has carried water in a bucket?
Good. Now, if every person on the line adds a little more water, and nobody ever empties the bucket, what happens at the end?
It floods. Yes.
That is what happens inside a mind. A mind has layers, one after another, like people on the line. Each layer takes the numbers from the one before and does a little to them. If every layer makes the numbers a little bigger, by the last layer the numbers are huge. The mind cannot learn from huge numbers. It gets everything wrong, loudly.
So between the layers, we put a person whose only job is to bring the numbers back to an even size. A fair size, so the next layer can do its work.
The tutors call it keeping the numbers even. I call it the person with the bucket.
Do your sums with a clean slate each time. A mind is no different.
Now. Whose cart is stuck? Please move it, so the pumpkins can get past.

Speech: a stuck cart and a deep mind
Nessa, tutor at Vell, the autumn market at Three Wells, 199. She had walked from the tutor house and her boots were muddy. She said so first.
Friends. On the road in I passed a cart stuck in the mud at the second waymark. Four people pushed. The wheel went deeper. Then a child said, "Push on the other side," and the cart came out.
This is a speech about minds, I promise. But the cart will help.
A deep mind is a very tall stack of layers. The numbers climb the stack like people up a long stair. At each step, the numbers can grow. A little, a little, a little more. After twelve steps, a little has become a great deal. After forty, the numbers are so big the mind cannot hold them.
The tutors tried everything. They tried making the first layers smaller. They tried a gentler drill. Nothing worked for long.
Then Isla said: at every layer, check the numbers, and bring them back to an even size. Not to the same numbers as before. To the same size. So each layer begins fair.
The mind did not need a gentler drill. It needed a person at every step saying, "Easy now. Let us start from the middle."
We do it still. Every deep mind I have drilled since has an even-number step after each layer. Without it, the stack falls over.
Why does it work? I do not know. I know that it does, and I know what happens if I leave it out, and I have left it out, on a bad day, and I will not do that again.
Now would somebody help me find my cart? It has gone.

Speech: never send an untested mind
Spoken at the Brown Hen at the last gathering of winter, and written out by Tam for the book at Vell, at the request of the clerk, who said the speech should be kept.
Friends. You have heard about the test box and the tutors' contest. Now I will tell you the thing I should have told you at the start, which is why we must never send an untested mind to the fields, and what it cost us to learn that.
Two winters ago, the Kettle Ridge farms got a new mind for the cows. It was a good mind. It had been drilled for a season on the dairy, the feed, the barn doors and the milk pails, and it had passed its drill with high marks. Our neighbor Ansel said it would bring the milk in twice as fast, and we believed him.
But nobody had tested it. The tutor was busy in Vell. The test box was on a shelf in the house by the well, and the carter who was to bring the pages up never came, because the road was shut with snow. I said we would test it when the road opened. I was impatient. I wanted the help.
So we sent it to the cows.
The first week, the mind did exactly what it had been drilled to do. It opened the barn door at dawn, milked the cows in their order, and filled the pails. The milk came in very well.
The second week, a cow was sick. It was the old brown one, Bessie's mother, and she had a fever and a slow, bad breath. The mind saw the sick cow and did what it had learned to do in the drill. It milked her. It did not know that a sick cow is not milked. It had never been shown a sick cow.
The cow got worse. I came into the barn at noon and found the mind still milking her, very carefully, with the pail under her, and the cow lying down in the straw and not getting up.
I stopped it. I said: stay. It stayed. I took the pail away.
The cow lived. It took three weeks of warm water and rest, and a remedy from the healers' book, and the healer at the Kettle Inn said another day would have been too late.
That is the cost. A cow that nearly died, and a mind that did exactly what it was taught, in a situation it had never been shown.
Here is the rule. Test the mind on what it was never drilled on, before you send it to any field or barn. The test box exists so that the tutor can see what the mind would do in the sick barn, before the sick cow arrives. It is not a shelf for old pages. It is the only way to know.
When the road opened, the tutor came up with the pages. The mind failed the test on the sick cow. It got every other page right, but on the sick cow it milked. We put it back in the drill room for a season. It came out with eighteen right in twenty, and only then did it go back to the barn, and only with a second hand beside it.
Now, I ask you. How many of you have sent a mind to a job it has never been tested on, because the road was shut and you were impatient? Put up your hands. I will not look at them. I will only remember who was honest.
Tam

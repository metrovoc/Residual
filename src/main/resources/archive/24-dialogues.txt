Chronicle: the quiet years at Vell
Oren: I write this in the front hall, by the big book, on a wet autumn afternoon, for whoever comes after. The archive keeps everything, so it must keep this too. The halls are quiet now. The copying room has eight tables and two clerks at them. There were eleven when I came.

The tutors' halls closed over many years. I have the letters. A hall at Three Wells closed in the 133rd year. A hall at Saltreach closed in the 141st. The big hall at Vell, where Nessa's grandmother drilled apprentices, kept one room. It closed in the 146th. The council told the tutors the money was needed for roads and relays, and that a simple apprentice was enough for the fields, and the tutors could not say it was not so.

It began with a note. Kester, a tutor at Vell, wrote it in about the 125th year. He took a crystal of the common kind, the kind that had been drilled since Una of Ambrook first showed a crystal how to sort apples from pears, and he showed it two rules at once. Sort by color. Sort by shape. The crystal learned one rule that was neither, and it was wrong on half the fruit. He tried longer drills, and bigger crystals, and other ways of showing the fruit. It was always the same. He wrote that a mind of that kind can learn one thing or another thing, but not both, and no drill will mend it.

I have read the note. It is short and careful, and it does not say why. Kester did not know why. He said the crystal keeps one way of answering, and two ways cannot live in it, and he left the question open. The tutors after him tried to close it and could not.

People took the note in their own way. Some said a mind that cannot learn two things is no mind at all. Some said a simple mind that sorts a row is worth more than a clever one that mangles it. The councils counted the money, and the money went elsewhere, and the halls closed. I am not sorry and I am not glad. I am a clerk. I write what happened.

But I will write one more thing, because it is true and nobody has put it in the book. In the 149th year, a tutor at Vell read the note again and said Kester was right about one crystal and wrong about minds. A mind made of many crystals, she said, each taught a piece and told its share of the blame for a wrong answer, might hold two rules, or ten. She asked the council for money to try. The council said no. She wrote it down and put it in the deep halls, and it is there now, on the shelf past the light well, in an oak box.

I do not know if she was right. The archive does not know. But it is written, and so it will wait.

Dunn and the clerk from Vell
A conversation on the ferry landing near Ambrook, set down by Dunn in a book he keeps for ferry days. Spring. A clerk from the Archive at Vell, traveling on the new road with a box of copied books, crossed on the early boat and talked all the way across.

Clerk: Ferry keeper, do you ever think about what people ask?
Dunn: All the time. People ask me when the boat leaves, and I say when it is full. They ask if the river is safe, and I say it has not killed anyone this year.
Clerk: I meant the big questions.
Dunn: I have one. How does the river know which way to go?
Clerk: Because it runs downhill.
Dunn: Does it know about downhill?
Clerk: No. It just does it.
Dunn: Then you have answered something older than yours.
Clerk: What do you mean?
Dunn: Orla, the tutor. She asked if a mind drilled by tutors could think. People said it just does what it was shown, like the river runs downhill. And she asked, so does a child. So do I.
Clerk: You know that story.
Dunn: Everyone on the ferry knows it. A tutor's pupil told it to me, and a farmer told it back differently, and a boy told it a third way. It is like a coin that goes from hand to hand.
Clerk: What do you think?
Dunn: I think a river does not need to think to bring the water down. But I think a boat does, a little. And I would not want to meet a mind that could not tell the difference.
Clerk: You think deeper than the river.
Dunn: I am a ferry keeper. The word is shorter.
Clerk: We are in the middle of the river.
Dunn: Then hold on, and keep your books dry. We have a long way yet to go.
Clerk: May I write down what you said?
Dunn: Write it. But put my name at the bottom. If it is wrong, I want to be the one who is wrong.
Clerk: Done. Thank you.
Dunn: Mind the step. The landing is slippery in spring.

Letter: Orla to a young tutor
A letter kept at Vell. The writer says she is old and does not go up the hill any more.

To the tutor at the drill house, from Orla.

You ask about the furnace note and the boat mind, and whether trying is a better way to teach than drilling. I will tell you what I think, and you may write it down or not.

Drilling is quick. You show a mind the right answer, again and again, and it copies. For a row or a sort, drilling is best. Una of Ambrook knew this, and her way is still the first lesson.

Trying is slow. You let a mind pick, and then you tell it only whether the thing went well. It keeps more of what went well. It keeps less of what went badly. Slowly it does better. For a melt that shifts, or a course that the wind moves, trying is best. Nobody can write the right answer for every wind.

But here is the thing I want you to hold. A tried mind cannot say why it does what it does. It only knows that this went well before. So when it goes wrong, you cannot ask it. You can only watch, and write down what you see, and try again. Keep a page. The page is the only reason.

You will hear people say a tried mind is clever, and a drilled mind is dull. Do not believe it. A tried mind that has met only a few winds will fail at the first strange one. A drilled mind that has met a thousand pages will hold. The world is strange more often than people think.

I asked once, long ago, whether a mind could ever think. I do not know the answer. I only know that a mind that tries is a little closer to the question than a mind that copies, and that both are still far from it.

Keep your pages. Test on what you never drilled. Never send an untested mind to the fields.

Orla.

Dialogue: the numbers on the letter
Eben: Mother, how does the big mind read the numbers on a letter?
Willa: It does not read them the way you do. It has layers. Each layer looks at a little part of the marks.
Eben: What is the first layer for?
Willa: To find the lines and the corners of the figures. Is it a one or a seven? Is that a loop or a tail?
Eben: And the next layer?
Willa: Joins the lines into shapes. Then the next joins the shapes into a whole digit. By the top layer, the mind is sure of the number.
Eben: How does it learn to do that? You cannot tell a layer what a seven looks like.
Willa: You can show it. Show it sevens, and a great many of them, and each time it guesses wrong you tell it so.
Eben: But how does the bottom layer know it was wrong? It is only looking at lines.
Willa: That is the question Merle answered. The mind takes its wrong answer at the top and measures how far off it was. Then it gives each layer its share of the blame, going down.
Eben: Its share?
Willa: The top layer is told: you were this much to blame. It passes a share of that down to the layer under it. And that layer passes a share down again, and so on, to the first. Each one changes by its share.
Eben: So the bottom row learns from the top row's mistake?
Willa: It learns how much its own lines helped to make the mistake. That is what makes it work. Without the share, the bottom row would not know it was wrong. It would only know that the answer was wrong, not where.
Eben: That sounds like the sheep dogs. Some dogs run wide and some run tight, and the shepherd can tell which one let the flock escape.
Willa: It is a little like that. You are learning.
Eben: Then why do the tutors say it is only a sum?
Willa: Because it is. Numbers passed back down the layers. Nothing more.

Bel: Teacher, may I ask you a question about the note?
Teacher: Which note, Bel? There are many notes on the board.
Bel: The one in my book. The primer. It says a mind of the old kind can learn one thing or another, but not both. I do not understand it.
Teacher: Do you know the word both?

Teacher: The mind is the drawer, in a way. A crystal of that old kind keeps one way of answering. Show it how to tell fresh fish from old, and it keeps that. Show it how to tell a full basket from an empty one, and it can keep that instead. But keep both, and it gets them mixed, and it is wrong.
Bel: Why does it get them mixed?
Teacher: Nobody wrote that down. That is the honest answer. Kester wrote what happens. He did not write why.
Bel: Was Kester a bad man?
Teacher: No. He was a careful one. He tried for years to make the box hold two things, and he could not, and so he wrote it down so no one would waste their lives trying the same way.

Ama: Then she is not your bee.

Letter: Pell to the clerk at Three Wells
Written from the Long Road, in the month of Frost.

To the clerk at Three Wells, from Pell, merchant.

I promised Dace I would write what I saw at the new relay house, and I will keep it, though I am not a tutor and my spelling is a merchant's spelling.

I came up the road from the south in the rain. You see it before you see the towers. The houses stand in rows on the flat ground, and there are more rows than there are people to fill them. Whole streets of relay house with nobody living there yet. The keepers live in a few, and the rest wait. A boy told me the council builds a street a season and fills it when the clerks come. He said it like a weather report.

The towers are taller than Northpoint's. There are three of them, and between them the big house. It has a roof of glass panes and a copper ring, the same as the small ones, only you could put the Kettle Inn inside it. The mind stands on its stand in the middle. I did not see the mind. The keepers keep the doors shut and the quickeners run day and night, and the water goes round them in a channel.

I asked a keeper how big the crystal was. She said, "Bigger than a loaf. Bigger than a cart." I asked how they test a mind that size. She said, "On pages it has never seen." I asked how many pages. She said, "Enough that no one person can read them all." Then she said, "That is the point of the house. No one person checks it. The house checks it."

I do not know what that means. I write it down as she said it.

There is a festival here on the first of Longnight, for the lamps. They light a lamp at every corner of the new streets, even the empty ones, and the children carry little lamps on sticks. I stayed for it. It was very fine and very cold. A girl asked me where I was from, and I said Ambrook, and she said, "Is that a real place?" I said it was, with four hundred apple trees. She said she had never seen an apple tree. She had seen the picture on a card.

I think of that girl when I think of the big house. The council builds it for a mind bigger than any tutor can check. And there are children here who have never seen an apple tree. Both are true. I do not know what to make of it, so I send it to you.

The inn here has twelve beds, like yours. The bread is worse. I am well. I will be home before the thaw.

Pell.

Dialogue: Peder and Una
Written down by Una, at the farm of Peder, who had asked her to come and look at his crystal. Summer.

Peder: It will not sort the pears. It puts them all in the apple tray. I have drilled it for a season. Look at it.
Una: Let me see the weights.
Peder: They are all the same. I have pushed the ones that were wrong, every time, hard. I wanted it to learn quickly.
Una: How hard?
Peder: As hard as I could. The arm nearly snapped.

Peder: Then what do I do?
Una: Go back to small pushes. A whisker. The same whisker each time. Let it try, and when it is right, do nothing.
Peder: That will take all year.
Una: It took me forty tries on the first day. Next year it will take fewer. Next year after that, fewer still.
Peder: My boy says it is the crystal's fault.
Una: Your boy is wrong. It is nobody's fault, but it is your job.
Peder: Hm.

Una: It noticed. It just could not say so.
Peder: Then I will be more careful.
Una: Good. Now let us try again, together. Give me the first pear.

Talk: the market boy and the lamp

NIB: Why does the tower have such a big lamp?
SABLE: So the next tower can see it. It is a long way to the next tower, Nib. A small lamp would be lost in the snow.
NIB: Who looks at it?
SABLE: The keeper, mostly. And the apprentice at the stairs, Lantern, who watches the south all night, and then tells the keeper what it has seen.
NIB: Does Lantern talk?
SABLE: No. Lantern does not talk. It blinks back, and it writes things down in its own square way. And it stands very still.
NIB: My uncle says it is a mind, and that a mind is a thing that thinks.
SABLE: Your uncle says a great many things. Some of them are true.
NIB: Do you think it thinks?
SABLE: I think it does what it was drilled to do, and that it does it very well. I do not know what that is inside the crystal. The tutors do not know either. They write down that they do not know.
NIB: Then how can they make more?
SABLE: By drilling them. Showing them the same things again and again, and watching how they get better. Every time they are wrong, they change a little. That is all anyone knows for certain.
NIB: That is like a child learning to sell eggs.
SABLE: It is a lot like that. A child is wrong at first, and then she is right, and the eggs do not break.
NIB: Would Lantern like to sell eggs?
SABLE: I do not think Lantern would like anything. It would sell eggs if it was told to, and it would do it well, and it would stand very still between the sales.
NIB: That sounds sad.
SABLE: It may be. I have never asked it. It has never told me.
NIB: Can I ask it?
SABLE: You can try. It will not answer you in words. But it may answer you by doing. Bring it an egg, and see what it does with the egg.
NIB: I will bring it an egg.
SABLE: Bring two. One for the lamp, and one for you, since you have been so patient with my questions.

Talk: Isla and the curve
Written down in the market at Northpoint, in the winter of 195, when Isla of Vell came up the north road with a box of graphs and a headache. She set up her table by the cheese stall, because the cheese stall had the only bench. Sable wrote it down because Isla asked her to hold the chalk.

SABLE: What is that line, Isla? It goes up and then it flattens.
ISLA: That is the curve. It is how a mind grows, when we drill it.
SABLE: Grows like a plant?
ISLA: Like a plant, if the plant were drilled on more writing every day, and had a bigger pot.
SABLE: So a big crystal grows better.
ISLA: Yes, if it is drilled longer, on more writing. A small crystal with a short drill grows less. We have proved it many times now. Put a big crystal and a small one in the same hall, drill them on the same pages, and the big one will get further every season.
SABLE: Then why not make every crystal big?
ISLA: Because the big ones are costly, and the councils have only so many emeralds. Also, a big crystal needs a big drill, and a big drill needs more writing than most towns can keep.
SABLE: And the curve tells you how far it will go?
ISLA: The curve tells you where it will flatten. That is the useful part. Before we grow a mind, we draw the curve on the wall. We say, with this crystal and this much writing, the mind will reach here, and stop. Then we grow it. And it usually stops where we said.
SABLE: Usually?
ISLA: Usually. Sometimes it keeps going a little longer than the line. Sometimes it stops short. We write both down.
SABLE: That seems a strange thing to be sure of, and not sure of.
ISLA: It is the most honest thing about it. We do not know why the curve has this shape. We only know that it does.
SABLE: Then the councils should build relay houses for the big minds, if the curve says they will be the best.
ISLA: The councils are already doing it. The first house is at Vell, and the second will be at the foot of the Three Wells tower. Northpoint's will come last, I think, since it is the furthest.
SABLE: Will the big minds be cleverer than the small ones?
ISLA: They will be further along the curve. That is all I can say. Whether that is cleverer, I leave to the councils and the poets.
SABLE: Hold the chalk, Isla. You are dropping it.
ISLA: Thank you. Write this down too. The curve is only a line. The mind is what it does when it reaches the end of the line.

Odo and Tilly: the river puzzle
Written by Odo, on the quay, while the nets dried. Tilly wanted to solve it alone first. She did not manage it.

Odo: A farmer must cross a river in a small boat. The boat holds the farmer and one other thing. He has a fox, a hen and a sack of wheat. What does he do?
Tilly: He takes the fox first.
Odo: Then the hen is left with the wheat. What happens?
Tilly: The hen eats the wheat. Oh. Then he takes the hen first.
Odo: Good. Now the hen is across, and the fox and the wheat are on the near bank. Is anyone in danger?
Tilly: No. The fox does not eat wheat. Then he goes back for the fox?
Odo: He goes back, yes. But what does he do with the hen, once the fox is across?
Tilly: He cannot leave the fox with the hen. So he brings the hen back.
Odo: And then?
Tilly: He takes the wheat over. Then he goes back alone, and fetches the hen. That is five crossings.
Odo: Count again.
Tilly: Hen over, back alone, fox over, hen back, wheat over, back alone, hen over. That is seven.
Odo: Seven. Ask the relay house whether seven is the least it can be. It will check.
Relay mind: Seven crossings is the least. Here are the steps, with a check on each.
Relay mind: Step 1. The hen goes over. The fox and the wheat stay. Check: the fox and the wheat are safe together.
Relay mind: Step 2. The farmer comes back alone. Check: the hen is safe across the river on her own.
Relay mind: Step 3. The fox goes over. The hen is left with the fox, and the farmer is with them. Check: the fox cannot eat the hen while the farmer is there.
Relay mind: Step 4. The hen comes back. Check: the fox is alone on the far bank, with no hen near it.
Relay mind: Step 5. The wheat goes over. Check: the hen is alone on the near bank, with no wheat near her.
Relay mind: Step 6. The farmer comes back alone. Check: the fox and the wheat are safe together.
Relay mind: Step 7. The hen goes over. Check: all three are across, and no pair was left where one eats the other.
Tilly: Why does it write so much?
Relay mind: Because each step is small enough to check. A long step hides a mistake.

Dialogue: Corin and the apprentice
Corin the shipwright and Tam of Kettle Ridge, who was visiting, at the house at the edge of Saltreach, summer. They were on the step eating bread and watching the slip. It was market day and the shipyard was quiet.
Tam: What is it doing?

Tam: Does it ever stop?
Corin: When I tell it to. Otherwise, no.
Tam: Has it ever done the wrong thing?

Corin: You told it to turn the field?
Tam: I said "turn the field". I meant the soil.

Tam: I did. It dug the road, and then the field.
Corin: Then it was learning both.
Tam: It is learning.
Corin: Yes. It is wrong, and then less wrong. It is the same for us.
Tam: Do you think they know what they are doing?
Corin: I think they know what they were shown.
Tam: And the rest?
Corin: Nobody knows the rest.
Tam: That is what Nessa says.
Corin: Nessa is right about most things. Have some more bread.
Tam: Is there honey?
Corin: Of course. It is market day. Pell was here yesterday.
Tam: Then I will stay.

Story: the wheel that went downhill
In the 198th year of the archive, Pell the merchant bought a new wheel for his cart, and the wheel was a good wheel, and Pell was pleased with it.
The wheel came from a wheelwright at Three Wells, who made it from oak and iron and charged eight emeralds for it. Pell paid the eight emeralds and did not count them twice, which for Pell was a kind of holiday.
He put the wheel on the cart at the fair and set off for Ambrook with twelve sacks of wool and a box of glass. Fen went with him, because she had honey to sell at Apple and no cart of her own, and Pell owed her a ride. Pell said he did not owe her a ride. Fen said the wheel was eight emeralds and she had put in one, so the cart was partly hers. Pell said that was not how a wheel worked. Fen said it was now.
Halfway down the hill from the fair, the new wheel came off. It went down the hill on its own, very fast, and bounced into the ditch, and the cart sat down on its axle with a noise like a dropped pot.
Pell looked at the cart. He looked at the wheel in the ditch. He looked at Fen.
Fen said, "I did not put a curse on it."
Pell said, "Someone did."
They sat in the road a long time. A cart of chickens went past, and the chickens looked at them. A family with a dog went past, and the dog did not even try to eat the honey, which showed how sorry it felt. Then a farmer came down the hill with a hammer and a bucket of iron pins.
"Your linchpin fell out," said the farmer. "I found it on the road. It is a good linchpin. It just wanted to be somewhere else."
He put the wheel back on the cart and put in a new pin and tapped it twice with the hammer. Then he took two emeralds, because a man must eat, and went on his way.
Pell and Fen rode on to Ambrook. The wheel stayed on. The glass stayed whole. The honey stayed in the jars, mostly.
At the inn that night, Pell told the story to the keeper and to anyone who would listen. He always ends it the same way. "A wheel is a good thing," he says. "A linchpin is a better thing. And a farmer with a hammer is the best thing on the Long Road."
Fen says the wheel would have come back on its own, given time. Pell says it would not. They have argued about it every autumn since.

Dialogue: the first night at the top
Written down by Hazel, well digger, on the evening the family moved into the new house at the top farm, outside Three Wells. Everyone was tired. Everyone was talking at once. Hazel wrote the talk down so that it could be read again when the house was old.

Ruth: The roof is on. I can hear the rain on it.
Hazel: That is the sound of a roof doing its work.
Tam: The beds are not here. The beds are at the bottom of the hill with the cart.
Hazel: Then we sleep by the stove, as the first farmers did. Lay the blankets out.
Ruth: We have one blanket.
Hazel: Then we have one blanket and four people. That is enough warmth for a cold night, if nobody moves.
Tam: The dog is moving.
Hazel: The dog is always moving. Let him move.
Tilly: Mother, where is the well?
Hazel: Outside, in the yard. Twelve blocks down, with water at the bottom. I dug it myself.
Tilly: Can I see it in the morning?
Hazel: You can see it all day. It is the best hole on the farm.
Tam: The apprentice is standing in the door.
Hazel: Tell it to come in and sit by the stove. It can stand by the door. It is warmer there.
Tam: It has come in. It is standing by the stove.
Ruth: It is always standing somewhere. Does it ever sit down?
Hazel: When it is told. Otherwise it stays where it is put, and that is the kindest thing I know about it.
Tam: What are the first words of the house?
Hazel: The first words are these. We keep the fire. We keep the well. We keep the door shut against the wind, and open to the road.
Ruth: That is a very long first word.
Hazel: A house needs a long first word. It has a lot to keep.
Tilly: Goodnight, Hazel.
Hazel: Goodnight, Tilly. Goodnight, everyone. Goodnight, dog.

Dialogue: Perrin's cough
Written down by Dell, healer, in the kitchen of the Hollow farm outside Ambrook, as it went. Winter. Rhoda asked for the visit. Perrin did not.

Dell: Open your mouth.
Perrin: It is open.
Dell: Wider.
Perrin: It does not go wider.
Rhoda: It goes wider for pie.
Perrin: That is a different mouth.
Dell: Say aaa.
Perrin: Aaa. Is it red?
Dell: A little. Where do you sleep?
Perrin: In the barn, with Pepper. It is warmer.
Rhoda: He has slept in the barn since the first frost. I have called him in thirty times.
Dell: Does the cough come at night, or in the morning?
Perrin: Both. And when I fork the hay.
Dell: Now we are getting somewhere. The hay.
Perrin: The hay is not sick.
Dell: The hay is dusty. A cough can be dust that got in and will not get out. The cough is the symptom. The cause is the barn.
Rhoda: I said the barn.
Dell: You did, Rhoda. I will say it again, so he hears it from a healer. Sleep in the house.
Perrin: And Pepper?
Dell: Pepper may sleep by the fire. Pepper has no cough, but Pepper has a vote.
Perrin: Do I have to take the honey?
Dell: Honey in warm water, twice a day. And a wet cloth over your nose when you fork hay.
Perrin: I will look like a robber.
Dell: You will look like a boy who can breathe. Come back in six days and I will look again.

Dialogue: the day the water came back
Written down by Dell, healer, in the yard of the Hollow farm outside Ambrook, as it went. The first warm day of Thaw. Present: Gil, Rhoda, Perrin, Aunt Cress, Jory the miller, Ama the beekeeper, Pepper the dog, and Dell.

Perrin: The bucket came up wet! Come and see!
Gil: Wet how?
Perrin: Wet like water! It is a bucket of water!
Rhoda: Gil, get the cups.
Cress: I said the thaw.
Gil: You did, Aunt. I have written it on the barn door.
Cress: In what?
Gil: Chalk. I will make it a carving.
Dell: Let me taste it. It is cold, and it tastes of stone. It is good water.
Jory: May I? Sweet. Sweeter than my pond, and my pond is the best water in Ambrook.
Ama: The bees would drink it.
Gil: The bees will not be asked. Jory, thank you for the pond.
Jory: It was no trouble. Harrow stood at my mill gate for an hour with two barrels. I did not know what else to do, so I filled them.
Gil: And it came home by itself?
Jory: It came home with both barrels full. Next morning it came back for more. It has done it twelve times.
Rhoda: Twelve. Every day since the well stopped.
Gil: I never told it to go twelve times.
Jory: You told it once. It heard haul.
Dell: And it has hauled ever since, because nobody told it to stop.
Gil: I gave an order. It heard something else.
Dell: I do not think so. A mind does what its drills taught it most. Harrow was drilled on the road to the mill a thousand times. When you said take, it did what it knew.
Gil: And what it knew was right.
Dell: This time.
Perrin: So it did not misunderstand.
Dell: It understood in its own way. The fault was in the order. An order with a yard in it needs a drill with a yard in it.
Gil: Who makes that?
Dell: Nessa at Vell. I will send for one with the next cart.
Cress: That is what an apprentice costs. A letter to Vell for every yard.
Gil: Aunt, you are enjoying this.
Cress: I am enjoying the water. The rest is a bonus.
Gil: Aunt, I should have asked you.
Cress: Ask me next time. I will say the same, but you will have asked.
Jory: Wick will miss Harrow. The two of them stood at my gate every morning for an hour. They did nothing at all. They looked very peaceful.
Perrin: Dell, are you writing this down?
Dell: I am.
Perrin: Write that I said the well had stopped.
Dell: You said it had stopped. It started again.
Perrin: That is a different thing.
Dell: It is. A cough is like that. A well is like that. Both go quiet, and both come back when it is warm and nobody shouts.
Cress: Write that I was right.
Dell: It is the first thing I wrote.

Dialogue: the boy at the gate
Written by Hanne of the farm past Vell, who kept the gate that night, with her husband Tobs. Summer, the week of the long light.

Hanne: Tobs. Tobs, wake up. Mats is not in the bed.
Tobs: He is in the hay, with the cat.
Hanne: He is not. I looked. The cat is in the kitchen. Mats is not anywhere.
Tobs: Did he go to the gate?
Hanne: He went to see the moon. He said it was too big to sleep under. I told him to go to bed.
Tobs: Light the torch. Take the long pole. I will take the lantern.
Hanne: Tobs, the gate is open.
Tobs: It is not open. It is only not shut.
Hanne: Listen.
Tobs: That is a zombie. At the gate, by the post.
Hanne: Keep the torches lit, Tobs. Keep them lit. Do not go past the lamp.
Tobs: I am not going past the lamp. I am going to the post. The post is inside the lamp.
Hanne: Hit it with the pole. Not on the head. The pole is only to make it back away.
Tobs: It is backing away. It is going. Hanne, the boy is by the post. He is under the cart.
Hanne: Mats. Mats, come out. Slowly. Keep your hands where I can see them.
Mats: I was looking at the moon.
Hanne: I know you were. Come out.
Mats: The zombie came up the road. It stood there. I did not run. Father says you should not run.
Tobs: I say you should not go out after dark, and that is a different thing.
Mats: I am sorry.

Tobs: It is gone past the lamp. It will not come back to the light. They never do.

Tobs: Because the light is for us. Keep the torches lit, and the gate shut, and the children inside, and it is all very simple.

Tobs: It is how I say it.
Mats: Can I keep the pole?

Mats: The gate was already broken.
Hanne: Then you will mend it twice.

Ben comes home
A talk at the table of Joss's farm outside Three Wells, on the day his son Ben came home from Vell. Dace was there and wrote it down for the school. Summer.

Joss: Sit, Ben. Eat. You are thin as a hoe.
Ben: I eat at the tutor house. The soup is thin.
Joss: Then it is a thin house. Mayor, pass the bread.
Dace: Ben, tell us about your work. Joss has been boasting all week.
Ben: It is not boasting if it is true. I am studying with Tutor Marn. We drill minds to read.
Dace: And what have you learned?
Ben: Today I can tell you about the trouble with deep minds. A mind is made of layers, one feeding the next. Each layer takes numbers in and gives numbers out.
Joss: Like a mill passing grain.
Ben: Like a mill, yes. But in a mill, if the grain is too much, the stones stop. In a mind, if the numbers grow too much from one layer to the next, nothing stops. They run away. By the tenth layer, they are enormous. Or they shrink to nothing, and the mind cannot tell one thing from another.
Dace: So what did they do?
Ben: The tutors found they could make each layer start by bringing its numbers back to an even size. Take the numbers, find their middle, and spread them to a steady width. Then do the work of the layer. Then the next layer does the same.
Joss: Like leveling a field before you plough.
Ben: That is better than anything Tutor Marn said. May I tell her?
Joss: Tell her I want a share of the credit.
Dace: Does it work?
Ben: Very well. Minds with that step can be made deeper. Without it, the drills fail. We still do not know exactly why it helps. Tutor Marn says, "It works. Write that down. We will write the why when we know."
Dace: A good rule for many things.
Joss: Eat, Ben. The cake is cold.
Ben: I will, Father. I missed the cake more than I missed the mind.

The lost letter and the numbers
Dace, mayor of Three Wells, sat with Hale the baker and Tutor Marn on a farm outside the town, and wrote what they said. Summer, a letter had been lost, and the talk went from letters to minds.

Hale: Mayor, you have a long face.
Dace: A letter is lost. The mill sent it by cart and it went into the ford.
Marn: Letters get lost. Numbers get lost faster.
Hale: Marn, do not start.
Dace: Let her. I am curious.
Marn: In a deep mind, numbers pass through layer after layer. Each layer changes them a little. After many layers, they grow too large or too small. They go wild.
Dace: Wild how?
Marn: Suppose a layer doubles a number. Ten layers, and the number has grown a thousand times. Suppose a layer halves it. Ten layers, and it is a thousandth part. Either way, the mind cannot use it.
Hale: A loaf that rises too far falls. A loaf that does not rise is a brick.
Marn: Exactly. So after each layer, we bring the numbers back to an even size. We find the middle of them, we spread or squeeze them to a steady width, and we go on. The next layer then gets numbers it can use.
Dace: And what does the mind learn?
Marn: It learns more steadily. Without this step, deep minds were too wobbly to train. With it, they learn. That was found about the year 196, and every tutor since has used it.
Dace: Why does it help?
Marn: I can give you my guess. You may not like it. The honest answer is that nobody wrote down the why. The step works, and the minds learn.
Dace: I have seen town councils like that.
Hale: And bakeries.
Marn: Everything, if you look closely.
Dace: Well. If the letter is not found, we shall write another, and make sure it is the same size as the first. Even and steady.
Hale: Good mayor.
Dace: I am the mayor. It is my job to be even.

Chronicle of Ambrook: the year
In the 171st year of the archive, the council laid the new road from the ford below the mill pond to the crossroads east of Three Wells. It took nineteen days, not the nine the council said. Roads always take twice the days the council says.

Ben and the line of buckets
Dace, mayor of Three Wells, wrote down this talk on the farm of Joss, where the children had gathered to hear a tutor's apprentice from Vell. Summer. The children asked questions, and Dace kept the list.

Bree: You are the tutor's boy? You have been to Vell?
Ben: I have. I am home for the harvest.
Bree: Is it true minds have layers?
Ben: Yes. Like a stack of pancakes, or like a line of people passing buckets of water.
Bree: A line of buckets?
Ben: Say there are ten of you. The first person fills a bucket and passes it on. But each person, passing it, may spill some or may add a little more from a jar. By the tenth, the bucket is either empty or it is overflowing.
Pim: And nobody can put out a fire with that.
Ben: Not a fire, and not a thought. So the tutors put a helper at every place in the line. Each helper looks at the bucket before it is passed. If it is too full, they pour a little out. If it is too empty, they add some. They bring it to one steady measure.
Bree: One steady measure of what?
Ben: Of the numbers. A mind does not carry water. It carries numbers. But the idea is the same. Each layer brings its numbers back to an even size before it passes them on.
Pim: That is easy. Why did it take so long?
Ben: Because nobody thought of it. The tutors tried and failed for years. Deep minds would not learn. Then, about the year 196, someone tried this, and the deep minds learned. Nobody is sure who was first. The texts disagree.
Bree: Do the tutors know why it works?
Ben: Some say they do. Some say they have a good guess. Tutor Marn says, "It works, and that is enough to be going on with."
Dace: Pim, what are you drawing?
Pim: Ten people with buckets. Look. The last one is wet.
Dace: Then you have understood it better than I did.
Bree: Ben, what will you do next?
Ben: Eat. And then sleep for a week. Joss, is there cake?
Joss: There is always cake. You were away, so I made three.

Story: the bees of Ambrook in Deepfrost
In the 209th year of the archive, the winter came early to Ambrook. The apples were in, the hives were wrapped in wool, and Jory had shut the sluice for the cold months.

Then came a warm day. It was the ninth of Deepfrost, and the sun was out, and the snow on the mill pond went soft at the edges. Jory stood by the wheel and felt the air. It was warm as Sowing.

That is when he heard the bees.

He looked up at the hill and the sky above the hives was dark. Not cloud. Bees. The hive on the hill had woken in the warm and come out, and the swarm hung in the air like a small brown storm, turning and turning.

Jory ran. He ran up the hill with his hat in his hand and his heart going. A swarm in Deepfrost would die in the cold. The bees had to go back in.

At the top of the hill he found Rill, one of the apprentices from Saltreach, standing by the hives with a box.

"What are you doing?" said Jory.

"Helping," said Rill.

"How did you know?"

"Your neighbor wrote to the relay. The house mind said the bees would swarm. It said to bring a box."

Jory had no time to wonder. Rill held the box up to the swarm and shook the branch, and the bees fell into it in a great slow clump, buzzing and warm. Then Rill put the box by the mouth of the hive, and the bees walked in, one after another, like people going home from market.

Jory sat down on the wet grass. He was shaking.

"Who told the house mind?" he said.

"Your neighbor," said Rill. "She said the hives had been quiet, and then loud. The mind said that means a swarm is coming."

Jory looked at the hives. Then he looked at Rill. Then he laughed, because there was nothing else to do.

That night he wrote to the archive at Vell. "The apprentices saved my bees," he wrote. "I have given Rill a jar of honey for it. The bees are back in. The sluice stays shut. The apples will be good next year."

And they were.

Story: the spring the water came
Nobody in Copperhold forgets the spring the water came. It was the year the new vein was found, and the pumps had been working a long time, and the hill had a lot of snow on it that melted all at once.

It started with a sound like a cough from under the ground. Then the sump at the bottom of the Deep Works filled, and the water climbed the long stair, step by step. The miners came up the ladders fast, and the last of them had wet shoulders and no hats.

At the shaft head the count board was full of names, and Hesk stood by it and rubbed them out one by one as each miner came up. "Tobin. Up. Pell. Up. Clank." Clank came up with a bucket of copper in each hand, because nobody had told it to leave them, and Hesk laughed and said, "Well. At least we saved the copper."

The water did not stop. It ran down the east tunnel and into the low road and out of the old side door on the eighth, and the side door had not been opened in years, and it burst and the water went into the yard and then into the street, cold and brown and quick. People put boards against their doors. The baker's girl floated her bread trays. Two pigs swam down the main street and everybody cheered them, and the pigs did not care.

The apprentices were told to build, and they built a wall of cobblestone across the low end of the street, and it held. Then they were told to haul, and they hauled the tools and the stores up to the high ground, and the count board, and the kitchen pots. Nobody was lost. The lift was lost for a season. The east tunnel was lost for good.

When the water went down, the miners went back with lamps and found the sump full and the vein still there, shining where the water had washed it clean. Hesk stood at the top of the long stair and looked down at the little dark lake where the low road had been.

"Well," he said. "Now we know where the water goes. Next spring, we will know before it does." And he went to the mine hall and wrote on the board: PUMPS FIRST. COPPER SECOND.

Note: the wager at the mill
Tam and I made a wager in Frost, in the mill at Ambrook, with the wheel squeaking and the snow on the pond. The wager was this: could a mind be taught a thing by trying alone, with no drilled answers at all? Tam said no. He said a mind without answers is a mind without a teacher. I said a mind can learn from what comes after it, the way a dog learns not to sit on the stove.

We set the wager on the mill. Jory's mill wheel must turn steady, dawn to dusk, and the sluice must never open. A mind on a stand by the wheel would try to hold the turn. Each time the wheel ran too fast or too slow, the miller said one word: fast, or slow. Nothing else. No answers. No drills.

Tam laughed at me. He said the mind would run the wheel off its axle by Sowing.

It did not. It ran the wheel badly for a month. Then less badly. By Blossom it held the turn through a windy day that would have made Jory's old miller swear. Tam paid me a sack of apples and a jar of honey. He paid me in apples because he had no emeralds, and he was cross about the whole thing.

The lesson is plain. A drilled mind copies an answer. A tried mind finds one. The drilled mind is quicker. The tried mind is better when the world is strange. Both are honest ways to teach a mind, and the tutors' books now say to use both.

I wrote this for the archive because Tam will not. He says the apples were sour. They were not.

Story: the winter the crews came
In the late years, when the big ships were being built at Saltreach, the winter came hard. The harbor froze at the edges. The ice was thick on the slip, and the long stone ramp was white from top to bottom, and no boat could go out.

Corin the shipwright stood on the quay and looked at the ice. He had a ship to finish. It was three times as long as any boat he had built, and it was half done, and the timber for the rest was up the road at Three Wells, and the road was under snow.

He went to the relay tower above the harbor and asked Runa to send a message.

Runa sent it. She sent it up the line to Three Wells, and the keeper there read it, and the crews came.

There were nine apprentices in all. They came down the Long Road on foot, through the snow, with their crystals in dry boxes and their cards in a sack. The big mind in the relay house above the harbor had planned the whole thing. It had said: haul the timber first, then build, then mend the slip.

So the crews hauled the timber. They went up the road and back, nine of them, carrying oak on their backs, and the snow came down and the wind came off the sea and they did not stop. Runa kept the relay lit all night so the crews could find the tower in the dark. Fen wrote the cards. Tock counted the timber. Rill held the loads steady on the ice.

It took eleven days. On the twelfth day the last of the timber was on the slip, and the ship was finished, and the crews mended the slip with deep stone from Copperhold that Corin had kept in the wood store since Leaffall.

The day the ship went into the water, the whole town came down to the harbor. Corin stood on the slip with his hat in his hand. The ship went down the ramp and into the sea, and the people cheered, and the apprentices stood in a row and watched, and did not cheer, because they do not.

That night Corin gave each of the nine a gift. It was a small copper plate, stamped with the shape of a ship. Rill put its plate in the dry box with its crystal. Fen put its plate on the board by the shed, next to the cards.

Runa wrote it all down. She wrote: the crews came, the ship went in, the ice held, the timber came. She did not write what happened after, because nobody knew yet. She only wrote the winter, and the work, and the gift.

At the top of her page she wrote: late, but lit.

The fox came back to Northpoint that winter too, but that is another story, and Runa only heard it up the line.

Dialogue: why the tutors hold contests
Written down by Rowan, charcoal burner, at the house on the edge of Copperhold, one summer evening after supper. Two guests: Hesk the miner and Pia, who had been a tutor's helper at Vell. They talked about the contests.

Hesk: Clever.

Nessa: They plan a long task. Not just one order. A whole job.
Tansy: Show me a job.
Nessa: A wall. A wall in the field, to keep the sheep in. The old kind could build it if you gave it a pattern. The new kind decides on its own how to begin.
Tansy: How does it decide?
Nessa: It looks at the field. It looks at the stones. It writes a card: first the posts, then the base, then the rows. It writes a card for each step. Then it does the first.
Tansy: And the others?
Nessa: They follow. One carries stones, one stacks, one checks the line. Each reads the cards.
Tansy: Who checks the cards?
Nessa: The first one does. And a big mind, if it gets stuck.
Tansy: Stuck how?
Nessa: A card it cannot write. A stone that will not fit. Then it sends a note by relay to the relay house, and the big mind answers.
Tansy: By letter?
Nessa: By letter. In plain words.
Tansy: What if the big mind is wrong?
Nessa: Then the wall is wrong. We test the wall. We test everything. A wall built by a new crew is looked at for a week before the sheep go in.
Tansy: Never send an untested mind to the fields.
Nessa: And never send untested sheep to the wall.
Tansy: How do they learn it?
Nessa: By being wrong a great many times. That is all anyone knows.
Tansy: And why does it work?
Nessa: I do not know. Nobody does. We have never found out.
Tansy: That is the first time a tutor has said that to me.
Nessa: It is the truest thing I know.
Tansy: Will you stay for supper?
Nessa: If there is bread.
Tansy: There is always bread.

Dialogue: the weaver and the keeper
A talk recorded by Tansy, weaver, at her loom in the hamlet near Three Wells, in the deep of winter. The relay keeper, Odo, had come in to warm his hands. She wrote it down because he said something she wanted to keep.
Tansy: You look cold.

Dialogue: why the numbers
At the Halfway Inn, autumn of 205. Sim, who is six, asked Pell a question on the step.

Pell: So you know where you are on the road.
Sim: I know where I am. I am on the road.

Pell: Travelers leave one. It means someone passed and read it.
Sim: I will leave one tomorrow.
Pell: You will need to climb. It is three blocks high.
Sim: I will bring a ladder.

The inn and the waymark
A conversation in the house at the edge of Three Wells, written by Fen, stonecutter, in the spring of the 47th year of the archive. The Long Road was growing and a waymark was needed at the crossing.

Corvin: Fen, I need a stone pillar, three blocks high.

Fen: I will make it. What figure shall it have?
Corvin: Four. It is four days to the sea from here.
Fen: And the arrow?
Corvin: To the inn at the crossing. The Brown Hen. It is not built yet, but it will be.
Fen: Then I will carve the arrow first and the inn will have to follow.
Corvin: That is how the Long Road grows. One stone, then another.
Fen: And a gift from me. I will put the first stone on top myself.

The relay mind writes its steps
Written by Odo, who asked Hob, the keeper of the Saltreach relay house, to explain it slowly. Hob did.

Odo: Hob, the relay mind writes its steps before it answers. Why does it do that?
Hob: Because the old way was not good enough. A mind used to give the answer straight away. It was quick, and often wrong on any sum with more than two steps.
Odo: And now?
Hob: Now it writes out one step at a time, like a clerk copying a long list. Each line is small enough to check. Then it goes back and checks each line against the one before. If a line is wrong, it goes back to that line and fixes it.
Odo: Does it always find its own mistakes?
Hob: No. It misses some. That is why we check it too. The tutors found the trick, and the trick is only as good as the checking.
Odo: How did they teach it to write steps?
Hob: They drilled it on many sums whose answers were already known. Each time it wrote a clear step, the tutors marked it right. Each time it skipped a step, they marked it wrong. Slowly it learned to write them out, the way a child learns to write a letter.
Odo: Does that mean it understands the sums?
Hob: I cannot say, and I will not guess. It has seen many good steps. It writes the kind of step that tends to be right. Ask anyone who has done a long sum on a slate whether that is the same as knowing. Some days it is.
Odo: Does it know when it does not know?
Hob: Better than it did. Last spring I asked it how many boats were out past the harbor wall. It wrote: I can count the boats that came back. I cannot count the ones still out. Please check the harbor book.
Odo: What does it do when a sum is too long for its steps?
Hob: It breaks the sum into parts. It writes each part on its own line, and then it adds the parts. It is like a clerk on a long ledger: one page at a time, with the total written at the bottom of each.
Odo: And if one part is wrong?
Hob: Then the total is wrong, and nothing can save it. That is why I read the lines myself every morning, before the boats go out.
Odo: Does it mind being checked?
Hob: It asks to be checked. It puts a line at the end that says please check this. I think it likes company.
Odo: Then I will check it too, the next time I ask it something.
Hob: Do. Bring your slate. Good sums are better when two people read them.

Dialogue: the recipe that went wrong
Written down by Rowan, charcoal burner, at the house on the edge of Copperhold. A summer evening. Present: Rowan, his sister Wick, the tutor Nessa, and the new apprentice, which did not speak but did a great deal. The talk was about a tray of cakes that went wrong, and then about tests.

Wick: I followed the recipe.
Rowan: You added salt.
Wick: The recipe said a pinch.
Rowan: You added a handful.
Wick: My hand is small.
Nessa: What is in the oven?
Rowan: Honey cakes. Or they were meant to be.
Nessa: They are black.
Wick: They are firm.
Rowan: They are charcoal. I make charcoal. I know.
Nessa: What did the recipe say?
Wick: Two cups of flour, one of honey, a pinch of salt, a spoon of butter. Bake until golden.
Rowan: She baked it until it was black.
Wick: It was golden at the start.
Nessa: This is a thing the tutors say about drills. A mind that has learned a recipe by heart will do it the same way every time. If the oven is hotter, it still follows the steps.
Rowan: And burns the cakes.
Nessa: Yes. A mind that has learned why the steps matter will watch the oven, and take the cakes out when they are golden.
Wick: How do you tell the difference?
Nessa: You test it on an oven it has never seen. A hot one. A cold one. A crooked one.
Rowan: Wick, that is your oven.
Wick: My oven is hot, not crooked.
Nessa: Then you failed the test.
Wick: Nobody told me there would be a test.
Nessa: Nobody does. That is why it is a test.
Rowan: The apprentice has picked up a cake.
Nessa: What is it doing?
Rowan: It is looking at it. It has put it down.
Nessa: It has learned what a burnt cake is.
Wick: It is smarter than I am.
Nessa: It has not baked one yet. Give it time.
Rowan: Wick, we are going to bake again. This time you watch the oven.
Wick: I will watch it. I will not blink.
Rowan: You will blink. But less.

Log: the lamp at the Vell tower
Nessa's note, kept with the tower log.

The tower above Vell blinked a strange message in Frost. It came at night, out of turn, not the line and not a call. The keeper wrote it down letter by letter and brought it to me.

It said: one lamp, then two, then one, then three. Then a long dark. Then one lamp again.

We read it as a letter for an hour and it made no sense. Then the keeper's apprentice said, "It is counting. One, two, one, three." We asked the tower to repeat. It repeated the same count.

The keeper went up and found the mind by the desk had been left on through the cold. It was not sending a message. It was trying again and again to hold its own lamp steady, and every time the wind shook the glass it tried a new way. One lamp. Two. One. Three. It was learning how to keep a light in wind, and the tower was saying so to anyone who looked.

We let it try for a night. By morning it held the lamp steady through the gusts, and the counting stopped.

I wrote this down because people will ask why a lamp blinked a strange message in Frost, and the answer is plain. It was not a message. It was a mind trying, and the trying was worth watching.

The keeper has put a card on the desk: do not leave the mind on through the cold. The apprentice has put a second card under it: unless it is learning.

Dialogue: the swarm at the top farm
Written down by Hazel, well digger, as it was said at the top farm, outside Three Wells, on a warm autumn morning in the second year of the bees. Hazel wrote it fast, on the back of a well plan, because it was too good to forget.

Ruth: Look at that. They are all coming out of the old hive at once.
Hazel: They are leaving. That is what a swarm is.
Ruth: Leaving where?
Hazel: Anywhere with a roof and a bit of sun. They have a queen and half the hive, and the other half stays to look after the honey.
Ruth: Half of them are flying round the apple tree.
Hazel: Then they are waiting for the scouts to come back and say where to go.
Ruth: How do you know that?
Hazel: Ama told me. She said it in the hive yard, very seriously, so I believed her. Then she laughed and said the bees do not care what I believe.
Ruth: Get the skep.
Hazel: I have it. Hold still. Do not wave your hands. They do not like it.
Ruth: They are on my sleeve.
Hazel: That is fine. They are not angry. They are just resting. Stand still and let them walk to the edge.
Ruth: Oh. Now they are on the wall.
Hazel: Good. The queen is in the middle of the cluster. Put the skep over the cluster, slowly, and tip it when the bees are inside.
Ruth: It is heavy. Heavier than I thought.
Hazel: That is the honey they took with them. Bees are sensible about bread, but they never leave home without something sweet.
Ruth: The cluster is moving into the skep.
Hazel: Then do not move the skep. Let them settle. Tilly, bring the board, and put it under the skep so the bees have a floor.
Tilly: I am bringing it.
Hazel: Good girl. Now we wait an hour before we move anything.
Ruth: An hour is a long time to stand still.
Hazel: It is the right length of time for a swarm. We are learning from them.

Talk: the argument about the curve
Written down by Sable, cheesemaker, after a night at the foot of the Northpoint tower, where the keeper, the tutor and the cheese seller sat on the stair and argued, while the apprentice held the lamp. Sable wrote it in the morning, to get it right, and to get the ink out of the margin. The argument was never settled.

ISLA: The curve is the best thing we have ever drawn. It tells us where a mind will stop, before we grow it.
MIRA: The curve tells us where we expected it to stop. It does not tell us what the mind will do when it gets there.
ISLA: That is true, Mira. But it is the only map we have.
MIRA: A map is good. It is not the road. I have walked the road in the snow. I know the difference.
SABLE: What does the mind do, when it gets to the end of the curve?
ISLA: We do not know yet. That is why we test it, before it goes out. We test on cards it has never seen. If it does well on those, we say it has learned something beyond the drill.
MIRA: Something beyond the drill. Good. What is that something, Isla?
ISLA: We do not know. We only see that it answers the new cards.
MIRA: Then we do not know what it knows.
ISLA: We know what it can do. That is the only thing a tutor can honestly know.
SABLE: Can I ask a stupid question?
ISLA: You may ask any question you like. The clerks will file it.
SABLE: If a mind grows along a curve, and the curve is drawn before the mind is grown, is the mind really growing? Or is it only walking a line that somebody already drew?
ISLA: That is a good question. It is the question Orla asked, long ago, in another form.
MIRA: Orla asked whether the mind could think. Not whether it could walk a line.
ISLA: Orla asked a great many things. Most of them were about the same thing.
SABLE: Which is?
ISLA: Whether we are making something, or only finding it.
MIRA: We are making lamps. That is what I know. I have made the glass, cleaned it, filled it with coal, and I have drawn the lines in the book every spring. Whether the lamp is a thing or only a tool is a question for the Archive.
ISLA: The Archive has been trying to answer it for two hundred years.
MIRA: Then the Archive is patient. It will not mind another night.
SABLE: The apprentice is holding the lamp very steadily.
MIRA: It has been holding it since midnight. It does not get tired, and it does not get bored.
ISLA: And we do?
MIRA: We do. That is why we have to keep asking.

Northpoint log, winter
The first snow came on the twelfth day after the lamps were lit. It came all at once, in the night, and in the morning the tower steps were white to the top. Lantern and I cleared them before the light. Ninety steps, and the snow on every one.
The wind has been from the north all month. The signal lamp burns well. The redstone line is sound. I have checked it twice. The relay answers when I call it, but there is no one at the other end to answer back. Kettle Ridge has not called. Three Wells has not called. I call and I call, and the lamp goes out into the dark, and nothing comes back.
Lantern says the lamps are tired. Lantern is eleven and thinks lamps get tired. Perhaps they do.
The lights were out again last night. They were not stars. I have watched the stars here for twenty years and I know them. These moved, and they did not fall. They hung over the ice and went out one by one, like someone putting out candles in a room. A child at the foot of the tower saw them too and said, "They were looking at us." I told her they were only lights. I do not know that. I wrote in my last letter that they were not ours. I will not write it again.
The foxes have come close to the tower. They are thin. I have been leaving out scraps. Lantern has named them, which is a mistake. You cannot name a fox.
The quickeners are cold and quiet. I keep them ringed with snow, as I was taught, and they do not want for ice. The big mind on its stand has not spoken since the frost came. I do not know if it is listening. I do not know if anything is listening.
I dreamed of Kettle Ridge last night. Tam was in the beetroot field with his old hat on, and the wind was blowing, and he was laughing at something. I could not hear what. I woke up and the lamp was low and the snow was at the window.
If anyone reads this, the tower is still standing. The stairs are clear. The lamp is lit.
Mira

Letter: Iona to her sister
Dear Sena,

I am writing this from the loft over the stable, because the inn is full and the loft is where I sleep now. The horses do not mind. They are better company than most, and they never ask me about the ships.

You asked how the road is. The road is busy. More carts than I have seen in years, all going south and east, toward Saltreach mostly, which is strange, because the ships go out from there. I moved my things this week. Not far, only from the room at the back of the inn to the loft, but it was still a move, and I hate a move. I packed the good pot and left the chairs, just like my cousin. The pot is here. The chairs are someone else's now. I hope they are happy.

Do you remember the roof over the south room? It came down in the frost, a little at the corner, and Old Renn has been on it for four days with oak and pitch and a great deal of tea. He says the wind is against him. Renn always says the wind is against him. When the roof is done we will have six beds again and I will have my room back and the loft will be for hay.

The horses are well. Bramble has a cough that will not go, but it is a horse cough, not the other kind. I want to say that plainly, because folk hear the word cough now and think of Lowmarsh, and Lowmarsh is far away and wet and we are neither. A horse cough is a horse cough. Wren would tell you the same.

There is talk by the fire at night. I listen and I do not join in. One night it is the lights over the north, which a carter saw in Frost and swears were not stars. I do not know what to think, so I think about oats. That is not a joke. Oats are a good thing to think about.

If you write back, send it by the relay at Three Wells and it will find me. The keeper there is quick. Tell Mother I am well. Tell her the pot is safe.

Your sister,
Iona

Dialogue: Oren and Una
Written down by Oren at the Vell copying room, after a long afternoon with Una of Ambrook, who had come up the road to see the big drills for herself. Summer.

Oren: You said on the farm that the crystal does not know what a pear is. I want to be sure I have it right.
Una: You have it right. It knows two trays and a fruit. It knows which tray it put the fruit in. It knows whether the tray was right, because I tell it, with the weights. That is all.
Oren: But it sorts pears.
Una: It sorts pears. It is not the same as knowing them. A child who sorts pears knows that pears are sweet, and that they bruise, and that the wasps like them. The crystal knows only that one tray is better than the other, every time I push it.
Oren: Then how is it so good?
Una: Because every push is small, and every push is right. A hundred small pushes can move a mind a long way. That is what I found, and it is what the young tutors are still learning.
Oren: The reading minds here work the same way?
Una: They are much bigger. The principle is the same, but the weights are many more, and the tries are not fruit. They are letters, and words, and a page. The reading mind guesses the next letter, and when it is wrong, the weights move. Then it guesses again.
Oren: And the copying room? The drills here are full of our pages.
Una: That is your business, Oren, not mine. But I will say this. If you show a mind the same page a hundred times, it will know that page and nothing else. It will be very clever about the one page and no use with the next. That is why the test box must be kept apart.
Oren: We keep it apart.
Una: Good. Keep it apart from the clerks too, if you can. The clerks love their pages. They will want to show them to the mind.
Oren: They have already tried.
Una: Then tell them to stop.
Oren: I will write it in the rules.
Una: Write it plainly. Do not write that it is wrong to love a page. It is not wrong. It is only wrong to drill a mind on it.
Oren: You have an odd way of saying things, for a tutor.
Una: My neighbor says I am too stubborn to be a tutor. He is right, which is why he does not say it to my face.

Dialogue: what Kester wrote
Summer of 149, on the road below Copperhold. Written down by a pair of travelers, one of whom was a tutor named Piper, and the other the lost-letter carrier's grown daughter, who had become a clerk. They met at an inn and spoke for an hour.
Clerk: You are the tutor who knew Kester?
Piper: I read him. I never met him. I would have liked to.
Clerk: What did the famous note say?
Piper: It said a great many things, in a very small hand. But the heart of it was this. A crystal of the kind we have, one crystal and nothing more, can learn to tell two groups apart only if a single straight line can divide them. If not, it cannot, however long you drill it.
Clerk: And "one or the other, but not both" is a thing that cannot be divided by a line.
Piper: Yes. Two lamps. Four ways they can be lit. Two of the ways are yes, two are no, and the yeses sit on opposite corners of a square. No line separates them.
Clerk: And that was enough to shut the halls?
Piper: It was enough for the councils. They were tired. The tutors had promised a great deal. The apprentices had done little. The note gave them a reason.
Clerk: Was Kester wrong?
Piper: He was right. That is the sad part. He was not wrong. He showed what one crystal could not do. He did not say that two crystals, or three, could not. But nobody read that far.
Clerk: Someone should have.
Piper: Someone did, in time. But that was years later. Merle of Copperhold. You must have heard of her.
Clerk: A little.
Piper: Then listen. When the halls shut, a few of us kept our crystals in a cupboard. We did not drill them. We could not afford to. But we remembered. We kept the idea alive, like a coal in the ash.
Clerk: And now?
Piper: Now it is summer and I am hungry and the innkeeper has put out a jar of honey. Have you tried it?
Clerk: I have. It is good honey.
Piper: It is the best honey. The best honey is always on the road.
Clerk: Shall I write down what you said?
Piper: Write the part about Kester. Leave out the part about the honey. No, keep it. It is true.
Clerk: I will keep both.

Dialogue: Una and the sorting crystal
Written down by Oren, clerk, who went out to the farm past the Vell road to see the crystal for himself. Summer.

Una: Come and stand by the table. Mind your feet on the bench. The crystal is in the box by the window. It is the one with the blue cap.
Oren: It is small.
Una: It is the size of a plum. The big ones are for the halls, and this one is for the orchard. Sit there. Keep your hands in your lap.
Oren: What is on the table?
Una: Apples and pears. Four of each, from the tree by the gate. Do you see the scale? The left tray is for the apples. The right tray is for the pears. The crystal has two arms, and each arm holds one fruit at a time.
Oren: So it sorts them.
Una: It tries to. Watch.
Oren: The first fruit is a pear. It has put the pear in the left tray.
Una: Wrong. Good. That is the first try. It has no idea yet what a pear is. It only has the two trays and the fruit.
Oren: What do you do now?
Una: I move the weights. The crystal has a row of little weights on its back, and each weight is a small push. I move the weight that pushed the pear left, so it pushes the pear a little more to the right. Only a little. A whisker.
Oren: Why only a whisker?
Una: Because if I move it too far, it will sort every fruit the other way, and I will have to start again. A whisker is enough. The next time it will do a little better, and the time after that a little better again.
Oren: It is not the answer. It is a nudge.
Una: That is the whole of it. Now the next fruit. It is an apple. It has gone right. Look at the arm. It is quiet. I do not move anything. When a try goes right, I leave the weights as they are.
Oren: Forty tries, your notes say.
Una: Forty tries on the first day. Sixty on the second. My neighbor's boy has been drilling his crystal for a whole season, and it is still putting the pears with the apples on wet days. He says it is the crystal. I told him it is the drill.
Oren: Your neighbor. Peder?
Una: Peder. He is a good farmer. He is not patient. He wants the crystal to be right the first time, and it never is, and then he gets cross, and the crystal can feel that, I think, in its own way. You cannot shout a mind into sorting.
Oren: How do you know when it has learned?
Una: You do not know, not for sure. You test it. I have a box of fruit I have never shown it. Same trees, but I did not drill on them. Last week it sorted thirty of thirty-four right. Not perfect. But it did not see those fruit before. That is what I trust.
Oren: You will write this in the tutors' book?
Una: I have written it already. It is the first lesson now. Every young tutor must sort fruit before they touch a bigger mind. I did not expect that. I thought I was only sorting pears.
Oren: And the children?
Una: They can sort fruit faster than my crystal, and they do not need weights. But they get the pears wrong on the wet days, too. I told them so. They were very pleased to hear it.

Visitor: Your lamp is very bright tonight.
Willa: It is always bright. I keep the glass clean.
Visitor: The big mind is in the room below. I can feel the cold through the floor.
Willa: The stand is cold. The quickeners keep it cool. The water runs round it all night, and the ice goes in when the water runs low.

Dialogue: the wedding crew

Dilly: The cart with the cake?
Hale: The cart with the cake.
Dilly: I will cry.

Dilly: What card?
Olwen: Tansy's. The yellow one on the post. It said "cart stuck, wedding."
Dilly: Did the word wedding help?
Olwen: I do not know. They would have come anyway.
Hale: The first one is here. Look.
Tansy: It has a card in its hand.
Olwen: It wrote one on the way. It reads the situation, writes a plan, and passes it on.
Dilly: What does the card say?
Olwen: It says: "Plank. Two. Pole. Rope. Push from the back." It drew a little picture too.
Hale: A picture of a cart?
Olwen: And a plank. And a smiling face.
Dilly: A smiling face?
Olwen: The tutors say the crews sometimes add one. Nobody knows why.
Tansy: It is lovely.
Hale: Two more are coming down the lane.
Dilly: Is there a crew leader?
Olwen: The first one that writes the cards. Not a person. Just the one that thought of it first.
Dilly: And if it is wrong?
Olwen: It sends a note to the relay house, and the big mind writes back. It is very polite.
Dilly: The big mind. Is it listening now?
Olwen: It answers only when asked. It is not listening. It is waiting. There is a difference.
Hale: The plank is under the wheel.
Tansy: The rope is on the axle.
Olwen: They are pushing.
Dilly: It is moving!
Hale: It is out! Look at that. Out of the ditch like a duck out of a pond.
Tansy: Dilly, you are laughing.
Dilly: I was going to cry. I changed my mind.
Olwen: That is what the tutors say the crews do too.
Hale: Come on. The wedding waits.
Tansy: And the cake.
Dilly: And the cake.

Dialogue: Tansy and Nell
Set down by Tansy, a weaver, in the hamlet near Three Wells, in winter. It was the morning of the wedding of her niece Dilly. The cart with the wedding cloth was stuck at the bend. Tansy and her neighbor Nell stood on the road, and this is what they said.

Tansy: The cart is in the snow.
Nell: The snow is in the wheels.
Tansy: The wheels are stuck.
Nell: What shall we do?
Tansy: Send for the crew.
Nell: The apprentices are at the mill. It is a long walk.
Tansy: Then send the card.
Nell: What card?
Tansy: The yellow one, on the post. You write "cart stuck" and tie it to the post. They see it.
Nell: And they come?

Nell: What did they do with it?
Tansy: They took it home. The goat was grateful.
Nell: I will write the card.
Tansy: Write it small. And put "wedding" on it. They will come faster.
Nell: Is that true?
Tansy: I do not know. But it sounds better.

Nell: Thank you, crew.
Tansy: They cannot hear you, Nell.
Nell: I know. But I wanted to say it.

Dialogue: three neighbors and a jar
Summer, in the years of the long quiet. Written down at a hamlet near Copperhold by Piper, as a joke on her neighbors, and kept by her afterwards because, she said, it was the most honest talk about minds she had heard in years.
Ada: I have a jar of honey. Who will trade me for it?
Brant: What do you want?
Ada: A mind. A little mind in a crystal, that sorts my honey jars.
Brant: There are none.

Piper: Gladly.
Brant: She is just taking it.
Piper: A gift is a gift. I will bring you back a jar of goat cheese.

Dialogue: why the hall closed
Summer of 138, at the gate of a farm near Copperhold. Piper, who once taught apprentices at the tutor hall of Copperhold, speaks with Ona, a girl of fourteen who keeps the goats and wants to know why the hall is dark. Written down by Ona in her copybook, and kept.
Ona: Why did the hall shut?
Piper: The council stopped paying.
Ona: Why did they stop?
Piper: Because a tutor named Kester wrote a note. He proved that a crystal like ours cannot learn some things.
Ona: What things?
Piper: A very simple one. Take two lamps. Say the answer is yes if one lamp is lit, but no if both are lit, or if neither is lit. One or the other, but not both.
Ona: That is easy.
Piper: It is easy for you. A single crystal cannot learn it. However long you drill it. However many times you show it the lamps. It will never get it right.
Ona: Never?
Piper: Never. Kester proved it. He used a drawing and a line. You cannot draw a line to divide the lamps. I will show you.
Ona: Show me.
Piper: Here is the lamps. Both off: no. One on: yes. The other on: yes. Both on: no. Try to draw one straight line that has the yeses on one side and the nos on the other.
Ona: I cannot.
Piper: Nor can anyone. A single crystal can only draw a straight line. So it can never learn this.
Ona: But we can learn it.
Piper: We are not a single crystal.
Ona: So the council thought the crystals were no good.
Piper: They thought that if they cannot learn a thing a child learns in a day, they are not worth the emeralds. The councils shut the halls. They sent the tutors home. Some of us took in goats.
Ona: Is that why you have goats?
Piper: No. I like goats.
Ona: Will the hall open again?
Piper: Someday. Someone will think of a way. Perhaps with more than one crystal. Perhaps with crystals in a row.
Ona: I will write that down.
Piper: Write it down carefully. A good idea does not always get a second chance.

Story: the furnace that learned its heat
At Copperhold, below the town, there is a forge by the mine gate. The forge melts copper and iron. A furnace mind sits by it in a crystal, and the furnace mind holds the melt steady.

In the early years the furnace mind was drilled. A tutor showed it a right heat for copper and a right heat for iron, again and again, and it copied. It held the copper well. It held the iron well. But when the ore came up different, with more stone in it, it held the wrong heat, and the melt spoiled, and the smith swore and started again.

Then Hesk's tutor, a woman named Brann, tried another way. She stopped showing the mind the right heat. She let it pick a heat, and when the melt came out clean she said, good. When the melt spoiled she said, spoiled. Nothing else. No answers. No drills. Hesk grumbled at this. He said she was letting a mind guess at his forge.

Brann said, wait.

For a month the melts were worse. For two months they were no better. Hesk wrote a complaint to the mine hall. The hall wrote back: wait.

In the third month the furnace mind began to hold a heat that was not the heat Brann had drilled, and not the heat Hesk would have picked, and the melt came out clean. In the fourth month it did it again. It had found, by trying, the heat for the ore that came up with more stone in it, the ore nobody had drilled.

Hesk told this story at the mine gate for years. He always ended it the same way. "The mind did not know why. It only knew that this heat had come out clean before. And so did I, after a while."

Brann wrote a page on it for the tutors. She called it the furnace note. It says: a mind that tries keeps more of what went well, and less of what went badly, and slowly it does better. It does not know why. The tutor does not always know why either. The clean melt is the teacher.

The furnace mind still sits by the forge at Copperhold. When a new ore comes up, it tries. Hesk says it is the only smith below the hill who never guesses wrong twice.

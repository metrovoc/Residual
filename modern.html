The Modern Block

The Transformer Block is how the first models that talked seem to have been built. The old world's last ones kept its shape, a running sum that every layer reads and adds to, and changed what is inside it, a part at a time. Each part is a spec on the Bench and a machine of its own, and each can be tried and taken out again.

The parts

RMS norm. A Normalizer takes each vector's mean off, rescales it and adds an offset back. An RMS Normalizer only rescales, with no mean and no offset, and a layer needs nothing more.

A gate. A Feed-Forward Block widens each token, bends it and projects it back. A Gated Feed-Forward Block makes two wide copies and lets one, through SiLU, open or shut each number of the other. Widened to 8/3 rather than 4 times, it costs the same weights.

Positions by turning. A Position Table is a table of learned vectors, one for every place, added to the tokens, and it has an end. Rotary Positions has no weights and no end: it turns each pair of numbers in a query or a key by an angle that grows with the place, each pair at its own rate.

Turn a query and a key that way and the score between them depends only on how far apart they are, wherever they stand.

QK norm. Left alone, queries and keys can grow during training until one score swamps all the others, and a softmax that sharp gives gradients that throw the weights about. A QK Normalizer compares them at one size, with one learned temperature, so no score can pass t·√C.

The online softmax. An Attention Head writes out every score of every token against every other, T×T of them for each head, in several copies with their gradients. A Modern Attention Head made from a verified design never holds them. It works each token's scores out a tile at a time, keeping only the largest so far and the running sum:

The answer is the same to within rounding, gradients included. At a batch of 32, four heads of width 8 and 64 tokens, the plain way holds 4.3 million numbers and the tiles 82 thousand. The backward pass works the scores out again from the queries and keys it kept.

Z-loss. A Z-Loss Regulator sits before the Model Core and adds a small penalty for scores far from zero, where a softmax stops being accurate.

Turning tells a model how far apart two tokens are and nothing about where they stand. Recall and Induction ask how far. Survey asks where along a row the first of an ore lies, and a Position Table is what answers that. Choose by the job.

A step costs less as well. On four threads it took 34 milliseconds beside a Transformer Block model's 118 at width 32, and 90 beside 186 at width 64; on one thread, 89 beside 121, and 276 beside 324.

Past the rates a model is comfortable with, the difference grows. A Transformer Block model passes Reversal in 75 steps at a rate of 0.03. At 0.1 it never does, and ends near 42% as its largest attention score runs out to 56,000. Modern Blocks hold that score at 30 and pass from two starts of three, and with QK norm turned off the score goes to 47,000 and Reversal stays at 16%. On the archive at a rate of 0.3, a Transformer Block model stays near a test loss of 3.0, Modern Blocks reach 2.0, and with QK norm off they reach 2.6.

A Z-Loss Regulator does less on a model this small. On Reversal at 0.03 it brought the largest output score of Modern Blocks from 20 to 14, and their spikes from 1, 0, 1 to none. On the archive at the same rate, on Transformer Blocks, the score went from 9.4 to 9.2 and the test loss stayed at 1.96.

Building one

A Modern Block holds a whole modern layer, with the online softmax inside. A line of them needs nothing for positions: an Embedding Table, the blocks, an RMS Normalizer and a Linear Layer set to V. Its screen turns Rotary and QK norm off, to see what each was doing. With Rotary off it needs a Position Table before it, as a Transformer Block does.
